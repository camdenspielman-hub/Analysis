# 14 — Technology, transport and material culture of Tokyo, 1870–1912 (real history only)

Compiled for the alt-history project. Real history only: no speculation about the premise. Every bullet gives the page it came from; I opened each one (Wikipedia pages were fetched as full HTML and read locally; statistical tables were downloaded as the original .xlsx files from the Hitotsubashi University IER data repository and read cell by cell).

**Conventions.** Dates before 1 Jan 1873 are given as Japanese lunisolar date + Gregorian date. Where a source gave only one of the two, I converted with a Chinese-calendar library (sxtwl) after checking it against nine known Meiji equivalences. It matched seven exactly and was **one day early** for two dates in Meiji 5 (months 9 and later) because the Japanese and Chinese calendars differed by a day there. Converted dates are marked "(conv.)" and should be read as ±1 day unless a source confirms them. "sen" = 1/100 yen; "rin" = 1/10 sen. Kotobank = コトバンク.

**Research limits.** Partway through, the shared WebSearch budget for this run (200 searches) ran out. After that I worked only from pages I already knew or could reach directly: Wikipedia titles, official sites, repository records. Some items are therefore thinner than intended. Gaps are listed in the last section.

## 1. Carriages, rickshaws, omnibuses, horse tram

### Private and official carriages (馬車 basha)
- Japan had no horse-drawn vehicles before the Meiji era. Fukuzawa Yukichi first saw a carriage in America in 1860. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/馬車
- The shogunate allowed carriages for freight in Edo and on the Five Highways on Keiō 2/10/1 (= 7 Nov 1866, conv.). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/馬車
- The first carriages were private ones that foreigners brought in after the ports opened and used around the treaty ports (Sekai Daihyakka, author 山本弘文). Foreign legations used carriages between Edo and Yokohama (Nipponica, 倉石忠彦). From the 1870s private carriages appeared one after another, and owners of both commercial and private carriages were taxed from the start (Sekai Daihyakka). "華族や高級官僚は自家用馬車をもつようになった" (Nipponica). — https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%A6%AC%E8%BB%8A-114246
- A Tokyo Metropolitan Government history says carriage use was first permitted on some Tokyo streets in Meiji 4 (1871), and that at first carriages were used by "政府高官や華族といった富裕層". — https://www.toshiseibi.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/documents/d/toshiseibi/pdf_keikaku_chousa_singikai_pdf_tokyotoshizukuri_1_02
- Tokyo Prefecture issued carriage regulations (馬車規則) on "1872年4月11日". The page does not say whether this is the Gregorian date or a lunar date. If Gregorian, it equals Meiji 5/3/4 (conv.). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/馬車
- **State carriages for ministers.** A Dajōkan file of January Meiji 17 (1884), 大臣参議乗用馬車製造費支出方ノ件 ("on paying for the building of carriages for ministers and councillors"), National Archives ref. 別00023100. Only the title is online; it gives no amounts. — https://www.digital.archives.go.jp/item/en/631663
- **A minister's carriage in 1878 (Ōkubo Toshimichi).** On 14 May 1878 Ōkubo left his Kasumigaseki house at about 8:00 a.m. in a **two-horse carriage (2頭立て)** for the temporary palace at Akasaka. He was attacked at about 8:30 at Kioichō Shimizudani. With him were the coachman (御者) **中村太郎 Nakamura Tarō** and the attendant (従者) **芳松 Yoshimatsu**. The assassins first cut the horse's legs with swords to stop the carriage. Nakamura jumped down unarmed to fight and was stabbed to death. Yoshimatsu escaped to the nearby Kitashirakawa-no-miya residence. The preserved blood-stained carriage is described as "英国製" (British-made). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/紀尾井坂の変
- **A minister's carriage in 1889 (Ōkuma Shigenobu).** On 18 Oct 1889, at about 4:10 p.m., Foreign Minister Ōkuma was riding back from Sakuradamon to his official residence. In front of the Foreign Ministry gate, 来島恒喜 Kurushima Tsuneki, wearing a morning coat and carrying a bat-wing umbrella in his right hand, walked up to the carriage and threw a bomb. It burst against a stone gatepost, and the fragments shattered Ōkuma's right ankle and shin as he sat in the carriage. The leg was amputated above the knee at about 10 p.m. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/大隈重信遭難事件
- **How many carriages, nationwide.** National passenger-carriage counts (乗馬車台数) from the Long-Term Economic Statistics (LTES), Table 92: 287 (1875), 653 (1877), 1,310 (1880), 1,728 (1882), 1,763 (1885), 1,994 (1887), 2,589 (1890), 2,903 (1895), 5,494 (1900), 5,556 (1905), 7,709 (1910), 7,860 (1912). — https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2019988 (file LTES_J06-92.xlsx)
- Imperial Statistical Yearbook, national passenger carriages (乗用馬車): 4,653 (end of FY1898), 6,631 (FY1903), 7,606 (FY1908), 8,478 (FY1909), 8,565 (FY1910), 8,932 (FY1911), 8,733 (end of FY1912). — https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2008167 (1913_t193.xlsx); https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2014005 (1920_t165.xlsx)
- **Tokyo was a city of grand, not humble, carriages.** In the 1907 yearbook table (repository date 31 Mar 1907), Tokyo had **106 one-horse and 130 two-horse passenger carriages (236 in all)**, against 4,783 one-horse freight wagons. Nationally the split was 6,139 one-horse to 466 two-horse. Tokyo alone held more than a quarter of Japan's two-horse passenger carriages. — https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2022999 (1907_t291.xlsx)
- Tokyo at the end of FY1912: 217 passenger carriages, 6,148 freight carriages, 22,394 rickshaws, 150,215 handcarts, 32,089 bicycles, 304 automobiles. At the end of FY1919: 105 passenger carriages, 2,190 passenger cars, 19,429 rickshaws. — https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2008167; https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2014005
- Road building for carriages ("馬車道") is a constant newspaper theme. In 1878 a carriage road to the navy observatory at Iigura-Mamiana was built past peers' mansions ("華族の邸宅が道筋に"). In 1888 the Toranomon Aoizaka–Akasaka-mitsuke carriage road contract was won for about ¥21,300. In 1890 the Metropolitan Police privately warned carriage operators not to overwork their horses. (Yomiuri headlines cited in T. Sireerat, "Tokyo under the Hoof", *jsn Journal* 14:1, 2024.) — https://so04.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/jsn/article/view/270264
- **Rules of the road.** The Metropolitan Police's 道路取締規則 of **21 June 1900** first adopted keep-left (左側通行). Its drafter, 松井茂, said one model was the 1889 (Meiji 22) 人力車営業取締規則 (rickshaw business regulations), under which vehicles meeting each other "互いに左に避ける" (each pass to the left). Keep-left became national on 1 Dec 1920 under the Home Ministry's 道路取締令. The idea that Japan copied Britain is called wrong. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/左側通行
- **Coachmen.** 馭者/御者 (gyosha) drove. 別当 (bettō), 馬丁, 馬手 were old words for grooms and horse attendants. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/馬丁. I found no source with numbers or wages for private coachmen, or saying whether gentlemen drove themselves. See the last section.

### Rokumeikan carriage culture (context)
- The Rokumeikan was built 1880–83 to a design by Josiah Conder, at a total cost of ¥180,000, on 8,532 tsubo of the former Satsuma 装束屋敷 site at Uchiyamashita-chō. The opening banquet on 28 Nov 1883 had about 1,200 guests (it was Inoue Kaoru's birthday). The main gate was the former Satsuma mansion's 黒門 "Black Gate". — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/鹿鳴館
- On the night of a ball the ground floor held cloakrooms separated by sex and rank (帽衣預所) and smoking rooms. The upstairs ballroom was used for waltz, polka and quadrille. The peak event was the Tenchōsetsu ball of 3 Nov 1886. Geisha trained in dance were brought in to make up numbers, because few Japanese women could dance. The Fancy Ball of 20 Apr 1887 was held at the Prime Minister's residence (350 guests; Itō as a Venetian noble, Inoue as a Mikawa manzai performer, Yamagata as a Kiheitai soldier). The era ended with Inoue's resignation in September 1887. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/鹿鳴館

### Jinrikisha (人力車)
- 和泉要助 Izumi Yōsuke, 高山幸助 Takayama Kōsuke and 鈴木徳次郎 Suzuki Tokujirō are officially credited as inventors (made by 西田虎次郎 per an 1899 Diet petition). They began operating near the notice-board site at the south end of Nihonbashi on Meiji 3/3/23 (= c. 23 Apr 1870, conv.). In 1870 Tokyo Prefecture licensed the three to build and sell rickshaws, on condition the vehicles not be showy and that accidents be punished. They styled themselves "人力車総行司", but the licence became meaningless within a few years. Driver licences began the same year. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/人力車
- Counts: in "明治4年旧暦12月" (≈ January 1872) there were said to be **40,000 in Tokyo Prefecture**, a few dozen in Kyoto, and one each in Shizuoka and Kusatsu. 114,000 nationwide in FY1875. **25,038 in Tokyo in 1876**. Over 200,000 nationwide by the end of the century. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/人力車. Contrary figure: "1871年末時点で1万台近く" in Tokyo and over 40,000 at the peak around 1900 (枝久保達也, trafficnews 2023). — https://trafficnews.jp/post/126269
- LTES national rickshaw counts: 102,529 (1875), 144,478 (1880), 149,452 (1885), 170,237 (1890), 186,163 (1895), 184,851 (1900), 148,049 (1905), 120,809 (1912). Yearbook: 204,419 (end of FY1898). Tokyo: 27,203 one-seat + 1,848 two-seat (1907 table); 22,394 (end of FY1912). — https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2019988; https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2022999; https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2008167
- When Tokyo's electric trams opened in 1903 there were 43,000 pullers; two years later there were 26,000. There were also women pullers. 秋葉大助 of Ginza designed the hooded, mud-guarded luxury rickshaw and exported many. Exports began in the mid-1870s, and about 10,000 a year went to India in the Meiji 40s. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/人力車
- Pullers covered 30–50 km a day at about 8 km/h. Well-off families kept their own puller. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rickshaw

### Omnibuses (乗合馬車) and the 円太郎馬車
- Yokohama–Tokyo omnibus. Sekai Daihyakka: 1869, by 川名幸左衛門, 下岡蓮杖 and others (8 people). Nipponica: application February 1869, service only from September 1870, under the name 成駒屋 Narikomaya. Bus Magazine: Narikomaya ran Yokohama–Nihonbashi from Meiji 2/5 (1869), took 4 hours, fare 75 sen, and closed when the railway opened in 1872. — https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%A6%AC%E8%BB%8A-114246; https://busmagazine.bestcarweb.jp/feature/column/205327
- In 1872 a Tokyo city line charged "一区一銭" between Asakusa Kaminarimon and Shinbashi (Nipponica). It ran until the horse tram opened in 1882 (Bus Magazine). In 1874 the 千里軒 of Asakusa Hirokōji ran Asakusa–Shinbashi (Sekai Daihyakka). — https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%A6%AC%E8%BB%8A-114246
- The omnibus driver blew a horn. The rakugo storyteller 4th 橘家円太郎 imitated it on stage, so omnibuses were nicknamed **円太郎馬車** (Nipponica). — https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%A6%AC%E8%BB%8A-114246
- The December 1880 馬車取締規則 limited omnibuses to 6 passengers for one horse and 10 for two. In 1881 a survey counted 121 carriages operating, mainly on Shinagawa–Asakusa. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/馬車; https://trafficnews.jp/post/126269
- Trafficnews also describes a 30-passenger double-deck "大馬車" drawn by four horses, making 6 round trips a day from 6:00 to 20:00. The article's context does not make clear which line this was. — https://trafficnews.jp/post/126269

### The horse tram: 東京馬車鉄道 Tokyo Basha Tetsudō
- Promoters 種田誠一 and others petitioned Governor 松田道之 in 1880. The company was approved on 28 Dec 1880 with capital ¥300,000. It opened on **25 June 1882** between Shinbashi and Nihonbashi (about 2.5 km). By October the Nihonbashi–Ueno–Asakusa–Honishichō–Nihonbashi loop was complete. Gauge was 1,372 mm. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東京馬車鉄道; https://www.toshiseibi.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/documents/d/toshiseibi/pdf_keikaku_chousa_singikai_pdf_tokyotoshizukuri_1_02
- At opening it had **31 cars, all British-built**, and 47 horses, rising to 226 horses by year-end. Fare was 3 sen first class / 2 sen second class per section. Passengers: 1,106,623 in 1882; 19,334,480 in 1903, with 300 cars and 1,174 horses. It absorbed the Shinagawa horse tram in 1899. In 1903 it was renamed 東京電車鉄道 and ran Tokyo's first electric trams (Shinagawa–Shinbashi), with full electrification the next year. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東京馬車鉄道; Tokyo PDF above
- The line linked Shinbashi station (south gate of the city) with Ueno station (opened 1883). A photograph of about 1893 shows an omnibus stand at Meganebashi. — Tokyo PDF above

## 2. First automobiles in Japan

- **Before cars.** Japan's first press report on cars appeared in November 1895 (*東洋学芸雑誌*, on the Paris–Bordeaux race). In January 1896 十文字信介 imported a Hildebrand & Wolfmüller motorcycle and demonstrated it in front of the Imperial Palace. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本における自動車の年表
- **1898: the first four-wheeled car.** The French engineer Jean-Marie Thévenet brought a petrol **Panhard-Levassor** (1897 model, "Mail Phaeton" body, probably delivered 8 Nov 1897; 2-cylinder, about 1,206 cc, 4 hp; tiller steering). It was probably carried on the French steamer *Laos*, which reached Yokohama on 31 Dec 1897. On **6 Feb 1898** he drove it from the Hotel Métropole at Tsukiji to Ueno Park (about 6 km), the first car run in Japan. Bigot drew it ("東京に初めて出現した自動車", *In the Far East*, Feb. 1898). It was put up for auction on 11 Mar 1898; the top bid was **¥5,300 against a ¥6,000 reserve**, so it went back to France (arriving 1 July 1898). The story was rediscovered by 齊藤俊彦 (1987) and proved by Toyota Automobile Museum (1996, a photo in *La France Automobile*, 16 Apr 1898). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本への自動車の渡来
- **The car for the Crown Prince's wedding (1900): verified as real, but it was not the first car.** Japanese residents of San Francisco, led by Consul 陸奥廣吉, raised **$5,311.15** for the wedding of Crown Prince Yoshihito and Kujō Sadako (10 May 1900). They earned about $1 a day (about ¥2 at the time), and many gave a day's or half a day's pay. They chose between a piano and a compressed-air car, then switched on 8 May 1900 to a ready-made luxury car, probably a **Woods electric**. Purchase, freight and insurance cost **$3,100**. It sailed on the 亜米利加丸 from San Francisco on 2 Aug 1900, reached Yokohama on 22 Aug, and was handed to the Imperial Household Ministry in September via 古河潤吉. Tokyo Dentō could not supply the DC needed to charge it, so 高田商会 did. Its engineer **廣田精一** drove it in the Aoyama parade ground (1901) and is thought to be the first Japanese to drive a car in Japan. On the second day, with a 2-ton load for a brake test, another driver had a minor accident near Miyakezaka or Kinokunizaka (stories vary: "into the moat", "avoiding a police box"). This is counted as Japan's first car accident. When it was later demonstrated to the Crown Prince, he reportedly said "自動車とはことの外速度のおそきものである" ("cars are surprisingly slow"). The Crown Prince watched a test run on 4 May 1902. It was moved to the Akasaka stables storehouse in 1903 and never used. Before the 1960s it was popularly called "Japan's first car", a story spread by 石井研堂's 『明治事物起原』 (1926 edition). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/皇太子献納車; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本への自動車の渡来; popular retelling: https://president.jp/articles/-/114470
- **Other early cars.** Niagara steam car (Bruhl Bros., March 1901), the first car imported for sale. On 22 June 1901 Richard Abenheim drove it Yokohama–Shinbashi (about 30 km), stopping at Yamagata Aritomo's mansion to give rides. In August 1901 吉田真太郎 (双輪商会, Ginza) got a Gladiator four-wheeler from a Russian legation secretary and is counted as the first owner-driver. On 3 Nov 1901 Japan's first motor race was held at Shinobazu Pond (3 vehicles). The **モーター商会**, the first Japanese-run car dealer, opened in Ginza in November 1901. In April 1902, 8 Locomobile steam cars arrived. 川田龍吉's Locomobile (September 1902) is the oldest surviving car brought to Japan and was used for commuting. 浅野総一郎 bought one too. In July 1902 an Englishman injured a shoemaker near the Yokohama Grand Hotel, Japan's first car injury. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本への自動車の渡来; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本における自動車の年表
- **1903.** Cars were exhibited and demonstrated at the 5th National Industrial Exhibition in Osaka (1 Mar–31 Jul 1903), the first time most Japanese saw one. Mitsui Gofukuten (later Mitsukoshi) took delivery of a French Clément delivery van in April. Aichi issued Japan's first motor-vehicle rules on 20 Aug 1903. Kyoto's 二井商会 began bus service (licensed 21 Nov 1903). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本における自動車の年表
- **1904: Yamaba Torao's steam bus (山羽式蒸気自動車).** After seeing the 1903 exhibition, 森房造 and 楠健太郎 decided in late April 1903 to build a car and hired 山羽虎夫 (born Okayama 1874). It was finished and test-run on **7 May 1904**, counted as the first car built in Japan. Later sources describe it as a 10-seat bus with a 2-cylinder, 25 hp steam engine, meant for an Okayama route. Tyre problems kept it out of service. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本における自動車の年表; Okayama City notice https://www.city.okayama.jp/shisei/0000018391.html (snippet only; not opened); webCG https://www.webcg.net/articles/-/37342 (snippet only; not opened)
- **1905–1906.** Japan's first bus service, Yokohama Station–Kabe (Hiroshima), opened 5 Feb 1905. Prince **有栖川宮威仁** brought home a French Darracq bought in England (arrived 11 Oct 1905) and drove it to the palace on 12 Oct 1905, the first time anyone came to court by car. Japan's first fatal car accident was at Sakai on 26 Oct 1905: a 5-year-old was run over. On 30 Mar 1906 a 50% tariff was put on cars. In November 1906 Prince Arisugawa commissioned a domestic car from 吉田真太郎 and **内山駒之助**, and Yoshida founded the 東京自動車製作所 at Kobikichō. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本における自動車の年表
- **1907.** In February the Metropolitan Police issued the 自動車取締規則. Licences were required: licence no. 1 went to 渡辺守貞, chauffeur to Mitsui Bank president 三井高保. Number plates were required: no. 1 went to Meidi-ya. In September 1907 the first **Takuri (タクリー号)** was largely complete. Prince Arisugawa tried it, and it was presented to him at the end of November, with special paint and trim. It was the first petrol car built in Japan. Commonly said to number about 10, named for its rattling ("ガタクリ") running. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本における自動車の年表; Kodansha book blurb https://www.kodansha.co.jp/titles/1000034790
- **1908–1912.** On 1 Aug 1908, 10 cars led by Prince Arisugawa drove from Hibiya Park to Tachikawa. On 15 Dec 1908 the Post Office first used motor mail vans in Tokyo. The Japan Automobile Club was founded at the Imperial Hotel on 20 Dec 1910. Japan's first taxi company, タクシー自働車株式会社 (Yūrakuchō), was founded 10 July 1912 and began service on 15 Aug 1912 with Model T Fords. For Emperor Meiji's funeral (13 Sep 1912) the Metropolitan Police bought its first car. Nine imperial cars (Daimler, Mercedes, Fiat) arrived between 31 Dec 1912 and February 1913. Emperor Taishō first rode in a car on 14 Feb 1913. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本における自動車の年表
- **Number of cars.** The Imperial Statistical Yearbook shows **535 automobiles nationwide at the end of FY1912 (31 Mar 1913), of which 304 were in Tokyo**. Then 761 (end FY1913), 1,284 passenger + 23 goods (FY1916), 5,109 + 444 (FY1919). The 1907 table has no automobile column at all. NDL Reference says no Meiji-era national car totals can be found in the standard long-term series. — https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2008167; https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2014005; https://crd.ndl.go.jp/reference/entry/index.php?page=ref_view&id=1000193608
- **Who owned cars.** Owners were upper-class hobbyists and firms: princes (Arisugawa), zaibatsu (Mitsui's chauffeur held licence no. 1), Meidi-ya, Ōkura Kishichirō (raced at Brooklands on 6 July 1907, second in the Montagu Cup; founded a car company in 1909), and foreign merchants. Cars were "骨董趣味の変形" (a variant of curio-collecting) and "上流階級の者たちの道楽" (an upper-class indulgence). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本における自動車の年表

## 3. Lighting and communications

### Gas lamps (ガス灯)
- The first Western gas lamps in Japan were lit in 1871 at the Osaka Mint, using gas from its machinery, inside the works and in nearby streets. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ガス灯
- **Yokohama.** The Yokohama Gas Company (高島嘉右衛門 with French engineer Henri Pelegrin) lit lamps on **Meiji 5/9/29 = 31 Oct 1872** from Ōebashi along Bashamichi and Honchō-dōri. 31 October is Japan's "Gas Day"; the Chinese-calendar library gives 30 Oct, a known one-day difference. The Wikipedia text gives the lunar date only. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ガス灯
- **Tokyo/Ginza.** On **18 Dec 1874 (Meiji 7)**, 85 gas street lamps were lit along Ginza-dōri from Shiba Kanasugibashi to Kyōbashi, from a gas works at 芝浜崎町. Pelegrin surveyed the route himself. Spacing was about 45 m from Kanasugibashi to Shinbashi and about 27 m from Shinbashi on. **Monthly charge per lamp: ¥3.555 (3円55銭5厘)**, billed to the property owner in front, many of whom had not asked for a lamp and did not pay. The equipment had been bought in 1871 with money from the Edo townsmen's 七分積金 fund and sat in a Fukagawa warehouse for three years. The Tokyo Prefecture ran it from May 1876; Shibusawa Eiichi pushed indoor gas. It was privatized as 東京瓦斯會社 in 1885. **Lamplighters (点消方 tenshōkata)** wore identifying hanten coats, carried fire-sticks (two at first, later one), and each looked after 50 to nearly 100 lamps; they worked in Tokyo until early Shōwa. Gas mantle lamps came in the Meiji 30s. Peak: **5,792 gas street lamps in Tokyo in 1917**. (Gas Museum exhibition text, 2014, curator 高橋豊.) — https://www.gasmuseum.jp/wp-content/uploads/gallery_sep2014.pdf
- Wikipedia gives Ginza's gas lamps as 1873 and says electric lamps outnumbered gas lamps in 1909 and gas lamps had "already vanished from Tokyo city" by 1913 (citing 下川耿史). Both conflict with the Gas Museum (1874; peak 1917). Also, in November 1880 a kabuki play at the Shintomi-za used gas light for a ghost effect. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ガス灯

### Electric light
- **25 Mar 1878**: Prof. W. E. Ayrton lit an arc lamp powered by Grove cells at the Kōbu Daigakkō hall, for the banquet opening the Central Telegraph Office. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/アーク灯
- **1882, Ginza**: the Tokyo Electric Light founders' office lit a **2,000-candlepower arc lamp in front of the Ōkura-gumi** in Ginza to drum up interest. Citizens marvelled and crowded there all night (*Tokyo Nichinichi*, 1 Nov 1882). The woodblock print 歌川重清「東京銀座通電氣燈建設之圖」 says its light "reached tens of chō as if it were broad day". In 1884 the Naka-za in Dōtonbori used 6 stage arc lamps. On 20 Sep 1886 Osaka Spinning used arc lamps for night work, the first private use. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/アーク灯
- **東京電燈 Tokyo Dentō.** Application on 18 Mar 1882 (Shibusawa Eiichi, Ōkura Kihachirō). Permission 15 Feb 1883 (capital ¥200,000; founders including 矢島作郎, 藤岡市助, 大倉喜八郎, 蜂須賀茂韶). Business began 5 July 1886. Supply began from Nihonbashi Kayabachō in November 1887. On **29 Nov 1887** its 第二電燈局 began city distribution, called "the first public supply", DC, thermal. Asakusa thermal station, with domestic 200 kW AC generators, was begun in 1893 and finished three years later. The company was bought by the Kōshū financier 若尾逸平 in 1895. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東京電燈
- At the 3rd National Industrial Exhibition (Ueno, 1890), Tokyo Dentō ran Japan's first electric tram (Sprague type). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/内国勧業博覧会
- In summer 1888 the Asakusa "Fujiyama" tower lit its summit electrically (25 July–30 September) at a cost of **¥120 a month**, while charging 4 sen admission. The power may have come from the 福住温泉 at Senzoku-mura, which had generated its own power with a 9 hp steam engine since 7 May 1885. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/富士山縦覧場

### Telephone
- **1877**: two telephones, the Bell model then on sale, were imported via Bavier & Co., Yokohama, a year after Bell's invention. The Tokyo Fire Department says November 1877. The Kōbushō (Ministry of Public Works) tested them. In **June 1878** the telegraph office's machine shop copied them: the first Japanese-made telephones, 41 made over about 5 years, stopped in 1883 because they were too faint. — https://www.ntt-west.co.jp/info/databook/pdf/081-088_denwakiayumi.pdf; http://www.tfd.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/learning/elib/qa/qa_68.html
- In 1885 a Tokyo–Yokohama long-distance test succeeded, and the Ministry of Communications (逓信省) was created. In 1887 the Gower-Bell set was imported. In 1889 the Tokyo–Atami test was "the first public call". — TFD page; NTT West PDF above
- **16 Dec 1890**: exchange service opened between Tokyo and Yokohama with **197 subscribers (Tokyo 155, Yokohama 42)** and **16 電話所** call offices, an early form of public phone at telegraph-office counters (15 Tokyo, 1 Yokohama). Rates: **annual fee ¥40 Tokyo, ¥35 Yokohama; Tokyo–Yokohama call 15 sen per 5 minutes**. — http://www.tfd.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/learning/elib/qa/qa_68.html; https://www.ntt-west.co.jp/info/databook/pdf/081-088_denwakiayumi.pdf
- In 1890 there were 11 operators in Tokyo, 9 of them women, and 4 in Yokohama. **In 1900 Tokyo alone had 330 women and 100 men operators.** In the 1920s short operators stood on geta to reach the high boards. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/電話交換手
- July 1896: Delville magneto phones. December 1897: the first desk telephones (甲号・乙号), "also useful as ornaments", for an extra ¥6 a year. February 1899: a Tokyo–Osaka trunk line opened with 178 subscribers at each end, **¥1.60 per 5 minutes**. From 1899 fees were tiered: ¥66 a year in Tokyo and Osaka, ¥54 in Kyoto/Yokohama/Nagoya/Kobe, ¥48 elsewhere. In May 1903 Kyoto got the first common-battery exchange ("gooseneck" sets); parts of Tokyo followed in 1909. — https://www.ntt-west.co.jp/info/databook/pdf/081-088_denwakiayumi.pdf
- Subscribers nationwide (fiscal year-end): 29,941 (FY1902), 35,013 (1903), 36,694 (1905), 58,562 (1907), 102,626 (1909), 157,167 (1911), **181,881 (FY1912), of which Tokyo 37,629** (12.19 per 1,000 people). Calls from public automatic telephones (自働電話): 326,874 in FY1902; 3,165,022 in FY1912. — https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2009100 (1914_t226.xlsx)
- Fire reports by telephone began at the end of 1887, and lines were run from fire HQ to the Saiwaibashi, Mansei-bashi and Asakusa-bashi posts. Fire calls cost money until 1 Apr 1919. — http://www.tfd.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/learning/elib/qa/qa_68.html
- Not found: the 1900 subscriber total (closest is FY1902, 29,941).

### Telegraph and post
- In 1869 (Meiji 2) British engineers strung a line between the Yokohama lighthouse office and the Yokohama court, using Breguet needle telegraphs that pointed at letters rather than Morse. Public Tokyo–Yokohama telegrams began in "1870年1月（明治2年12月）". The day commonly quoted, Meiji 2/12/25, converts to 26 Jan 1870 (conv.). In 1871 Great Northern laid the Vladivostok–Nagasaki cable. Tokyo–Nagasaki was linked in 1873. 1.65 million telegrams were sent in 1879. All prefectural capitals were linked by about 1890. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/電信
- Post: Maejima Hisoka proposed a postal system in Meiji 3/5 (1870). Edicts were issued Meiji 4/1/24 (= 14 Mar 1871, conv.). Service opened **Meiji 4/3/1 = 20 Apr 1871** between Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka, with the first post offices and the first stamps. In 1873 about 1,100 village headmen agreed to run postal agencies from their homes. The 1882 郵便条例 set first- to fourth-class mail. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/郵便
- A postcard cost 1 sen anywhere in Japan from 1873. — Gas Museum PDF above

## 4. Elevators, towers, the 1890 exhibition

### Ryōunkaku 凌雲閣 (Asakusa Jūnikai, "Twelve Storeys")
- **Opened 11 Nov 1890.** It was first announced for 10 November and put back a day for the guests' convenience (*Yomiuri* 4 Nov 1890; *Kokumin* 12 Nov 1890). Japan's "Elevator Day" (10 November) follows the original announcement. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/凌雲閣; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/エレベーター
- Proposed by 福原庄七, a wealthy merchant from Nagaoka. Basic design by **William K. Burton** (British, an oyatoi engineer and photographer). Site works under 伊澤雄司. The first president of Ryōunkaku Co. was the photographer and city councillor **江崎礼二**. **Height 173 shaku (about 52 m)** (first reported as 220 shaku). Brick to the 10th floor, wood on the 11th and 12th. Footprint 122.31 m². Address 浅草区千束町二丁目三十八番地. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/凌雲閣
- Admission **8 sen adults, 4 sen children**. Floors 2–7 held 46 shops selling foreign goods. Floor 8 was a rest room. Floors 10–12 were viewing rooms with telescopes, and the view reached the mountains of the eight Kantō provinces. Three electric lamps per floor shone out of 176 windows (*風俗画報* no. 23, 1890). 沖牙太郎 (founder of Oki Electric) put in telephones for publicity. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/凌雲閣
- **The elevator.** Japan's first passenger (electric) lift. Its design was reported as by 藤岡市助 of Tokyo Dentō (*Yomiuri* 8 Apr 1890); Burton's relatives later said he opposed installing it. Ryōunkaku article: two 10-person cars, 7 hp motor, running between floors 1 and 8 only. Elevator article: one 7.5 hp DC motor in the basement driving two counterbalanced cars in an "M"-shaped rope arrangement, stopping only at floors 1 and 8; about 15 m/min; each car about the size of 3 tatami with cushioned benches; hemp ropes; belt-shifted reversing; written "エレベートル". It broke down repeatedly from opening day. **It was stopped in May 1891** after an engineer sent by the Metropolitan Police declared it "危険なり" (dangerous), and a lawsuit with Tokyo Dentō followed. It was put back in 1914. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/凌雲閣; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/エレベーター
- In 1892 the American Robert Gardiner wrote that the electric lift was badly kept and he climbed the stairs. He still recommended the tower as a first stop in Tokyo. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/凌雲閣
- Later: the 十二階演芸場 opened downstairs on 1 June 1911, and the 浅草国技館 opened next door in February 1912. In the Great Kantō Earthquake of 1 Sep 1923 it broke off above the 8th floor. Of 12–13 visitors, all but one died; the survivor was caught on a 福助足袋 signboard. Army engineers blew it up on 23 Sep 1923. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/凌雲閣
- **Other lifts.** The 玄鹿館 photo studio (Kobikichō 5-chōme, Ginza, opened 1895) was a 150-tsubo Western-style building with "エレベーター設備や回り舞台" (an elevator and a revolving stage) and a **2,500-candlepower arc lamp** for night portraits. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/鹿島清兵衛. In 1896 an Otis hydraulic goods lift for moving coin (30 m/min) went into the Bank of Japan, the first imported Otis. In 1915 the first Japanese-made passenger lift (東松式 push-button automatic) was installed at Itō Marubeni Gofukuten in Osaka. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/エレベーター. I found no earlier hydraulic or hand-cranked passenger lift (see the last section).

### The towers before it
- **富士山縦覧場 "Asakusa Fuji"** (Asakusa Park, 6th district, lot 4). A papier-mâché-and-plaster Mount Fuji on a timber frame: 18 ken (32 m) high, on 1,054 tsubo, with a flat 25-tsubo summit holding several telescopes. Visitors climbed 200 ken up the inside and came down 230 ken on the outside, ten turns each way. It was finished in October 1887 and opened 6 Nov 1887; one entry says 4 sen admission. People mocked it as "栄螺を伏せた" (an upturned turban shell) and "さざえの化物". It was badly damaged in the storm of 31 Aug 1889. A stuffed Indian python was put on the summit in December 1889 to win back visitors. Demolition began 14 Feb 1890, and the Nihon Panorama-kan took its place. It was inspired by the 1 sen charged in 1886 to climb the Sensōji pagoda's repair scaffolding. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/富士山縦覧場
- Osaka had the 眺望閣 (1888, 5 storeys, 31 m, "Minami no Gokai") and its own 凌雲閣 (1889, 9 storeys, 39 m, "Kita no Kyūkai"). Kipling visited the Osaka tower in 1889 and called it "the Eiffel Tower of Osaka". — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/凌雲閣

### The 3rd National Industrial Exhibition (第三回内国勧業博覧会)
- Ueno Park, **1 Apr–31 Jul 1890 (122 days); 1,023,693 visitors**. Tokyo Dentō ran Japan's first electric tram (Sprague). Uemura Shōen, aged 15, won a first-class certificate (bought by the Duke of Connaught). Toyoda Sakichi visited. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/内国勧業博覧会
- Kashima Seibei exhibited giant photographs there, and Meidi-ya exhibited a beer poster photographed by Kashima with the geisha **ぽん太** as model. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/鹿島清兵衛
- A popular theory holds that Ryōunkaku was built to cash in on the exhibition crowds. — https://n-elekyo.or.jp/about/elevatorjournal/pdf/Journal39_08.pdf (search snippet; the PDF itself gives only the opening fact)

## 5. Astronomy, stars, lantern shows, planetaria

### Tokyo Astronomical Observatory (東京天文台)
- In 1872 the Navy Hydrographic Office set up an 観象台 at 麻布区飯倉 (now Azabudai). In **1888** the Tokyo University observatory, the Navy observatory and the astronomy section of the Home Ministry's Geography Bureau were merged on the Navy site as the **東京天文台**, attached to the Imperial University, and meteorology was split off to the Central Meteorological Observatory. The first director was **寺尾寿 Terao Hisashi (1888–1919)**. Instruments included a Troughton & Simms telescope bought by the Home Ministry in 1880 (now an Important Cultural Property). Buildings were damaged in the 1894 Meiji Tokyo earthquake. City lights made Azabu unsuitable: land at Mitaka was bought in 1909, building began in 1914, and the move came in 1924. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東京天文台
- The Astronomical Society of Japan was founded **19 Jan 1908** with Terao among the founders. Its popular monthly is 『天文月報』. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本天文学会

### Lantern (幻燈 gentō) culture
- In 1803 a Dutch "エキマン鏡" show inspired 都屋都楽 to create **写し絵 (utsushi-e)**, a lantern performance for the variety halls. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/幻灯機
- **1874**: Ministry of Education official **手島精一 Teshima Seiichi** brought back from America, per Ishii Kendō, a lantern and slides: "**天文十七枚、自然現象十二枚、人身解剖二十枚、動物二十一枚**" (17 astronomy, 12 natural phenomena, 20 anatomy, 21 animals). Teshima ran the Education Museum 1877–1888. (岩田, Chūkyō Univ. paper; 佐藤知条, *湘北紀要* 33, 2012.) — https://chukyo-u.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/18534/files/102010020102iwata-chukyo-u.pdf; https://shohoku.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/742/files/KJ00008624779.pdf
- **1880**: Teshima tried to have lanterns supplied to every prefectural normal school, commissioning the photographers **鶴淵初蔵 Tsurubuchi Hatsuzō** and **中島待乳 Nakajima Matsuchi**. (青山貴子 2008, *生涯学習・社会教育学研究* 33.) — https://repository.dl.itc.u-tokyo.ac.jp/record/25113/files/life33_3.pdf. Artscape: the Ministry of Education commissioned both men. Slides were hand-painted or photographic, and Nakajima's wife 秋尾園 painted slides. — https://artscape.jp/artword/5885/
- 13 Mar 1884: educational use of lanterns was promoted inside the Ministry of Education (Iwata). In 1886 Tsurubuchi edited and published 『人智啓発 幻燈図解』 no. 1 (planned by 竹内宰爾). — https://www.museum.or.jp/report/1088 (Tokyo Photographic Art Museum exhibition "マジック・ランタン", 14 Aug–14 Oct 2018)
- About 1892, Tsurubuchi's shop **鶴淵幻燈舗** issued a sales catalogue, 『教育学術 改良幻燈器械及映画定価表』, with slide sets on astronomy (天文), physics, natural phenomena, human and pregnancy anatomy, hygiene, animals, plants, silkworms, the 幼学綱要, the Age of the Gods, and the life of the Buddha. Single slides covered ethics, history, flowers, famous people, world customs, "日本貴顕" (Japanese dignitaries), "**内外婦人肖像**" (portraits of Japanese and foreign women), foreign buildings, and the Nōbi earthquake. — Aoyama 2008, above
- The Toraku catalogue (1895) still offered "**天文學の部 運轉付**", a 17-slide astronomy set with hand-cranked rotating slides, matching Teshima's 17. Kyoto University Museum holds 20 educational lantern slides of 1886 including astronomy (examined 28 Jul 2021). The 1889 『幻灯使用法』 (教育品製造会社) recommends lanterns for lectures on "博物・天文・地質・自然現象・生理・衛生・歴史・修身". — Iwata paper; Aoyama paper above
- From the late 1880s schools bought lanterns, and "教育幻燈会" (educational lantern evenings) were attended by local adults as well as pupils. A lantern boom came in the Meiji 20s (1887–96). Charity lantern shows were held during the Sino- and Russo-Japanese Wars. — https://shohoku.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/742/files/KJ00008624779.pdf; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/幻灯機; https://artscape.jp/artword/5885/

### Planetaria (for scale only)
- **Osaka Municipal Electric Science Museum (大阪市立電気科学館)** opened **13 Mar 1937** with Japan's first planetarium, a Zeiss Model II on the 6th floor. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/大阪市立電気科学館
- **Tokyo: 東日天文館** opened **30 Oct 1938** in the Tokyo Nichinichi Shimbun building, Yūrakuchō, with a Zeiss II whose projector cost ¥500,000. Admission was 50 sen adults, 25 sen children. It burned in the 1945 air raids. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東日天文館

## 6. Roses, horticulture, greenhouses, glass

### Western roses in Meiji Japan
- Japan had native roses (ノイバラ, テリハノイバラ, ハマナス) and Chinese garden roses (モッコウバラ, コウシンバラ) under the Tokugawa. Wikipedia says that after the Restoration the government imported **'La France' (Japanese name 天地開)** as a trial plant and grew it at the **青山官制農園** (Aoyama government farm). So many people came to smell it that the plant was **fenced with wire mesh**. Because roses are propagated by grafting, growing spread to grafting centres at 安行 (Kawaguchi, Saitama) and 山本 (Takarazuka, Hyōgo), with patrons among the imperial family, peers and senior officials. Roses became popular only in the Taishō–Shōwa period. **This passage is unsourced on Wikipedia.** — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/バラ
- The 駒場バラ園 (Komaba rose garden, Meguro) is listed as opened Meiji 44 (1911). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/バラ
- 'La France' (Guillot, 1867) is the first modern rose, and the hybrid-tea class was named in 1882. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/バラ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_roses
- Not found: rose societies, rose shows, a Meiji "rose boom", rose prices, or roses at Shinjuku before 2000 (the present beds date from 2000/2002 — https://policies.env.go.jp/national-garden/shinjukugyoen/highlights/rose/).

### Japanese wild roses in Europe
- **Rosa multiflora** (ノイバラ; Thunb. 1784; syn. *R. polyantha* Siebold & Zucc. 1845) is a parent of the **polyantha** roses, which appeared in France in the late 19th century from *R. chinensis* × *R. multiflora*. In 1907 Dines Poulsen crossed polyantha and hybrid tea ('Rödhätte'), founding the floribundas. *R. multiflora* is also a parent of the hybrid musks ('Aglaia', Lambert 1896). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ノイバラ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_roses
- **Rosa wichuraiana / R. luciae** (テリハノイバラ; described 1871) is a parent of the ramblers 'Dorothy Perkins', 'Albéric Barbier', 'New Dawn' and 'Albertine'. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/テリハノイバラ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosa_luciae
- **Rosa rugosa** (ハマナス) reached England from Japan in 1796, Germany in 1845 and North America in 1845, and was naturalized on Nantucket by 1899. Hybrid rugosas include 'Blanc Double de Coubert' (Cochet 1893). "Garden roses" says rugosa was "introduced into the West circa the 1880s", which conflicts with 1796. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosa_rugosa; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_roses

### Shinjuku: experimental station → imperial garden (official Ministry of Environment history)
- 1872: the Finance Ministry set up the **内藤新宿試験場** on the former Naitō mansion (58.3 ha), growing about 3,000 kinds of fruit and vegetables, Western ones included. 1874: 農事修学所 (later Komaba Agricultural School). **1875: the first glasshouse, about 100 m²**. May 1879: handed to the Imperial Household as the **植物御苑**. 1886: renamed 新宿御料地. **1892: a proper heated Western-style greenhouse ("加温式の本格的な洋風温室")**, especially for orchids. 1896: Western-style rest house (御休所) for imperial visits to the greenhouse. 1898: Fukuba Hayato put in charge. 1900: Fukuba exhibited three large-format chrysanthemums at the Paris Exposition and asked Henri Martinet to design the remodelling. 1902–06: remodelled; reopened as **新宿御苑** in May 1906. **1914: over 800 orchids grown, including names such as *Cattleya* Shinjuku and *Laelia* Shinjuku.** Cherry-viewing parties moved there in 1917. — https://policies.env.go.jp/national-garden/shinjukugyoen/intro/history/; https://policies.env.go.jp/national-garden/shinjukugyoen/highlights/greenhouse/
- **福羽逸人 Fukuba Hayato**: born 13 Dec 1856 (Ansei 3/11/16), died 19 May 1921. The official site says he joined the station as a trainee in 1877; Wikipedia says 1875. Official site: he studied in France and Germany for three years from 1886. Wikipedia: Italy and France, then America. He taught Japan's first horticulture course in 1890 and became an imperial technician in 1891. His strawberry, raised from Vilmorin 'General Chanzy' seed, was announced in 1899 (Wikipedia) or made in Meiji 31/1898 (official site); it was the first Japanese strawberry variety. He grew melons and Muscat of Alexandria under glass and served hothouse produce and orchid decorations at palace banquets. He designed the Western flowerbeds at Hibiya Park (opened 1903), a glasshouse for Changgyeonggung in Korea (1907), and the Muko (Suma) detached palace garden (1908). He became a viscount in 1907. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/福羽逸人; Shinjuku official pages above
- Wikipedia: "日本で最初のガラスを使った温室は、1870年に東京青山の開拓使官園に設置された" (Japan's first glass greenhouse was built in 1870 at the Kaitakushi garden in Aoyama). In the 1890s Fukuba introduced wooden frames covered with glass sashes in place of oil-paper ones. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/温室 (see the last section: the Aoyama Kaitakushi garden is usually dated 1871–72)

### Lily bulbs and the Yokohama Nursery Co. (横浜植木)
- Founded **7 Feb 1890 (Meiji 23)** as 有限責任横浜植木商会 under **鈴木卯兵衛 Suzuki Uhei**, the first Japanese firm to import and export plants, with a San Francisco branch. Incorporated 1 Jun 1891 (capital ¥50,000). Renamed 横浜植木株式会社 in October 1893, when it showed bonsai and a Japanese garden at the Chicago World's Fair. New York office December 1898. Iris (hanashōbu) garden at Kamata March 1903. London branch February 1907. Grand prize and two RHS silver cups at the 1910 Japan–British Exhibition. Handled the export of Tokyo City's cherry trees to Washington in 1912. Exhibited at Chelsea every year 1913–1939. — https://www.yokohamaueki.co.jp/corporate/profile/
- Japanese lilies became a craze after the **1873 Vienna exhibition**. Yokohama Nursery published the catalogue 『百合花選』 in 1894. **In 1908 Japan exported nearly 12 million lily bulbs worth nearly ¥450,000, mostly to Britain and the US; Yokohama handled over 90%.** The quoted price of Easter-lily bulbs in Yokohama was "一本あたり2円50銭～5円" (data from the Agriculture Bureau survey of May 1909; JACAR B06151090600). Okinoerabu lilies were sold abroad as "永良部百合". — https://www.jacar.go.jp/modernjapan/p02.html
- *L. longiflorum* bulbs went to England in 1819 and again in 1853, then via Bermuda to Philadelphia in the 1880s (Thomas Sargent). A virus wiped out Bermuda's lilies in 1898, and Japan became the main supplier, sending about 30 million bulbs a year to the US until 1941. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/テッポウユリ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lilium_longiflorum
- ja Wikipedia also claims lily bulbs were Japan's second-largest export after silk. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ユリ (doubtful; see the last section)

### Glass
- After the **品川硝子製造所** (Shinagawa Glass Works) was set up in **1876 (Meiji 9)**, the word "ガラス（硝子）" replaced 玻璃・瑠璃・ビードロ・ギヤマン. In early Meiji, window glass went mainly into Western-style houses. From the Meiji 30s (1897–1906) it spread to schools, factories, offices, hospitals and better houses, and it reached ordinary homes only after prices fell in mid-Taishō. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ガラス
- The Kōbushō Shinagawa Glass building is preserved at Meiji-mura. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/博物館明治村

## 7. Rings and jewelry

Main source: 鈴木はる美「指輪の普及とその要因の探究―明治期から大正初期を中心に―」(ジュエリー文化史研究会), http://www.j-bunka.jp/article/data/suzuki_harumi_01.pdf, unless stated.

- **Early Meiji.** A Meiji 6/3 (1873) *雑誌* no. 81 noted "金銀の指環を掛ける者多し" (many wear gold or silver rings). 『明治事物起原』「指輪の始まり」 says: until about Meiji 10 (1877), gold-alloy or gilt-silver rings engraved with family crests were worn only by archery-booth girls (矢取女) and tea-stall waitresses (麦湯の姐さん). After the 1st National Industrial Exhibition (1877), **cheap Western-style imitations set with fake stones became the fashion until about Meiji 20 (1887)**. By Meiji 31 (1898), hardly a "gentleman" or "lady" lacked a ring. In the Meiji 10s (1877–86), geisha in Tsukioka Yoshitoshi prints wear wide flat rings (平打), and materials were domestic agate, silver and gold.
- **1890s.** In the Meiji 20s (1887–96) stone rings (pearl, diamond, ruby) came in and platinum became common. **Tenshōdō's first illustrated ring advertisement (1890)** showed 18 rings: none flat, 2 engraved, **16 set with stones**. In 1892 玉寶堂 advertised "美術金製指環 宮内省御用" (artistic gold rings, purveyor to the Imperial Household) in *風俗画報*. In April 1894 Tenshōdō advertised "寶玉入金製指環" (gold rings set with gems) in *国民之友*. In the Meiji 30s (1897–1906) mail-order catalogues spread rings nationwide.
- **Wedding and engagement rings.** 『東京風俗志』 (1899) describes Christian weddings in church in which the bridegroom gives his ring to the bride. In May 1903 Takashimaya's 『新衣裳』 reported an "Australian custom" of an engagement ring: diamond if the man is rich, otherwise the birth-month stone. **1904 (Meiji 37): 植田商店 ran the first wedding-ring advertisement, in 『服装新聞』**. — also https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/結婚指輪
- **The 1900 imperial wedding.** The "神前結婚式" (Shinto wedding) format took shape with Crown Prince Yoshihito's wedding on **10 May 1900**, held for the first time before the 賢所 (palace sanctuary). The 神宮奉賛会 (now Tokyo Daijingū) devised a civilian version and held a demonstration wedding on 3 Mar 1901. No source I opened mentions rings at the 1900 ceremony. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/神前式
- **In literature.**
  - **清水紫琴 Shimizu Shikin, 「こわれ指環」, 1891 (Meiji 24)**, her debut while a reporter at 『女学雑誌』. The heroine keeps wearing the ring she broke, renewing her resolve morning and evening. Shikin was born Shimizu Toyoko, Keiō 3/12 (January 1868), and died 31 Jul 1933. She left her first husband, who kept a mistress, in 1889. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/清水紫琴; Suzuki paper
  - **尾崎紅葉 『金色夜叉』**, serialized in the *Yomiuri* from 1 Jan 1897 to 11 May 1902 (unfinished). Diners in a restaurant gasp "金剛石!" (diamond!) at a ring, and Kan'ichi reproaches Miya for having had eyes for "300円のダイヤモンド" (a 300-yen diamond). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/金色夜叉; Suzuki paper
  - 内田魯庵「指輪」 (*時好*, 1905): "指環は此頃の女の命 … 金の延べは田舎臭く、彫刻のあるのは野暮臭く、廉ッぽい宝石は品格悪し如何にしても金剛石、金剛石、金剛石！" ("These days a ring is a woman's life… plain gold is rustic, engraving is vulgar, cheap stones lack class: it must be diamond, diamond, diamond!"). Natsume Sōseki's 『それから』 (1909): Daisuke gives Michiyo a wedding ring.
- **Prices.** In 1907 the *Jiji Shimpō* beauty contest offered as prize **a Tenshōdō ring worth ¥300**. The *Jiji* New Year supplement of 1 Jan 1908 was Okada Saburōsuke's 「ゆびわ」: a kimono-clad beauty with a diamond solitaire on her left ring finger, the same design as Tiffany's 1886 platinum solitaire. In May 1911 the 金田時計店 sold 18K rings, 12 designs, all at **¥2**. In January 1912 the いせ竹小間物店 sold crest rings at **18K ¥3, 9K ¥1.80**, and a third grade listed as "純金製 35銭" (probably a misprint for gilt).
- **Cultured pearls.** Mikimoto cultured half-pearls in 1893 (Meiji 26) and round pearls in 1905 (Meiji 38).

## 8. Clothing, hair, uniforms

- **Men's hair.** The 散髪脱刀令 of Meiji 4/8/9 (= 23 Sep 1871) permitted cropped hair and going without swords. Some held out: Shimazu Hisamitsu and Tadayoshi kept topknots, and Tadayoshi wore his at the 1889 Constitution ceremony. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/断髪令
- **Women's short hair forbidden.** Some women took the order as applying to them and cropped their hair, so Tokyo Prefecture issued a **女子断髪禁止令 on Meiji 5/4/5 (= 11 May 1872)**. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/断髪令
- **束髪 sokuhatsu (Western-influenced updo).** Promoted in 1885 (Meiji 18) by the physician 渡部鼎 and 石川暎作 through the 日本婦人束髪会 as hygienic, cheap and convenient against the oiled Japanese coiffure. Styles included 西洋上げ巻, イギリス巻き, マーガレット, 夜会巻き and, later, 二百三高地. About the 1890s Kimuraya in Ginza sold a "束髪パン" bun shaped like the knot, with raisins inside. The 庇髪 (pompadour) was started by the actress Kawakami Sadayakko around the Meiji 30s and was so common among schoolgirls it became a nickname for them. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/束髪
- **The Empress in Western dress.** Itō Hirobumi pressed for it from 1884, and the Emperor at first refused ("ナラヌ"). Permission came on 23 Jun 1886. Empress Haruko first wore Western dress in public on **30 Jul 1886** for a visit to the Peeresses' School. From 1886 she wore Western clothes for everything except sleep, and on **17 Jan 1887** she issued the 思召書 encouraging Western dress for women. Dr Bälz objected (corsets), and Itō replied that in Japanese dress Japanese women were looked at "like toys or dolls". — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/昭憲皇太后. Rokumeikan ladies wore bustle dresses. — Suzuki ring paper
- **Girls' hakama.** The 行灯袴 (skirt-type hakama) worn by schoolgirls from mid-Meiji is said to have been designed by **下田歌子 Shimoda Utako** of the Peeresses' School (華族女学校, founded 1885; Tsuda Umeko taught there 1885–1900). Photographic evidence cited by 福永操: in her mother's 1895 girls'-school photo **none of 126 pupils and staff wear hakama**; in the 1900 Tokyo Women's Higher Normal School graduation photo **all 31 graduates do**; from 1901 (Urawa Girls' High School) on, all do. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/袴; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/華族女学校
- **Gymnastics dress.** Early girls' PE was done in men's hakama or kimono. In 1885 the Women's Higher Normal School ordered hakama and shoes for gymnastics. **Bloomers were introduced there in 1903 by 井口阿くり**, back from America, and spread to girls' schools in late Taishō–early Shōwa. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ブルマー
- **Men's school uniforms.** The earliest stand-collar uniforms date from about 1873 at the Kōbushō Kōgakuryō (navy Norfolk-style jacket, government-issued, with a Glengarry cap) and Sapporo Agricultural College. The Gakushūin set a dress code in 1879. The **Imperial University's 1886 uniform** is the origin of the standard 学生服, adopted the same year at the Higher Normal School and then by middle schools. Colours were black or navy in winter and white or grey in summer. Uniforms were costly: most students wore kimono, geta and a student cap with a furoshiki. Ishibashi Tanzan recalled that few Imperial University students owned both summer and winter uniforms, so at the July graduation, which the Emperor attended, they sweated in winter black and joked "忠義とはあついものだ" (loyalty is a *hot/thick/fervent* thing). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/学生服
- **"Haikara".** From 1898–99, 石川半山 (安次郎) of the *Tokyo Mainichi* mocked Westernized politicians with high collars as the "ハイカラア派". On **10 Aug 1900**, at a farewell party for 竹越与三郎 at the Hotel Métropole, Tsukiji, 小松緑 joked that Ishikawa himself was wearing a "very high collar" tonight. The papers reported it and the word took off, at first meaning affected, later stylish or up-to-date. Even schoolchildren called a boy haikara for owning an overcoat. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ハイカラ
- **Ribbons.** Okada Saburōsuke's 1908 『ダイヤモンドの女』 shows a beauty in a 二百三高地 hairstyle with a ribbon ornament. Girls' prints of the period show schoolgirls in the "マーガレット" style and a fashionable rose hair ornament. — Suzuki ring paper
- **Clothing prices.** Not found. Wage proxy: a Western tailor (洋服仕立男工) earned 29 sen a day in 1885 and 59 sen in 1900. — https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2019698

## 9. Photography and printed images

- **Studios.** Japan's first studio was Orin Freeman's in Yokohama, 1860. 鵜飼玉川 opened the first Japanese-run studio at Yagenbori in 1861. Ueno Hikoma (Nagasaki) and Shimooka Renjō (Yokohama) opened in 1862. 内田九一 photographed Emperor Meiji in 1872. Kitaniwa Tsukuba opened at the Asakusa Hanayashiki in 1871 and in 1874 founded Japan's first photo magazine, 『脱影夜話』. In 1871 浅沼藤吉 founded the first photographic supply shop (Nihonbashi), and in 1875 made the first Japanese photograph mounts. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本写真史
- **"Yokohama photographs".** Hand-coloured albums with lacquer and mother-of-pearl covers (Beato, Kusakabe Kinbei) were a major export, and some included nudes. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本写真史
- **Copyright and technique.** The 1876 Photograph Ordinance (太政官布告90号) gave 5 years' exclusive rights. In 1883 the Asakusa photographer **江崎礼二** used imported Swan gelatin dry plates to photograph a naval mine explosion on the Sumida, freeing photography from the darkroom tent. The 1887 写真版権条例 gave 10 years. In 1889 the 日本寫眞會 was founded (Esaki, Ogawa Kazumasa, Burton, Bigelow, Kashima Seibei). Film was first used in Japan in 1894 by the Army survey photographer 小倉倹司. A women's photography school (女子写真伝習所) opened in 1902. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本写真史; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/鹿島清兵衛
- **小川一眞 Ogawa Kazumasa** (1860–1929) trained in Boston 1882–84, opened the studio 玉潤館 at Iidamachi (Wikipedia gives 1884 and 1885), and photographed the 1887 total solar eclipse corona for the Home Ministry. He began Japan's **first collotype** printing (1888 per one passage, 1889 per others) and printed 『国華』 (from 1889). His 小川写真製版所 at 京橋区日吉町 opened in 1889. He brought back **halftone (網目凸版)** presses from America in 1893–94 and began halftone plate-making in 1894. He was made an Imperial Household Artist in 1910. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/小川一真; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/コロタイプ
- A collotype-and-lithograph postcard boom came during the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/コロタイプ
- **The 1891 "Tokyo Hyaku Bijin" (東京百美人).** After the elevator was ordered stopped, Ryōunkaku hung photographs of geisha chosen from the pleasure quarters along its staircase walls to keep visitors coming. Its formal name is unknown; papers called it "東京芸妓美人えり抜き百名", "百美人品評会", "選美投票" and so on. **Ogawa Kazumasa closed his studio and shot all candidates in a special studio against the same background**, partly for fairness and partly because few women had photos of their own. Visitors voted, and **the top 5 of 100 were honoured**. 「洗い髪お妻」 (枡田屋小つま, real name 安達ツギ, aged 17) missed the top five but became a postcard and advertising model. It was not Japan's first beauty contest: an April 1890 report already mentions judging submitted photos for prize money (*Yomiuri* 19 Apr 1890). (Sources include 井上章一『美人コンテスト百年史』1992 and 木村絵里子 2019.) — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/凌雲閣; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/小川一真
- **Kashima Seibei (鹿嶋清兵衛, 1866–1924)**, heir to a rich sake-wholesaling house and called the "写真大尽" (photography millionaire). He studied photography with Esaki from 1885 and redeemed the Shinbashi geisha **ぽん太** (the model for Meidi-ya's beer poster). His 玄鹿館 studio (1895) had an elevator, a revolving stage and arc lamps. In 1894 he gave the Emperor a 6-by-9-shaku photograph of Mt Fuji for the imperial silver wedding. He was ruined by about 1897. He was the model for Mori Ōgai's 『百物語』. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/鹿島清兵衛
- Not found: carte-de-visite (名刺判) prices and sizes, and specific trade in photographs of schoolgirls. The lantern catalogue's "内外婦人肖像" slides (about 1892) are one documented mass image of women (Aoyama 2008, above).

## Telling details

- **The 1880s illustrated serial novel was a real format.** Tsubouchi Shōyō's 『当世書生気質』 came out as 17 illustrated fascicles in movable type, Japanese-bound, from June 1885 to January 1886. Illustrators were 歌川国峰, 葛飾正久, 長原止水 and 武内桂舟. The doctor Noguchi Seisaku feared readers would take the dissolute medical student "野々口精作" for him, and changed his name to Hideyo. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/当世書生気質
- **Tokyo's carriages were for show.** In the 1907 count, two-horse passenger carriages (130) outnumbered one-horse ones (106) in Tokyo, while nationally one-horse carriages outnumbered two-horse ones about 13 to 1. — 1907 yearbook table
- **Assassins stopped carriages by cutting the horses' legs**, as with Ōkubo in 1878. His coachman died fighting bare-handed. Ōkubo's carriage, kept blood-stained, was British-made. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/紀尾井坂の変
- **Ōkuma's assassin approached the carriage in a morning coat with a bat-wing umbrella**, at 4:10 p.m. at the Foreign Ministry gate, 18 Oct 1889. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/大隈重信遭難事件
- **The first Japanese keep-left rule was for rickshaws.** Under the 1889 rickshaw regulations, oncoming vehicles both swerved left. The general rule came only on 21 June 1900. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/左側通行
- **The Crown Prince thought cars slow.** Shown his wedding-present electric car (about 1901), he reportedly remarked "自動車とはことの外速度のおそきものである". It never left storage. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/皇太子献納車
- **Lamplighters.** Ginza's 85 gas lamps of 1874 were lit by 点消方 in identifying hanten coats carrying fire-sticks, each responsible for 50 to about 100 lamps. Shopkeepers were billed ¥3.555 a month for a lamp they had not asked for. — Gas Museum PDF
- **Crowds stayed out all night** to see the 2,000-candlepower arc lamp in front of the Ōkura-gumi in Ginza in 1882. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/アーク灯
- **The Twelve Storeys glowed** with three electric lamps per floor through 176 windows. Its lift had two 3-tatami cars with cushioned benches on hemp ropes, stopping only at the 1st and 8th floors. After the police shut the lift, the owners lined the staircase with 100 geisha photographs and let climbers vote. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/凌雲閣; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/エレベーター
- **A photographer designed it and a photographer ran it**: W. K. Burton and Esaki Reiji, both founder-members of the 日本寫眞會 (1889). Ogawa Kazumasa shut his own studio to shoot the contestants against one backdrop. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/凌雲閣; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/鹿島清兵衛
- **Asakusa had a fake Mount Fuji** of plaster, cloth and bamboo, 32 m high, climbed by a spiral path (10 turns up inside, 10 down outside), with telescopes on its white summit. It was electrically lit for summer 1888, and a stuffed Indian python was put on the peak to revive business. It was mocked as an upturned turban shell and knocked down in 1890. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/富士山縦覧場
- **A photo studio with a revolving stage and an elevator**: Kashima Seibei's 玄鹿館, Kobikichō, 1895, with a 2,500-candlepower arc lamp for night portraits. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/鹿島清兵衛
- **The first imported educational lantern set began with 17 astronomy slides** (Teshima, 1874). Catalogues still offered a 17-slide astronomy set "運轉付" (with handle-cranked rotating slides) in 1895. — Iwata paper
- **Tsurubuchi's lantern catalogue (about 1892) sold slide portraits of "内外婦人"** (Japanese and foreign women) and "日本貴顕" (Japanese dignitaries) alongside astronomy and anatomy. — Aoyama 2008
- **'La France' under wire**: crowds came to smell the government's 'La France' rose at the Aoyama farm until it was fenced in. (Unsourced on Wikipedia.) — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/バラ
- **Orchids named "Shinjuku"**: by 1914 the imperial glasshouses held over 800 orchids, including Cattleya, Laelia and Coelogyne Shinjuku. The glasshouse also produced Japan's first strawberry variety, 'Fukuba' (about 1898–99). — Shinjuku Gyoen official pages
- **Twelve million Japanese lily bulbs** left the country in 1908, over 90% through Yokohama, mostly for Britain and the US, for Easter. — https://www.jacar.go.jp/modernjapan/p02.html
- **Ring snobbery, 1905**: "金の延べは田舎臭く…如何にしても金剛石、金剛石、金剛石！" (Uchida Roan). — Suzuki paper
- **Diamond rings as prizes**: the 1907 *Jiji Shimpō* beauty contest prize was a ¥300 Tenshōdō ring, the same sum as Miya's diamond in 『金色夜叉』 ten years earlier. — Suzuki paper
- **Women's cropped hair was briefly fashionable**, after women misread the 1871 order, until Tokyo banned it on 11 May 1872. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/断髪令
- **Schoolgirls' hakama spread fast**: none of 126 in a 1895 school photograph, all 31 Women's Higher Normal graduates in 1900. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/袴
- **Kimuraya's 束髪パン** (1890s), a raisin bun shaped like the new Western-style women's knot. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/束髪
- **Itō on Western dress for women**: in Japanese dress, he said, foreigners saw Japanese women "like toys or ornamental dolls". — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/昭憲皇太后
- **Early telephone operators were mostly women** (9 of 11 in Tokyo in 1890; 330 women and 100 men in 1900). — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/電話交換手
- **The first desk telephones (1897) were sold partly as ornaments** and cost ¥6 a year extra. — NTT West PDF
- **Rokumeikan balls drafted geisha** trained in Western dance to fill the floor. — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/鹿鳴館

## Uncertain / conflicting

- **Rickshaws in Tokyo, early 1870s.** Wikipedia: 40,000 in Tokyo Prefecture by Meiji 4/12 (≈ January 1872). Trafficnews: "nearly 10,000 at the end of 1871", with 40,000 only at the peak around 1900. The 1876 count (25,038) and the LTES national total for 1875 (102,529) make 40,000 in Tokyo by 1872 look high. Wikipedia also says invention 1869, operation from Meiji 3/3/23 (1870), and contradicts itself in one sentence by calling both 1869.
- **First Yokohama–Tokyo omnibus**: 1869 (Sekai Daihyakka; Bus Magazine, Meiji 2/5) against operation only from September 1870 (Nipponica). The promoters are named differently (川名幸左衛門・下岡蓮杖 vs 成駒屋).
- **Horse-tram ridership.** Trafficnews says "年間3300万人" (33 million a year), but the company table on Wikipedia gives 19.3 million for 1903. The year of the 33 million figure is not given.
- **Tokyo carriage rules "1872年4月11日"**: unclear whether Gregorian or lunar.
- **Search snippets I could not open**: "1882 Tokyo passenger carriages 1,920" and "taxed ¥25 a year for a two-horse carriage". The search results did not let me open the page behind them. Excluded from the body.
- **Private carriages: owners, types, prices, coachmen's wages.** No source opened gives Meiji Tokyo counts by owner, body types (landau, brougham, etc.), carriage prices, or whether gentlemen drove themselves. Only the 1884 state-carriage file, the Ōkubo and Ōkuma cases, and the yearbook's one-horse/two-horse split were found.
- **Cars in 1907.** The widely repeated small figures (for example "16 cars") are **unverified**. The yearbook has no car column before FY1912 (535 nationally). Wikipedia says Tokyo issued number plates from 1907 (no. 1 to Meidi-ya) but gives no total.
- **Crown Prince's car cost.** Sources say "約3万円" (about ¥30,000), but the Wikipedia article itself gives $3,100 and says about $1 = ¥2, which makes about ¥6,200. The ¥30,000 may be the yen value of the whole $5,311 fund, or simply wrong.
- **Yamaba steam bus specs** (10 seats, 2-cylinder, 25 hp; the route): known only from search snippets of webCG and Okayama City pages I did not open. The 7 May 1904 test date comes from the Wikipedia timeline.
- **Takuri numbers** ("about 10") are from secondary blurbs, not primary records.
- **Ryōunkaku lift**: 7 hp (Ryōunkaku article) vs 7.5 hp (Elevator article). Stopped "May 1891" vs "about half a year later" (consistent). Why it stopped: frequent breakdowns, and a police technician called it "危険なり". The two articles agree in substance. I found **no earlier passenger lift in Japan**, hydraulic or hand-cranked, at exhibitions or elsewhere. That does not prove there was none.
- **Gas lamps.** Ginza's first gas lamps: 1873 (Wikipedia) vs 18 Dec 1874 (Gas Museum). I prefer the Gas Museum. The decline also conflicts: Wikipedia says gas lamps were gone from Tokyo city by 1913, while the Gas Museum gives a peak of 5,792 street lamps in 1917.
- **Tokyo Dentō's first public supply.** Wikipedia says "直流200V". Other secondary sources often say 210 V. Not checked.
- **Telegraph day** Meiji 2/12/25 (= 26 Jan 1870) is the conventional date. The page I opened says only "1870年1月（明治2年12月）". Construction start, Meiji 2/9/19, converts to 23 Oct 1869, Japan's Telegraph and Telephone Day, but that comes from my conversion and memory, not a page I opened.
- **Telephone import, 1877**: NTT West gives 1877 in one passage and "1878年（明治11年）輸入されたベルの電話機" in another. The Tokyo Fire Department says November 1877. Yokohama subscribers in 1890: 42 (Tokyo Fire Department) vs 44 (some anniversary websites).
- **Tokyo Observatory**: founded 1888, exact month not verified. Wikipedia's Gautier meridian circle caption reads "1903年（明治33年）", but 1903 is Meiji 36.
- **Tōnichi Tenmonkan opening**: 30 Oct 1938 (Wikipedia). Popular accounts sometimes give 3 Nov 1938. Not checked.
- **The 'La France' at Aoyama story** is unsourced on Wikipedia, and I could not date it. The "Aoyama government farm" and the Kaitakushi's Aoyama garden may be the same place.
- **First glass greenhouse**: Kaitakushi Aoyama garden, 1870 (Wikipedia 温室), against Shinjuku's ~100 m² glasshouse of 1875 (official) and Shinjuku's heated greenhouse of 1892 (official; the task's "1892/1893" — 1892 is correct per the official site). The Aoyama Kaitakushi garden is usually dated 1871–72, so "1870" is suspect.
- **Fukuba**: joined as trainee 1875 (Wikipedia) or 1877 (official). Studied in Italy, France and the US (Wikipedia) or France and Germany (official). Strawberry 1898 (official) or 1899 (Wikipedia).
- **Lily-bulb price** "テッポウユリ 1本あたり2円50銭～5円" (JACAR). This seems very high per bulb: the 1908 average export value works out to about 3.75 sen a bulb. It may be per 100 bulbs, per case, or for a special grade. The ja Wikipedia claim that lily bulbs were Japan's second export after silk is doubtful against Meiji trade structure, where tea, coal and cotton goods were large. Not verified.
- **Rosa rugosa**: introduced to England in 1796 (en Wikipedia Rosa rugosa) vs introduced to the West "circa the 1880s" (en Wikipedia Garden roses).
- **Collotype**: Ogawa's start is given as 1888 or 1889, and Gyokujunkan as 1884 or 1885, within the same Wikipedia articles.
- **Ring prices**: "純金製 35銭" in the 1912 advertisement is almost certainly a misreading of 鍍金 (gilt) or a typo in the paper. The Suzuki paper also miscites 『明治事物起原』 as "明治11年刊" (it is 1908).
- **LTES series**: LTES-R and LTES-W are long-run constructed series, not one city's records. LTES-R is probably Tokyo prices (not confirmed). The manservant's and maid's wages for 1880 (¥3.28 and ¥2.22) against 1885 (¥1.38 and ¥0.75) reflect the post-1881 Matsukata deflation, but the size of the fall is striking. Use the 1885 values with care.
- **The newspaper in LTES-R** ("新聞, 1ヵ月, 月ぎめ") is not named. The fall from ¥0.85 to ¥0.30 in 1887–88 probably reflects a change in the paper sampled.
- **Not found at all**: rickshaw fares; carriage prices; Meiji rose societies, rose shows or a rose craze; the 1900 telephone subscriber total; popular astronomy books; carte-de-visite prices; prices of Western suits, boots or ribbons; women's riding habits; girls'-school tuition; the substitute-teacher pay of Ishikawa Takuboku (1906, often quoted as ¥8, not verified); and the origin of the word "gakuran".

- **Also re-checked and CONFIRMED** (not among the 25):
