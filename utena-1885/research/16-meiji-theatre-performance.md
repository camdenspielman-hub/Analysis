# 16 — Theatre, performance, popular spectacle and early cinema in Meiji and Taishō Japan (1868–1926)

Real history only. I opened every page cited here and read its text (Japanese Wikipedia through its raw wikitext, Kotobank entries, Aozora Bunko texts, and other pages in full). "ja.wp" means Japanese Wikipedia. Kotobank entries collect several encyclopedias; where it matters I name the one quoted: Nipponica (日本大百科全書), Heibonsha (改訂新版 世界大百科事典), Britannica (ブリタニカ国際大百科事典 小項目事典), Kokugo (精選版 日本国語大辞典), Shinsen (新撰 芸能人物事典 明治～平成, Nichigai).

**A caution about two kinds of source.** Wikipedia and Kotobank give the factual skeleton. The most vivid material comes from two literary memoirists, available on Aozora Bunko:
- Okamoto Kidō (岡本綺堂), *Meiji gekidan: Ranpu no shita nite* (明治劇談 ランプの下にて). This is an eyewitness memoir: Kidō was a newspaper drama critic from February 1890.
- Hasegawa Shigure (長谷川時雨), essays from *Kindai bijin den* (近代美人伝). These are partly novelised portraits, with invented dialogue.

Facts from these two are flagged as memoir.

**Calendar.** Japan used the lunisolar calendar until Meiji 5/12/2. Meiji 5/12/3 became 1 January 1873. The conversions below come from sources that give both dates, or follow from such anchors:
- Meiji 4/8/9 = 23 Sep 1871 (散髪脱刀令, the decree allowing men to cut off the topknot): https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/散髪脱刀令
- Meiji 5/10/4 = 4 Nov 1872 (opening of the Tomioka silk mill): https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/富岡製糸場. So the 10th month of Meiji 5 began on 1 Nov 1872, and Meiji 5/10/13 = 13 Nov 1872 (my derivation).
- Meiji 5/11/25 = 25 Dec 1872 (birth of Soeda Azenbō): https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/添田唖蝉坊. So Meiji 5/11/8 = 8 Dec 1872 (my derivation).
- The lunar 1st month of Meiji 5 began on 9 Feb 1872 (standard tables). I give this from memory and did not check it against a source.

## 1. Kabuki in Meiji

### 1a. Morita Kan'ya XII and the Shintomi-za (新富座)

- **Morita Kan'ya XII (十二代目 守田勘彌)**
  - Born Kōka 3/9/21 (= 9 Nov 1846); died 21 Aug 1897, of stomach cancer. Real name 守田壽作. Second son of Nakamura Gansaemon, the Morita-za's accountant. He was adopted into the Morita family in 1863, at age 18 (counted in the traditional way), and became Kan'ya XII and proprietor (座元) of the Morita-za in 1864. Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/守田勘彌_(12代目)
  - He also wrote plays under the name 古河新水. He staged reportage plays about the Satsuma Rebellion, translated Western plays, and "improved" (kairyō) drama. Same page.
- **Move to Shintomi-chō, 1872**
  - Kan'ya moved the Morita-za from the Saruwaka-chō theatre quarter (猿若町) in Asakusa, where the theatres had been confined since the Tenpō era, to Shintomi-chō in Kyōbashi. It was the first of the three licensed Edo theatres to leave. Sources: Britannica and Nipponica in https://kotobank.jp/word/新富座-82366
  - Nipponica gives the opening of the new building as 1872 (明治5) 10月13日. That is a lunar date: 13 Nov 1872 by my conversion. https://kotobank.jp/word/新富座-82366
  - The ja.wp article on Kan'ya says only "September 1872" (1872年9月). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/守田勘彌_(12代目)
  - The 1872 building made part of the auditorium chair seating. Source: Heibonsha, under 興行, quoted in the Kotobank 新富座 entry.
- **Renaming and incorporation, 1875.** The theatre was renamed Shintomi-za in 1875 and became a joint-stock company. Nipponica gives March 1875; the Kan'ya article gives January 1875. https://kotobank.jp/word/新富座-82366 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/守田勘彌_(12代目)
- **Fire and rebuilding.** The theatre burned in a neighbourhood fire in November 1876. A temporary theatre opened in April 1877. The rebuilt theatre opened on **7 June 1878** with gas lighting (ガス灯). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/新富座
- **The 1878 Western-style opening ceremony**
  - Guests included Prime Minister (太政大臣) Sanjō Sanetomi (三条実美) and the foreign ministers. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/新富座
  - Kan'ya and the actors, Danjūrō IX included, wore tailcoats (燕尾服) while a military band played. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/演劇改良運動
  - Heibonsha adds that the staff (座方) wore frock coats and that the ceremony was much talked about. https://kotobank.jp/word/新富座-82366
- **Firsts and failures at the Shintomi-za**
  - First evening performances in Japan, on the Western model (日本で初めて夜芝居興行). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/新富座
  - March 1879: 『人間万事金世中』, an adaptation of Bulwer-Lytton's *Money*.
  - September 1879: a joint show with a Western troupe, 『漂流奇談西洋劇』. It flopped, and Kan'ya's debts began to mount. Same page; Heibonsha in https://kotobank.jp/word/新富座-82366
  - 1879: the first Western-style drop curtain (緞帳) in a major theatre. Source: Heibonsha, under 幕, quoted in https://kotobank.jp/word/引幕-609212
  - Heinrich von Siebold and Erwin Bälz presented the Shintomi-za with a stage curtain (引幕) as a gift. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川團十郎_(9代目)
- **Decline and Shōchiku**
  - From about 1882 the theatre ran at a loss. It operated under other names: 猿若座, 桐座, 深野座 (Nipponica); the Yamakawa dictionary gives the years 1882, 1889 and 1891.
  - In 1888 Kan'ya formed the "Four-Theatre Alliance" (四座同盟), dated 26 Sep 1888, with the Ichimura-za, Nakamura-za and Chitose-za against the new Kabuki-za. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/新富座
  - The last show under Kan'ya was in March 1892. He lost title to the theatre in a lawsuit in December 1895.
  - Shōchiku bought the theatre in 1909 or 1910 (sources differ). It burned in the 1 Sep 1923 earthquake fire and was not rebuilt as a playhouse. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/新富座 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/新富座-82366
- **Kan'ya's later career.** He was the de facto producer of the 1887 imperial viewing and stood beside the throne in morning dress. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/天覧歌舞伎

### 1b. "Dan–Kiku–Sa" (團菊左)

- The phrase names the age of Danjūrō IX, Kikugorō V and Sadanji I, centred on the Kabuki-za after 1889. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/歌舞伎 (Meiji section)
- **Ichikawa Danjūrō IX (九代目 市川團十郎)**
  - Born Tenpō 9/10/13 (= 29 Nov 1838); died 13 Sep 1903 at his villa in Chigasaki (uremia and pneumonia). Real name 堀越秀. Fifth son of Danjūrō VII by a concubine. He was adopted at birth into the Kawarazaki-za family. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川團十郎_(9代目)
  - His adoptive father was stabbed to death by a burglar in autumn 1868 while the boy hid in a closet. He became Danjūrō IX in July 1874.
  - As a young actor he was mocked as a "daikon" (ham) and as "お茶壺権ちゃん". He was later called 劇聖, "the saint of the theatre".
  - He promoted "living history" plays (katsureki, 活歴) and set the forms of the Ichikawa house repertory (新歌舞伎十八番).
  - He had two daughters and no son. He let both daughters choose their own lovers. The elder became Ichikawa Suisen II (二代目 市川翠扇, 1888–1944), an actress and head of the Ichikawa school of dance. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川翠扇
- **Onoe Kikugorō V (五代目 尾上菊五郎)**
  - Born Tenpō 15/6/4 (= 18 Jul 1844); died 18 Feb 1903. Real name 寺島清. He became Kikugorō V in August 1868.
  - He created Benten Kozō in Mokuami's 『青砥稿花紅彩画』 (1862): a male thief disguised as a young woman who reveals that he is a man.
  - He championed the zangiri-mono and chose the house repertory 新古演劇十種.
  - In January 1891 he played the balloonist Spencer in 『風船乗評判高閣』 at the Kabuki-za and went to Keiō to learn English for the part.
  - All three of his children were born to his geisha mistress, Akita Gin. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/尾上菊五郎_(5代目)
- **Ichikawa Sadanji I (初代 市川左團次)**
  - Born Tenpō 13/10/28 (= 30 Nov 1842) in Dōtonbori, Osaka; died 7 Aug 1904. Real name 高橋榮三. Adopted by Ichikawa Kodanji IV in 1864.
  - Edo audiences heckled his Kamigata accent. Mokuami retrained him, and he broke through as Marubashi Chūya in March 1870 (Meiji 3/3, lunar).
  - He built the Meiji-za in 1893 and ran it as proprietor.
  - In 1899 he staged Matsui Shōyō's 『悪源太』, the first script by an outsider to the theatre world, which opened the way to "new kabuki". https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川左團次_(初代)

### 1c. Kawatake Mokuami (河竹黙阿弥) and the zangiri-mono (散切物)

- **Life**
  - Born Bunka 13/2/3 (= 1 Mar 1816); died 22 Jan 1893 at home in Honjo (stroke), his last play being at the Kabuki-za that month. Real name 吉村芳三郎. Until 1881 he worked as Kawatake Shinshichi II. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/河竹黙阿弥
  - He wrote more than 300 plays. Tsubouchi Shōyō called him "the Chikamatsu of Meiji" and "our Shakespeare".
  - In 1881 he announced his retirement with 『島鵆月白浪』 and took the name 黙阿弥. In practice he went on writing as an "assistant" (スケ).
- **What zangiri-mono were.** The name comes from the cropped hair (散切頭) of men after the 1871 haircut decree (Meiji 4/8/9 = 23 Sep 1871). The plays put rickshaws, Western clothes, blankets, trains, newspapers and diamonds into the old domestic-drama form (sewamono). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/散切物 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/散切物-70573
- **First examples, Kyoto 1872.** Two adaptations of Nakamura Masanao's translation of Smiles' *Self-Help*: 『鞋補童教学』 and 『其粉色陶器交易』, adapted by 佐橋富三郎.
  - Heibonsha dates them "72年11月". If that is the lunar month, it means roughly Dec 1872; this is not stated.
  - Britannica says only "Meiji 5". https://kotobank.jp/word/散切物-70573
- **First in Tokyo.** Mokuami's 『東京日新聞』 (known as 鳥越甚内), at the Morita-za in November 1873. Britannica notes that it drew on the news columns of newspapers. https://kotobank.jp/word/散切物-70573
- **Main titles, most premiered by Kikugorō V**
  - 『富士額男女繁山』 ("The Woman Student", 女書生), 1877 — see 1g.
  - 『人間万事金世中』 and 『綴合於伝仮名書』 (Takahashi Oden), both 1879.
  - 『霜夜鐘十字辻筮』, 1880.
  - 『島鵆月白浪』, 1881.
  - 『水天宮利生深川』 (筆屋幸兵衛, about a ruined samurai), 1885.
  - 『月梅薫朧夜』 (Hanai Oume), 1888.
  - Britannica counts "twenty-odd" zangiri plays by Mokuami.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/散切物 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/散切物-70573
- **Verdict of the reference works.** Nipponica and Heibonsha say these plays copied new fashions while keeping old dramaturgy, and that from about 1900 they lost out to shinpa. https://kotobank.jp/word/散切物-70573

### 1d. The theatre-reform movement and the Engeki kairyōkai (演劇改良会, 1886)

- **1872.** Kabuki people were summoned to the Tokyo prefectural office. They were told to stage moral plots fit for nobles and foreigners and to give up "wild fictions" (狂言綺語). The month is not given in my sources. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/演劇改良運動 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/歌舞伎
- **28 Apr 1878.** Itō Hirobumi and Yoda Gakkai (依田学海) urged Kan'ya, Danjūrō and Kikugorō to reform, at the home of Matsuda Michiyuki. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/演劇改良運動
- **Katsureki ("living history")**
  - 1883: Danjūrō formed a research circle, the 求古会.
  - From April 1884 he staged historically accurate plays, starting with 『二代源氏誉身換』 at the Shintomi-za.
  - The word katsureki (活歴) comes from the writer and critic Kanagaki Robun. He used it mockingly: "this is not theatre". Same page.
- **The Engeki kairyōkai, August 1886**
  - Founded on the initiative of Suematsu Kenchō (末松謙澄). Members included Shibusawa Eiichi and Toyama Masakazu. Its prospectus appeared in the 6 Aug 1886 issue of 『歌舞伎新報』.
  - Proposals: abolish onnagata and use actresses, abolish the hanamichi, reform the theatre buildings, review the role of the theatre tea-houses.
  - Three stated aims: improve old abuses, make playwriting an honourable profession, build a proper theatre.
  - January 1887 plan: a 2,000-seat theatre costing ¥200,000 on about 600 tsubo of land.
  - The Wikipedia article lists Iwasaki Yatarō among the business backers. He died in 1885, so that is an error, probably for Iwasaki Yanosuke.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/演劇改良運動 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/演劇改良会-821446
- **End.** The society faded after Foreign Minister Inoue Kaoru fell in September 1887 and dissolved in 1888. Its successors were the 日本演芸矯風会 (1888) and the 日本演芸協会 (1889), which Tsubouchi Shōyō and Mori Ōgai joined. Same sources.

### 1e. The emperor's first viewing of kabuki (天覧歌舞伎), 26–29 April 1887

All points in this subsection: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/天覧歌舞伎 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/天覧劇-102829

- **Place and pretext.** Foreign Minister Inoue Kaoru's residence at Toriizaka, Azabu. A tea-house, the 八窓庵, had been moved there from Tōdai-ji, and its unveiling served as the pretext for the visit.
- **Earlier precedents.** Nō had already been performed before the emperor (1876), and so had sumō (1886).
- **Programme, 26 April, for the emperor**
  - Started at 2:10 p.m.
  - 『勧進帳』: Danjūrō as Benkei, Sadanji as Togashi, Nakamura Fukusuke IV (later Utaemon V) as Yoshitsune.
  - Also 『高時天狗舞』, 『操三番叟』, 『漁師の月見』 and 『元禄踊』; encores 『山姥』 and 『夜討曽我狩場曙』.
  - The emperor left at 10:10 p.m.
- **The other days**
  - 27 April: the empress.
  - 28 April: senior officials and foreign envoys.
  - 29 April: the empress dowager.
- **The staging**
  - The temporary stage was 7 ken (about 12.7 m) wide, with a short, angled hanamichi.
  - Suematsu removed the gidayū chanters, the black-clad stage assistants (kurogo) and the drums, and edited the texts down to the particles. Fukuchi Ōchi attacked these cuts in the 東京日日新聞.
- **What was said and seen**
  - The emperor told the actors it was "easier to follow than nō".
  - During Kikugorō's puppet-style 三番叟, the emperor kept looking up at the ceiling for strings.
  - Sadanji shook visibly on stage.
  - Danjūrō lost 1 kan 500 monme (about 5.6 kg) over the four days.
  - He said afterwards: "Actors who could not go out without a woven hat (編笠) have played before the sovereign."
  - Heibonsha notes that the event fitted the diplomacy aimed at revising the unequal treaties.

### 1f. The Kabuki-za, 1889

- **Opening.** Opening ceremony **21 Nov 1889**, first performances the next day. Location: Kobiki-chō 3-chōme, Kyōbashi. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/歌舞伎座 ; Heibonsha in https://kotobank.jp/word/歌舞伎座-46220
- **Founders.** Fukuchi Ōchi (福地桜痴), a member of the reform society, and the moneylender Chiba Katsugorō (千葉勝五郎). https://kotobank.jp/word/歌舞伎座-46220
- **The building**
  - Western exterior; Japanese-style three-storey cypress interior.
  - 1,824 seats.
  - Stage opening 13 ken (23.6 m).
  - A 9-ken (16.29 m) revolving stage with a 7-ken inner ring (蛇の目回し).
  - Main hanamichi 5 shaku (1.52 m) wide.
  - Sources: Nipponica and Britannica, same page.
- **Ownership.** Fukuchi failed as manager within a year and stayed on as house playwright. Kan'ya and then Tamura Nariyoshi ran it. It became a joint-stock company in March 1896. Shōchiku took over the management in 1913 or 1914. https://kotobank.jp/word/歌舞伎座-46220
- **Kawakami at the Kabuki-za.** In 1895 Kawakami's shinpa troupe played the Kabuki-za. Legend says Danjūrō then had the stage boards planed down because they had been "soiled". https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川團十郎_(9代目) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/川上音二郎

### 1g. Newspapers into kabuki

- **Takahashi Oden (高橋お伝)**
  - Executed 31 Jan 1879.
  - Reported first in the 東京日日新聞 (12–13 Sep 1876), then in the small newspapers.
  - Serialised as "毒婦高橋お伝" in the 東京曙新聞, 1–7 Feb 1879.
  - Turned into Kanagaki Robun's book 『高橋阿伝夜刃譚』 (1879) and Mokuami's play 『綴合於伝仮名書』 (Shintomi-za, May 1879, with Kikugorō V).
  - In April 1881 Kan'ya, Kikugorō, Sadanji and the storyteller San'yūtei Enchō helped put up a grave for her at Yanaka.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高橋お伝
- **Torioi Omatsu (鳥追お松)**
  - Kubota Hikosaku's "true-crime" serial 『鳥追お松の伝』 ran in the 仮名読新聞 in 1877–78.
  - It was staged by Kawatake Shinshichi III in April 1878, at the Haruki-za according to the Nichigai title dictionary.
  - Sources: https://kotobank.jp/word/鳥追お松-1095616 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/鳥追お松の伝-2097184
- **『富士額男女繁山』, the "Woman Student" (女書生), 1877**
  - Mokuami's four-act play, premiered at the Shintomi-za in **April 1877** (Nipponica; the ja.wp table of his works says March). Kikugorō V played 妻木繁 and Sadanji I played 御家直.
  - Source story: a newspaper report about a woman in men's clothing taken into custody by police at Kumagaya in Jōshū.
  - Plot: Shigeru is the daughter of a private-school owner at Ikaho and was raised as a boy. She goes to Tokyo dressed as a man and becomes a live-in student (書生) to a high official, Jinbo Masamichi. She steals from him to visit her sick father. At Kumagaya the rickshaw-man Nao discovers she is a woman and forces himself on her. She confesses to Jinbo and becomes his mistress, kills Nao (who had murdered her father) on the Sumida embankment, and turns herself in.
  - Nipponica notes its "perverse eroticism of the woman in male dress" (男装の女の倒錯的エロティシズム).
  - A Kyoto production titled 女書生繁 is recorded for January 1882.
  - Sources: https://kotobank.jp/word/富士額男女繁山-1585971 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/女書生繁-2108444 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/河竹黙阿弥
- **Shinpa and kabuki adaptations of newspaper novels.** See section 3. Kabuki also took them up; for example 『不如帰』 was staged as kabuki at the Tōkyō-za in September 1904. https://kotobank.jp/word/不如帰-133743

### 1h. Onnagata in Meiji

- **Sawamura Tanosuke III (三代目 澤村田之助)**
  - Born Kōka 2/2/8 (= 15 Mar 1845); died July 1878, at 34.
  - The most popular onnagata of the 1860s. Hairstyles, collars and clogs were sold under his name.
  - Gangrene led to amputation of his right leg above the knee by J. C. Hepburn on Keiō 3/9/15 (1867). He went on acting on an artificial leg and later lost fingers as well.
  - His farewell role: Meiji 5, 1st month (lunar; about February–March 1872) at the Murayama-za.
  - One theory blames lead in his white face make-up.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/澤村田之助_(3代目)
- **Iwai Hanshirō VIII (八代目 岩井半四郎)**
  - Bunsei 12 (1829) – 19 Feb 1882.
  - Took the name in Meiji 5, 2nd month (lunar; about March–April 1872).
  - Known for living like a woman offstage. He created Ojō Kichisa (お嬢吉三), a male thief who goes about dressed as a girl.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/岩井半四郎_(8代目)
- **Nakamura Fukusuke IV, later Utaemon V (五代目 中村歌右衛門)**
  - Keiō 1/12/29 (= 14 Feb 1866) – 1940.
  - Took the name Fukusuke IV in May 1881. Played Yoshitsune at the 1887 imperial viewing.
  - His Yodogimi in Tsubouchi's 『桐一葉』 (Tōkyō-za, March 1904) was a famous success.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/中村歌右衛門_(5代目)
- **Danjūrō IX in women's roles.** He took female roles too, for example Princess Sarashina, a demoness in disguise, in 『紅葉狩』; see 5c. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/紅葉狩_(映画)
- **The debate about abolishing onnagata.** The reform society proposed it (1886). When mixed casts were allowed in August 1890, Okamoto Kidō says most theatre-goers asked who could replace the living onnagata Iwai Matsunosuke, Bandō Shūchō, Sawamura Gennosuke and Nakamura Fukusuke, and concluded that onnagata were "uniquely Japanese". Memoir: https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000082/files/49526_42385.html

## 2. Women on stage

### 2a. All-women kabuki (onna-shibai, 女芝居) and Ichikawa Kumehachi (市川九女八)

- **Origins.** The *okyōgenshi* (お狂言師) were women who performed kabuki for the shogun's inner palace and the households of daimyō, where men could not enter. After 1868 they lost these patrons and formed all-women troupes in public theatres. Their base was the Misaki-za in Kanda. Named players: Kumehachi, 中村仲吉 (翠蛾), 中村仲次, 市川鯉昇, 松本錦糸, 中村歌扇. Source: Heibonsha in https://kotobank.jp/word/女芝居-456055
  - Britannica calls Nakamura Kasen the last *onna-yakusha*, active into early Shōwa. https://kotobank.jp/word/女役者-168179
- **Ichikawa Kumehachi I: life**
  - Born Kōka 3/11/28 (= 14 Jan 1847) in Kanda; died 24 Jul 1913 while appearing at the Mikuni-za in Asakusa. Real name 守住けい.
  - Learned dance from age 8 and held a dance name by age 13 (counted in the traditional way).
  - Joined Iwai Hanshirō VIII in 1873 as Iwai Kumehachi.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川九女八
- **Ichikawa Kumehachi I: Danjūrō IX**
  - Called "女団洲" or "女団十郎": a female Danjūrō, Danshū being Danjūrō's poetry name.
  - Became Danjūrō IX's pupil in 1888 (ja.wp), as 市川升之丞 and then 市川粂八.
  - Expelled in 1893 for performing 『勧進帳』 in Niigata without permission. Readmitted in 1897 through Fukuchi Ōchi, and took the name 九女八.
  - ja.wp calls her Danjūrō's only female pupil.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川九女八 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/市川九女八-31231
- **Ichikawa Kumehachi I: later career**
  - In 1893, when the Misaki-za went over to all-women kabuki, she became head of its company (座頭).
  - Also acted in shinpa and in writers' amateur theatre under the name 守住月華.
  - In 1906 she founded an actresses' school with Wakayagi Enjō. In 1908 she taught at the Kawakamis' actress school.
  - Okamoto Kidō says she finally stood on the Kabuki-za stage in autumn 1905, in the memorial for Danjūrō's third death anniversary.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川九女八 ; Kidō memoir https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000082/files/49526_42385.html
- **Kidō's eyewitness account, March 1890** (memoir)
  - Kumehachi was at the Kotobuki-za in Honjo. Kidō watched on the critics' day, 2 March, with doors at 9 a.m.
  - She played Princess Yaegaki and the mountain witch (山姥), both in Danjūrō's manner. Her voice was "thick and muddy" from years of male roles and imitating Danjūrō, but her stage face was beautiful.
  - Kidō judged her Yaegaki better than Danjūrō's own.
  - He called her "the last of Japan's onna-yakusha" and said she was born "forty years too early".
  - Source: https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000082/files/49526_42385.html
- **Danjūrō's reported comment** (Hasegawa Shigure's portrait, which includes invented dialogue)
  - Danjūrō is said to have told the scholar Takata Sanae (高田早苗) that "she is a fine actor; if she were a man she would be my partner" (男だったら、俺の相手だが).
  - In the same piece Kumehachi teaches her pupils and sends one girl, dressed in a maroon hakama, to Yoda Gakkai for lessons in classical Chinese.
  - Source: https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000726/files/45974_26599.html
- **The Misaki-za (三崎座)**
  - Opened 27 Jun 1891 on a former drill ground in Kanda Misaki-chō. It was the first theatre to open in the "small theatre" (小劇場) class created by the August 1890 regulations. About 1,500 seats.
  - For more than ten years at its height it played every month without a break, as "the only permanent theatre of women's kabuki".
  - Its audience included many young women, as well as students and literary men. Actresses brought babies to the dressing room in prams.
  - A 1899 guidebook (『新撰東京名所図会』) explains its success by the charm of a resident company made up entirely of women.
  - Renamed Kanda Gekijō in 1915. Burned in 1923.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/三崎座

### 2b. Legal status of mixed-sex casts

- **The ban lifted in August 1890: confirmed by an eyewitness memoir.** Okamoto Kidō, writing of 1890 (his chronology runs from the February 1890 Kabuki-za season, and he dates the craze for the word "haikara" to "nine years later, about Meiji 32"):
  - The Metropolitan Police (警視庁) newly ruled in August that men and women performing together in theatres was "henceforth no objection".
  - It surprised the newspaper offices but caused "no response" in practice. No theatre hired actresses outside the women's companies.
  - Source: https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000082/files/49526_42385.html
- **The same month's regulations.** In August 1890 the theatre regulations (劇場取締規則) were revised to divide theatres into "large" and "small" and to limit their number. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/三崎座
- **I did not find the text of the decree that ended the mixed-sex ban.** Kidō does not name it, and I could not confirm whether it was part of the 劇場取締規則 revision.
- **Earlier step, 1877.** A revision of the yose (variety hall) regulations (寄席取締規則) let women entertainers appear in yose. Nipponica in https://kotobank.jp/word/女義太夫-456030
- **Clothing law, 1872 (Tokyo).** The Tokyo minor-offences code (違式詿違条例) was issued on Meiji 5/11/8 (= 8 Dec 1872 by my conversion).
  - Its listed contents include banning wrestling between men and women and shows of "ugly" sights.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/見世物小屋 (citing 倉田喜弘『幕末明治見世物事典』); https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/警察犯処罰令
  - The Shōtō Museum's 2022 exhibition on cross-dressing (「装いの力―異性装の日本史」) says the code banned cross-dressing, with penalties, and lasted about 8 years. It dates the code to Meiji 6. https://www.tokyoartbeat.com/articles/-/the-power-of-clothing-history-of-cross-dressing-in-japan-report-2022-09
  - The exact wording, and any exemption for actors, is not verified here.

### 2c. The first "actresses": Chitose Beiha and the Saibikan (済美館), 1891

- **The troupe.** In November 1891 Ii Yōhō (伊井蓉峰) founded the Saibikan (also read Seibikan), billed as "men-and-women joint reformed drama" (男女合同改良演劇), at the Azuma-za in Asakusa. It was backed by Yoda Gakkai and opened with Gakkai's 『政党美談淑女之操』. https://kotobank.jp/word/済美館-1350297 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/伊井蓉峰-29795
- **The actress.** It cast Chitose Beiha (千歳米坡), formerly the Nihonbashi geisha 米八. Gakkai gave her the stage name. She is called "the first of the actresses" (女優登場の嚆矢). She later played in women's kabuki and the yose.
  - Born Ansei 2 (1855).
  - Died 2 Aug 1918 according to Shinsen; 1913 according to Nipponica's shinpa entry.
  - Sources: https://kotobank.jp/word/千歳米坡-1365680 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/新派劇-538751
- **Ii Yōhō (伊井蓉峰)**
  - Meiji 4/8/16 (= 30 Sep 1871) – 15 Aug 1932. Son of the photographer Kitaniwa Tsukuba, who had a studio at the Hanayashiki amusement garden in Asakusa. Worked at Mitsui Bank in Kōbe before going on the stage.
  - Two explanations of his stage name: Daijisen and ja.wp say it puns on ii yōbō, "good looks"; Shinsen says Gakkai took it from a poetic name for Mount Fuji.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/伊井蓉峰 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/伊井蓉峰-29795

### 2d. Kawakami Sadayakko (川上貞奴)

All points from https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/川上貞奴 unless another source is given.

- **Early life**
  - Born Meiji 4/7/18 (= 2 Sep 1871) in Nihonbashi; died 7 Dec 1946. Real name 小山貞. Twelfth child of a money-changer's family that fell on hard times.
  - Adopted at 7 by the geisha house Hamadaya in Yoshichō. Became the geisha "Yakko", patronised by Itō Hirobumi and Saionji Kinmochi.
- **Marriage to Kawakami Otojirō**
  - Year disputed: 1891 (Shinsen, Nipponica, ja.wp timeline); 1894 (ja.wp main text); October 1893 (Leslie Downer's biography, cited in en.wp). https://kotobank.jp/word/川上貞奴-48191 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sada_Yacco
  - Go-between: Kaneko Kentarō.
- **Debut abroad, 1899**
  - The troupe sailed on 30 Apr 1899 with 18 people, organised by the promoter Kushibiki Yumindo.
  - The San Francisco promoter billed her as the star. She debuted there dancing 『道成寺』; Shinsen adds 『袈裟と盛遠』. Downer gives the debut date as 25 May 1899.
  - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sada_Yacco ; https://kotobank.jp/word/川上貞奴-48191
  - Their manager ran off with the takings in San Francisco.
  - The sketch "Geisha and Knight" (芸者と武士) was created in Seattle.
- **Paris, 1900.** At the 1900 Exposition the troupe played the Loie Fuller theatre, opening on 4 July. Rodin, who was at the opening, asked to sculpt her and was refused. In August she danced 『道成寺』 at a garden party given by President Émile Loubet. A "Yacco" kimono-style dress became fashionable. France gave her the Officier d'Académie decoration.
- **First appearance in Japan.** 1903, 『オセロ』 at the Meiji-za, as a "straight-drama" (正劇) actress. Shinsen and Heibonsha in https://kotobank.jp/word/川上貞奴-48191
- **Actress school, 1908.** She founded the Teikoku Joyū Yōseijo (帝国女優養成所) in Shiba in 1908, opened in September 1908 according to Heibonsha. https://kotobank.jp/word/帝国女優養成所-1371181
  - Entry rules: age 16–25, higher-elementary education, and two guarantors.
  - First students included Mori Ritsuko and Murata Kakuko.
  - Shibusawa Eiichi spoke at the opening, as head of its backers.
  - The school passed to the Imperial Theatre as its training school (技芸学校). Yamakawa dates this to 1909.
- **After Otojirō.** He died on 11 Nov 1911. She retired in 1917 after playing 『アイーダ』. Then she lived with the "power king" Fukuzawa Momosuke and ran a children's theatre, 1924–32.

### 2e. Matsui Sumako (松井須磨子)

All points from https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/松井須磨子 unless another source is given.

- **Early life**
  - Born 8 Mar 1886 (her family register says 1 November); died 5 Jan 1919. Real name 小林正子. Youngest of nine children of a former samurai of the Matsushiro domain.
  - Divorced twice.
  - Turned down by an actor-training school for her flat face, she had wax injected to raise the bridge of her nose. The wax later shifted and caused painful inflammation.
- **Training.** Entered the Bungei Kyōkai (文芸協会) Drama Institute of Tsubouchi Shōyō in 1909, in its first year.
- **1911.** Ophelia, at the Imperial Theatre in May 1911. **Nora** in 『人形の家』 (translated by Shimamura Hōgetsu) in a private performance in September 1911, and again at the Imperial Theatre in November–December 1911.
- **Geijutsuza.** Her affair with Hōgetsu forced them both out of the Bungei Kyōkai. They founded the Geijutsuza (芸術座), which opened in 1913.
- **Katyusha, 1914.** She played Katyusha in 『復活』 (Tolstoy's *Resurrection*, adapted by Bataille, translated by Hōgetsu), the Geijutsuza's third production, at the Imperial Theatre in March 1914. The song is covered in section 8.
- **Later songs**
  - 1915: 「ゴンドラの唄」.
  - 1917: 「さすらいの唄」 and 「今度生まれたら」. The latter was banned in several prefectures for the line "かわい女子と寝て暮らそ" and is called Japan's first banned record. Sales of "270,000" are claimed.
- **Death**
  - Hōgetsu died of the Spanish flu on 5 Nov 1918.
  - She hanged herself on 5 Jan 1919 in the property room of the Geijutsu Club at Ushigome Yokoderamachi, during a run of 『カルメン』 at the Yūrakuza.
  - Her written wish to be buried with Hōgetsu was refused by his widow.

### 2f. The Imperial Theatre (帝国劇場, 1911) and its actresses

- **Opening.** Opened 1 Mar 1911; first performances 4 Mar 1911. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/帝国劇場
- **Building and business methods** (Heibonsha and Nipponica in https://kotobank.jp/word/帝国劇場-100028)
  - Designed by Yokogawa Tamisuke in a Renaissance-revival style. 1,700 seats, all chairs, with an orchestra pit.
  - Abolished the theatre tea-houses, numbered the seats and sold tickets in advance.
  - The word "teketsu" (from "ticket") for tickets and for the women who clipped them started here.
- **House company and repertoire.** Kabuki actors on contract, led by Onoe Baikō VI and Matsumoto Kōshirō VII, alternated with "actress plays" (女優劇) by graduates of the theatre's own school. The slogan 「今日は帝劇、明日は三越」 ("Today the Imperial Theatre, tomorrow Mitsukoshi") became a catchphrase. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/帝国劇場
- **Mori Ritsuko (森律子)**
  - 30 Oct 1890 – 22 Jul 1961. Daughter of the lawyer and Diet member Mori Hajime. Graduate of Atomi girls' school.
  - The star of the school's first class of 11 students.
  - Debut: Princess Uraishiro in Yamazaki Shikō's 『頼朝』 at the opening of the Imperial Theatre in March 1911.
  - Known for Masuda Tarō Kaja's comedies, such as 『ドッチャダンネ』. Toured Europe in 1913.
  - Sources: https://kotobank.jp/word/森律子-16959 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/森律子
  - It is said that Atomi struck her from its alumni list, and that in 1916 her younger brother, a First Higher School student, killed himself after his classmates demanded that "a lowly actress" leave the school sports day.
  - ja.wp disputes the expulsion, citing her later attendance at alumni meetings (Yomiuri, 1915 and 1917).

## 3. Shinpa (新派)

### 3a. Sōshi shibai: Sudō Sadanori (角藤定憲), December 1888

- **Sudō Sadanori**
  - Born Keiō 3/7/16 (= 15 Aug 1867) in Okayama; died 20 Jan 1907 in a dressing room in Kōbe.
  - Former Kyoto policeman who resigned rather than suppress the Freedom and People's Rights movement. Joined Nakae Chōmin's newspaper, the 東雲新聞, where he serialised a novel about himself, 『剛胆の書生』.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/角藤定憲
- **The first performance**
  - **3 Dec 1888**, Shinmachi-za, Osaka (Heibonsha). Chōmin encouraged it.
  - Troupe name given as 「大日本壮士改良演劇会」 (Heibonsha, Nipponica) or 「日本改良演劇」 (another Heibonsha entry).
  - Plays: Sudō's 『耐忍之書生貞操佳人』 and 『勤王美談上野曙』, the second credited to Kōtoku Shūsui.
  - Sources: https://kotobank.jp/word/壮士芝居-89399 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/角藤定憲
- **The reason for it.** After the 1887 Peace Preservation Ordinance (保安条例), political speeches were all but banned. Activists (sōshi, 壮士) turned to plays to spread their ideas. Nipponica in https://kotobank.jp/word/壮士芝居-89399
- **Later.** Sudō reached Tokyo only in June 1894 (Azuma-za), too late to matter. A stone at Shinmachi-minami Park, Osaka, put up in 1953, marks the "birthplace of reformed drama". https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/角藤定憲

### 3b. Kawakami Otojirō (川上音二郎) and "Oppekepe"

- **Early life**
  - Born Bunkyū 4/1/1 (= 8 Feb 1864) in Hakata; died 11 Nov 1911.
  - Ran away to Osaka in 1878. Said to have been a temple boy who became Fukuzawa Yukichi's servant-student at Keiō, and briefly a police constable.
  - Called himself "Jiyū Dōji" (自由童子, "the Liberty Kid"). Barred from political speaking for a year on 13 Sep 1883. Licensed as a storyteller in 1885. Performed as the comic 浮世亭◯◯.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/川上音二郎
- **Shosei shibai**
  - Founded his "student theatre" (書生芝居) at the Unohi-za in Sakai: **5 Feb 1891** per Heibonsha; February 1891 per the Kawakami article; March 1891 per the ja.wp shinpa article.
  - June 1891: Nakamura-za, Tokyo, with 『板垣君遭難実記』 and the Oppekepe song.
  - Sources: https://kotobank.jp/word/書生芝居-80439 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/新派
- **The Oppekepe song (オッペケペー節)**
  - Words by Kawakami, dated 1889. The melody came from the Kamigata rakugo storytellers.
  - He sang it as an encore wearing a red battle surcoat (陣羽織) and a headband, waving a fan printed with the rising sun.
  - The surviving lyrics mock Westernised fops. One verse scolds women who are taken to see kabuki actors (see Telling details).
  - Recorded by the Gramophone Company during the 1900 European tour; it is called the first recording of Japanese voices. The ja.wp article says Otojirō's own voice is not on it.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/オッペケペー節
  - Kurata Kiyohiro (Nipponica) says it was written at the end of 1889, first sung at a yose in Shinkyōgoku, Kyoto, and peaked after June 1891. https://kotobank.jp/word/演歌-170980
- **War plays, 1894–95**
  - January 1894: detective plays 『意外』 and 『又意外』, about the Sōma affair, at the Asakusa-za.
  - When the Sino-Japanese War broke out he staged 『壮絶快絶日清戦争』: August 1894 (Nipponica) or September 1894, Asakusa-za (ja.wp). Takata Minoru played Li Hongzhang.
  - He then visited the front in Korea and staged 『川上音二郎戦地見聞日記』.
  - 1895: 『威海衛陥落』 at the Kabuki-za, the first time "amateur" players appeared there.
  - Sources: https://kotobank.jp/word/新派劇-538751 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/川上音二郎 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高田実
- **Later projects**
  - Kawakami-za, Kanda, opened 2 Jul 1896.
  - Lost two Diet elections in 1898.
  - Tried to escape his creditors by small boat from Tsukiji.
  - 1903: "straight drama" (正劇) versions of 『オセロ』 and 『ハムレット』.
  - 1908: Teikoku-za in Kitahama, Osaka.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/川上音二郎 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/新派劇-538751

### 3c. From Saibikan to the "three leaders" (三頭目)

- **Troupes after Kawakami**
  - July 1892: Yamaguchi Sadao's troupe, which trained the onnagata Kawai Takeo and Kitamura Rokurō.
  - September 1896, Osaka: the Seibidan (成美団), founded by Takata Minoru, Kitamura and others. Its second incarnation began in June 1900 at the Asahi-za.
  - 1898, Tokiwa-za, Tokyo: Mizuno Yoshimi's 奨励会, with the onnagata Kawai and Kojima Fumie.
  - 1902: Ii Yōhō staged Chikamatsu plays at the Masago-za.
  - 1904 onward: the Hongō-za company of Takata and Kitamura.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/新派 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/新派劇-538751
- **The "three leaders" (三頭目) era** (from about 1917): Ii Yōhō, Kawai Takeo and Kitamura Rokurō. The ja.wp shinpa article says shinpa was at its peak around 1907. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/新派
- **Who watched.** The critic Ihara Seiseien observed that the early shinpa audience was mostly educated to higher-school level or above. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/新派

### 3d. Shinpa adaptations of newspaper novels

- **『金色夜叉』 (Ozaki Kōyō)**
  - Serialised in the Yomiuri Shinbun from 1 Jan 1897 to 11 May 1902, unfinished.
  - First staged in March 1898 at the Ichimura-za (Nipponica); ja.wp credits the Kawakami troupe.
  - Ii played Kan'ichi; Takata Minoru played Arao.
  - The karuta-game scene was written to be read during the New Year holiday.
  - Sources: https://kotobank.jp/word/金色夜叉-67257 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/金色夜叉 ; Heibonsha in https://kotobank.jp/word/新聞小説-82525
- **『不如帰』 (Hototogisu, Tokutomi Roka)**
  - Serialised in the Kokumin Shinbun from 29 Nov 1898 to 24 May 1899.
  - First staged in February 1901 at the Asahi-za, Osaka, adapted by 並木萍水. Akizuki Keitarō played Takeo; Kitamura Rokurō played Namiko.
  - Kitamura's faithful version at the Hongō-za in April 1908 was a great success.
  - Namiko's last words: "I'll never be born a woman again" (もう女なんぞに生まれはしない).
  - Source: https://kotobank.jp/word/不如帰-133743
- **『婦系図』 (Onna keizu, Izumi Kyōka)**
  - Serialised in the Yamato Shinbun in 1907.
  - First staged at the Shintomi-za in September 1908 (Nipponica, Heibonsha; the Nichigai title dictionary says October 1908). Adapted by Yanagawa Shun'yō. Ii played Hayase; Kitamura played Otsuta.
  - Kyōka afterwards wrote the famous 「湯島の境内」 scene himself, after seeing the play; it is not in the novel.
  - Source: https://kotobank.jp/word/婦系図-42006
- **Other repertory.** 『己が罪』, 『乳姉妹』, 『滝の白糸』 and others. Kawakami's troupe staged 『滝の白糸』 at the Asakusa-za in 1895, and Kitamura played Shiraito at the end of 1896. https://kotobank.jp/word/新派劇-538751 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/喜多村緑郎_(初代)

### 3e. Shinpa onnagata and actresses

- **Kitamura Rokurō I (初代 喜多村緑郎)**
  - Meiji 4/7/23 (= 7 Sep 1871) – 16 May 1961. Son of a Nihonbashi pharmacist.
  - First appeared in 1892. Played almost only women's roles.
  - Offstage he was a dandy in Western suits with a cigar, who drank coffee and whisky, went to foreign films and played mahjong and golf.
  - Named a Living National Treasure in 1955.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/喜多村緑郎_(初代)
- **Kawai Takeo (河合武雄)**
  - 13 Mar 1877 – 21 Mar 1942. Son of the kabuki actor Ōtani Baju III. Debuted secretly in 1893.
  - His style was florid and cheerful. He played Sappho (1904) and Juliet (1904), and in the 公衆劇団 (1913–18) Hofmannsthal's *Elektra*.
  - Wrote 『随筆 女形』 (1937).
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/河合武雄
- **Takata Minoru (高田実)**, the "Danjūrō of shinpa": Meiji 4/3/19 (= 8 May 1871) – 24 Sep 1916. The ja.wp shinpa article wrongly gives 1918. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高田実
- **Mizutani Yaeko I (初代 水谷八重子)**, a later star actress
  - 1 Aug 1905 – 1979.
  - First stage appearance in September 1913, in the Geijutsuza's first production.
  - First film in 1921, 『寒椿』, credited only as "覆面令嬢" (the masked young lady) because her school, Futaba girls' school, would not allow her name to be used.
  - Joined shinpa after the 1923 earthquake and was a regular from 1927.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/水谷八重子_(初代)

## 4. The musume-gidayū craze

### 4a. Background and scale

- **Before Meiji.** Women chanting gidayū (the narrative music of the puppet theatre) is recorded from the Bunka–Bunsei era. It was banned in 1805 and suppressed in the Tenpō reforms of 1841, when 36 women were jailed (Heibonsha). https://kotobank.jp/word/女義太夫-456030
- **Kyoto, 1872.** "About 200" women gidayū performers in the city. Source: Kokugo, citing the Kyoto Shinbun no. 51 (1872). https://kotobank.jp/word/女義太夫-456030
- **Legalisation, 1877.** The revised yose regulations let women entertainers appear. https://kotobank.jp/word/女義太夫-456030
- **Arrivals in Tokyo**
  - Takemoto Kyōshi (竹本京枝) from Nagoya: 1882 (Heibonsha, ja.wp) or 1883 (Nipponica).
  - Takemoto Tōgyoku (竹本東玉) from Osaka: 1885.
  - Sources: https://kotobank.jp/word/女義太夫-456030 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/女義太夫
- **Growth.** Gidayū performers of both sexes grew three- to five-fold between 1875 and 1887 (『諸芸人名録』, 『統計集誌』). By about 1900 Tokyo had **more than 1,000** musume-gidayū. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/女義太夫
- **Headline halls in Tokyo.** 宮松 (Ningyōchō), 両国, 若竹 (Hongō), 東橋亭 (Azumabashi) and 新柳. Same page, citing 篠田鉱造『明治百話』.
- **Peak.** Nipponica dates the peak "to about 1895, the end of the Sino-Japanese War". It says audiences cared less for the art than for the looks and gestures of girls aged 10 to the early 20s. https://kotobank.jp/word/女義太夫-456030
- **Early testimony.** Kokugo quotes the 国民新聞 of 17 Jan 1892: in the yose, musume-gidayū had taken over and the other narrative music styles (Kiyomoto, Tokiwazu) were "like a fire gone out". https://kotobank.jp/word/娘義太夫-140545 (the Kotobank page for 娘義太夫; it redirected to this ID when fetched)

### 4b. Takemoto Ayanosuke I (初代 竹本綾之助)

- **Basic facts**
  - 10 Jun 1875 – 31 Jan 1942. Born in Minami-tatamiya-machi, Osaka. Real name 石井園.
  - Came to Tokyo at 10 or 11: 1885 (Nipponica), 1886 (Shinsen) or 1887 (ja.wp).
  - Debuted as 竹本玉之助. Became Ayanosuke in 1888, under Takemoto Ayase-dayū.
  - Called **"八丁荒らし"**: she emptied every hall for eight chō around her.
  - Retired in 1898 to marry the trading-company man Ishii Kenta. Returned in 1908.
  - Source: https://kotobank.jp/word/竹本綾之助-1361183
- **Boyish style.** Nipponica: Ayanosuke and Takemoto Kotosa (竹本小土佐, who came from Nagoya in 1886), both in their early teens, "drew crowds with a boyish style" (ボーイッシュ・スタイル). Nipponica (Ayanosuke entry) also describes her in a boy's topknot (稚児髷) and a long-sleeved kimono. https://kotobank.jp/word/女義太夫-456030 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/竹本綾之助-1361183
- **Hasegawa Shigure's portrait** (memoir-essay) https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000726/files/45982_26593.html
  - The aunt who raised her kept her in a boy's cropped haircut (五分刈) and let her play on stilts. The neighbours called her "男おんな" (man-woman).
  - On stage her hair went from the crop to a boy's topknot (稚子髷) and then to a man's topknot (男髷). Kotosa also wore a man's topknot.
  - In 1890, at 16, Ayanosuke was compared to the cherry, Koshiko to the wisteria and Kotosa to the peach.
  - During a sold-out run in 1885 a jealous plot to put mercury in her teacup was discovered, and the run was stopped.
  - At her first headline run, so many curtains were given by fans that they had to be swapped in one after another. A sake wholesaler advertised her on the horse-drawn trams. A woodblock publisher put her portrait beside the storyteller Enchō and the gidayū master Koshiji-dayū.

### 4c. The dōsuru-ren (堂摺連 / どうする連)

- **The name.** Fans shouted "dōsuru, dōsuru!" ("What will happen? What will she do?") at the emotional climax (sawari) of a piece. The earliest citation is the 風俗画報 no. 198 (1899), which defines 堂摺連 as "the rowdy element of the gidayū audience". Daijisen dates the term from about 1887–1897 to early Taishō. https://kotobank.jp/word/どうする連-580709
- **What they did**
  - Clapped in time and ground teacup bottoms together.
  - Pushed or ran beside the rickshaws of their favourites.
  - Fought over hairpins that fell (by design) from a performer's hair.
  - Formed named fan clubs: 花菱連 and 糸成連 for Takemoto Kyōko.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/女義太夫
- **Hasegawa's account** (memoir) https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000726/files/45982_26593.html
  - The first organised fans were students of Keiō (Mita) and the Kōgyokusha school in Shiba. Keiō's leader was 坂本易徳, later the eccentric writer 紅蓮洞.
  - They followed their "queen" from hall to hall in groups, in rain or snow. The keenest hung on her rickshaw shafts.
  - In the hall they kept the beat with their wooden shoe-check tokens (下足札) and called "ドウスル".
  - Ayanosuke's "martyrs": a young policeman dismissed for his devotion; a student who tried to kill himself; a man who began to commit seppuku, whose mother came to plead with her; a thirty-year-old man who followed her carrying a red handkerchief and a scent bag and sent 100-odd letters in six months, written in the style of jōruri texts.
- **Backlash**
  - 1898: Education Minister Toyama Masakazu commented on immorality in the yose. Promoters began barring fans from the dressing rooms (Nipponica). https://kotobank.jp/word/女義太夫-456030
  - 1900: the Yorozu Chōhō (Kuroiwa Ruikō's paper) attacked young men "crazed by such wenches as musume-gidayū" (「匹婦娘義太夫如きに狂する」) as "rot, rot, the great rot of youth". https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/女義太夫
  - Heibonsha says the problem became a social issue in 1900 on grounds of public morals (風紀紊乱).
  - **I found no specific police order** in the sources consulted; the backlash described is ministerial and journalistic. See "Uncertain".
- **Later fans.** Shiga Naoya followed Toyotake Shōnosuke from about 1903. Kinoshita Mokutarō followed her sister Shōgiku. Takahama Kyoshi was devoted to Takemoto Kotosa. Socialist meetings of 1906 had gidayū as entertainment. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/女義太夫
- **Toyotake Roshō (豊竹呂昇).** She raised the standing of the art and appeared at the Yūrakuza "masters' concerts" (Nipponica). Retired in 1924. The Yoshizawa company recorded her voice to play alongside films. https://kotobank.jp/word/女義太夫-456030 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/吉沢商店

### 4d. Comparable fan phenomena

- **Asakusa Opera "peragoro"** (1917–23); see section 7.
- **Benshi.** Audiences called out to benshi as they did to kabuki actors. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/活動弁士
- **Katyusha song.** Schools banned singing it; see section 8.
- **Taishō bromide buyers.** See section 9.

## 5. Magic lantern, screen and early cinema

### 5a. Magic lantern (gentō, 幻灯) and utsushi-e (写し絵)

- **Utsushi-e.** The dyer 亀屋熊吉 (stage name 池田都楽 / 三笑亭都楽) built his own lantern on the model of the Dutch "Ekiman" glass. He first played the yose in March 1803 (Kyōwa 3) with moving, painted glass slides called utsushi-e. Successors included 両川亭船遊 and 玉川文楽; the 2nd Tamagawa Bunraku carried the art into Shōwa. In Osaka the same art was called nishiki-kage-e (錦影絵). https://kotobank.jp/word/写し絵-440781 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/幻灯-492807 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/錦影絵-1384695
- **The word "gentō".** Coined in early Meiji as a translation of "magic lantern". Heibonsha credits Tejima Seiichi (手島精一) of the Education Ministry with the coinage. Tejima brought lanterns back from America in 1873 (Nipponica) or 1874 (Bazzell). https://kotobank.jp/word/幻灯-492807 ; Bazzell, EAJRS 2011 slides https://www.eajrs.net/files/happyo/bazzell_tokiko_11.pdf
- **Educational slides.** Bazzell lists an Education Ministry commission to **Tsurubuchi Hatsuzō (鶴淵初蔵)** and Nakajima Matsuchi (中島待乳) for slides known as 幻燈板. No date or quantity is given. I could not find a Japanese entry for Tsurubuchi.
- **The boom.** The lantern boom ran from about 1889–90. During the Sino-Japanese War (1894–95) domestic machines spread as toys. Uses included sermons, lectures and school "lantern meetings" on ethics, geography, history and natural history. Sets of 12 connected slides were sold for home use (Nipponica). Kokugo says lanterns "sold explosively" in Meiji 20s (1887–96). https://kotobank.jp/word/幻灯-492807
- **The word "eiga".** The word 映画 itself first meant a lantern slide or its projected image; an 1886 newspaper uses it with the reading うつしえ (Heibonsha). https://kotobank.jp/word/幻灯-492807
- **Decline.** Utsushi-e lasted into the late Meiji yose (Kokugo). Lanterns gave way to moving pictures from late Meiji and came back as educational slides around 1935. https://kotobank.jp/word/写し絵-440781 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/幻灯-492807

### 5b. Arrival of moving pictures, 1896–97

- **Kinetoscope (Kōbe, 1896)**
  - Imported by a foreign trading firm in the Kōbe settlement and bought by the gun dealer **Takahashi Shinji (高橋信治)**.
  - Shown privately to Prince Komatsu Akihito on 17 Nov 1896.
  - Public showing at the **Shinkō Club (神港倶楽部), Kōbe, from 25 Nov 1896**. The advertisement called it 「ニーテスコップ（電気作用写真活動機械）」 and announced five films in rotation until 29 Nov.
  - Then at the Nanchi Enbujō, Osaka (3–22 Dec 1896; again 1–12 Jan 1897), with the professional barker **Ueda Hoteiken (上田布袋軒)** explaining. He is often called the first benshi.
  - Admission: 10 or 15 sen.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/キネトスコープ
- **Cinematographe, 1897.** Inabata Katsutarō (稲畑勝太郎) had studied in Lyon with Auguste Lumière. He brought the machine back with the Lumière operator Constant Girel.
  - First projected show in Japan: **15 Feb 1897**, Nanchi Enbujō, Osaka (to 28 Feb).
  - Then Kyoto (Tōkō-za, 1 Mar – 5 Jun 1897) and Tokyo (Kawakami-za, 8–28 Mar 1897).
  - A 2020 study found five machines working in Japan by July 1897.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/シネマトグラフ
- **Vitascope, 1897**
  - Araki Waichi (荒木和一) of Osaka: Shinmachi Enbujō, 22–24 Feb 1897 (the article also says 21 Feb), billed as 「蓄動射影会」.
  - The Arai company (新居商会) of Tokyo: its agent Shibata Chūjirō bought the machine and 16 films for ¥3,500. Trial at the Kabuki-za, 27 Feb 1897. Public run at the **Kinkikan (錦輝館), Kanda, 6–22 Mar 1897**, under the name 「活動大写真」.
  - Shown before Crown Prince Yoshihito on 26 Apr 1897.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ヴァイタスコープ

### 5c. The first Japanese films

- **First film shot by a Japanese.** Asano Shirō (浅野四郎) of the Konishi photographic shop shot 『日本橋の鉄道馬車』 on 31 Dec 1897 (the camera had been imported in October 1897).
- **First programme of Japanese films.** 20 Jun 1899 at the Kabuki-za, billed as 「日本率先活動大写真」 and organised by **Komada Kōyō**: street scenes plus **geisha dances**. The dancers were Yanagibashi, Yoshichō and Shinbashi geisha and Gion geisha of the Ichiriki house; the dances included 『道成寺』 and 『かっぽれ』. Shown again at the Meiji-za from 14 Jul 1899.
- Source for both: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/浅野四郎
- **『紅葉狩』 (Momijigari, 1899)**
  - Shot on **28 Nov 1899** by Shibata Tsunekichi (柴田常吉), in daylight on a temporary stage in the garden of the Umebayashi tea-house behind the Kabuki-za.
  - Cast: Danjūrō IX as **Princess Sarashina, a demon in disguise**; Kikugorō V as Taira no Koremochi; the 14-year-old Onoe Ushinosuke (later Kikugorō VI) as the mountain god.
  - Danjūrō hated the cinema and allowed it only if it was not shown while he lived. Wind made him drop his fan; there was no retake.
  - Shown privately at his house on 3 Nov 1900. First shown in public at the Naka-za, Osaka, on **7 Jul 1903**, after Kikugorō's death.
  - It is the oldest surviving Japanese-made film. Rediscovered in 1939 and bought for ¥50. It became the first film designated an Important Cultural Property, in July 2009.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/紅葉狩_(映画)

### 5d. Benshi (活動弁士)

- **Early benshi.** Every early showing had an explainer. Ueda Hoteiken was born in Kaei 2 (1849) and had been a gidayū chanter and circus barker. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/活動弁士
- **Somei Saburō (染井三郎).** Real name 浦川成雄; died 30 Dec 1960.
  - Started by narrating "train films" (汽車活動), in which seated customers watched scenery pass a train window.
  - From about 1906 he was the benshi at the Denkikan in Asakusa, Japan's first permanent cinema.
  - Famous for the Italian epic *Antony and Cleopatra* (1914). His calm "objective explanation" (客観説明) style made him the leading figure of the early benshi.
  - Source: https://kotobank.jp/word/染井三郎-1672114
- **The Denkikan.** Opened in October 1903 by the Yoshizawa company, in a former hall for electrical demonstrations. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/吉沢商店
- **Licensing, 1917.** The Metropolitan Police's moving-picture regulations (活動写真興行取締規則), 14 Jul 1917, made benshi licensed. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/活動弁士
- **Numbers.** In 1927 there were 6,818 benshi, including 180 women. Posters often showed the benshi's photograph. Source: en.wp citing David Cook (1990). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benshi
- **A woman benshi.** At showings of the 1914 film 『カチューシャ』, a woman benshi sang the song. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/立花貞二郎

### 5e. Studios and companies

- **Yoshizawa (吉澤商店)**, run by Kawaura Ken'ichi
  - Earlier exported woodblock prints and stamps, then made lanterns.
  - Showed a Cinematographe at the Minato-za, Yokohama, on 9 Mar 1897.
  - Filmed the Boxer War and showed it from 18 Oct 1900 (Kinkikan).
  - Opened the Denkikan in 1903.
  - Built Japan's first studio, a 24 m × 30 m glasshouse at Gyōninzaka, Meguro, begun January 1908 and working by April 1908.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/吉沢商店
- **M. Pathé (M・パテー商会)**
  - Founded 4 Jul 1906 by Umeya Shōkichi. "M" stands for Umeya; "Pathé" was borrowed without permission. Its first show was at the Shintomi-za. Profits went to Sun Yat-sen's cause.
  - In 1908 it began fiction films, casting the women's-kabuki actress **Nakamura Kasen**, among others.
  - 1909: Ōkubo studio and the film 『新不如帰』.
  - 1911–12: filmed the Shirase Antarctic expedition.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/M・パテー商会
- **Nikkatsu (日活)**
  - Merger of Yokota Shōkai, Yoshizawa, Fukuhōdō and M. Pathé.
  - Agreed in March 1912 as 「日本活動フィルム株式會社」 with ¥10 million capital. Formally founded **10 Sep 1912**; the M. Pathé article says 1 Sep.
  - The Mukōjima studio for contemporary (shinpa-style) drama opened in 1913.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日活 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/M・パテー商会
- **Makino Shōzō and Onoe Matsunosuke**
  - Makino Shōzō (牧野省三), who ran the Senbon-za in Kyoto, shot 『本能寺合戦』 in 1908 for Yokota.
  - From 『碁盤忠信 源氏礎』 (filmed 17 Oct 1909) he made **Onoe Matsunosuke** (尾上松之助) Japan's first film star, "Medama no Matchan" (Matchan with the goggling eyes), in more than 1,000 films.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/牧野省三 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/尾上松之助

### 5f. Onnagata on film, the first film actresses, and shinpa on screen

- **Tachibana Teijirō (立花貞二郎)**
  - March 1894 – 11 Nov 1918. A former kabuki child actor and shinpa onnagata.
  - Entered films in 1909. Became Nikkatsu Mukōjima's star heroine as **Katyusha** in 『カチューシャ』 (October 1914) and was called "Japan's Mary Pickford".
  - He timed his movements to the sound of the hand-cranked camera.
  - Died of tuberculosis; some say lead poisoning.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/立花貞二郎
- **Other onnagata on film.** Kinugasa Teinosuke played Omiya in Nikkatsu's 『金色夜叉』 (1918). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/金色夜叉
- **Nikkatsu drops onnagata**
  - Its last onnagata film, 『京屋襟店』, opened on 30 Dec 1922.
  - About 13 Mukōjima actors, onnagata among them, left for Kokkatsu (国活).
  - Nikkatsu hired Okada Yoshiko and other actresses.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日活
- **First film actresses, 1919**
  - Kaeriyama Norimasa (帰山教正) of Tenkatsu made 『生の輝き』 (shot summer 1918) and 『深山の乙女』 with the Geijutsuza-trained **Hanayagi Harumi (花柳はるみ)**: 24 Feb 1896 – 11 Oct 1962, real name 糟谷いし.
  - Release was delayed a year by benshi hostility. Both films opened on **13 Sep 1919**: the first at the Azabu-kan, the second at the Hōgyoku-kan.
  - Both films are lost.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/生の輝き ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/花柳はるみ
  - The Tachibana article says Shōchiku Kamata began training actors and actresses in 1920. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/立花貞二郎
- **Rensa-geki (連鎖劇)**, about 1908–17: shows that alternated live acting with filmed scenes, the same actors appearing in both. Mostly shinpa melodramas.
  - In March 1917, 13 houses in Tokyo's 15 wards showed them (Gonda Yasunosuke's survey).
  - After rensa-geki were banned, Sawada Shōjirō's Shinkokugeki and the period-drama film rose.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/連鎖劇
- **Shinpa on film.** For example 『金色夜叉』 was filmed in 1912 by both Yokota and Yoshizawa, by Nikkatsu in 1918, 1921 and 1924, and by Shōchiku in 1922. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/金色夜叉

## 6. Takarazuka and the Shōchiku revues

### 6a. Kobayashi Ichizō and the railway

- **Kobayashi Ichizō (小林一三).** Born 3 Jan 1873 in Yamanashi; died 25 Jan 1957. Keiō graduate (1892), worked at Mitsui Bank until 1907. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/小林一三 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/小林一三-65799
- **Minoo Arima Electric Railway (箕面有馬電気軌道)**
  - Company founded in October 1907. Opened the Umeda–Takarazuka and Ishibashi–Minoo lines on **10 Mar 1910**.
  - Two collisions in the first eight days killed three people.
  - Kobayashi sold housing plots along the line.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/箕面有馬電気軌道
- **Takarazuka Shin-Onsen (宝塚新温泉).** Opened **1 May 1911** on the left bank of the Muko River. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚ファミリーランド
- **The "Paradise".** Opened 1 Jul 1912: a three-storey reinforced-concrete building with a "rare-machines room", an indoor sports hall, shops, and Japan's first indoor pool. The pool failed because it was unheated and men and women were not allowed to swim together. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/小林一三 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚ファミリーランド

### 6b. From choir to company

- **Model.** The Mitsukoshi boys' band (三越少年音楽隊) was formed in **1909**. It became the 三越音楽隊 in 1915 and was disbanded in 1925. Source: ja.wp 三越, citing a paper by 玉川裕子. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/三越
  - Kobayashi copied the Osaka Mitsukoshi boys' band and took advice from Mitsukoshi (ja.wp 小林一三, citing 伊井春樹 2017). The Shirokiya girls' band is also named as an inspiration. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/小林一三 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
  - Nagoya's いとう呉服店少年音楽隊, founded 18 Mar 1911 and the ancestor of the Tokyo Philharmonic, was told that only military bands and the Mitsukoshi band then existed. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東京フィルハーモニー交響楽団 ; Nagoya City Library reference report https://www.library.city.nagoya.jp/img/reference/chosahoukoku_56.pdf
- **Formation.** The 宝塚唱歌隊 (Takarazuka Choir) was formed in **July 1913** and renamed 宝塚少女歌劇養成会 in December 1913. The girls had left ordinary elementary school and were paid as much as university graduates. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- **Teachers.** Music: 安藤弘 and his wife 智恵子, both from the Tokyo Music School. Stage direction: 高尾楓陰 and 久松一声, who had run children's fairy-tale theatre in Osaka. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/小林一三
- **First performance: 1 Apr – 30 May 1914**, in the Paradise Theatre, made from the pool, with the dressing rooms as stage and the pool as auditorium.
  - Programme: the opera 『ドンブラコ』 (Momotarō, by 北村季晴), 『浮れ達磨』 (本居長世 and 吉丸一昌), the dance 『胡蝶』, an orchestral piece and choruses.
  - Average audience about 1,100 a day. Bathers at the spa got in free.
  - The ja.wp timeline gives "March 1914" and should be read with caution.
  - A ja.wp photo caption calls the show an attraction at a "wedding exposition" (婚礼博覧会); I found no other source for this.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/1914年の宝塚歌劇公演一覧
  - Nipponica: 16 performers. https://kotobank.jp/word/宝塚歌劇団-92817
- **The first male-role player.** **Takamine Taeko (高峰妙子)** played Momotarō and is called the first otokoyaku star.
  - Born 6 May 1899 (ja.wp; Nipponica says 1900). Joined at 14 in July 1913.
  - Said to have been hired on the spot for singing the scale as "do-re-mi" instead of "hi-fu-mi".
  - Left in 1927.
  - Kumoi Namiko (雲井浪子, 1901–2003) played the monkey, aged 12.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高峰妙子 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/雲井浪子
- **Stage names.** Taken from the Hyakunin Isshu poems on Kobayashi's idea, for example 高峰妙子 from Akahito's Fuji poem. https://kotobank.jp/word/宝塚歌劇団-92817 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高峰妙子
- **Kobayashi as playwright.** He wrote early pieces himself: 『紅葉狩』 (1914), 『竹取物語』 (1916), 『クレオパトラ』 (1918) and others. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/小林一三
- **Early stars**
  - Around 1919 the "four heavenly kings" were Kumoi Namiko, Shinohara Asaji, Takamine Taeko and Takasago Matsuko. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/篠原淺茅
  - In the early years the female-role players (娘役) were the more popular. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
  - Kumoi Namiko left in 1919 and married Tsubouchi Shikō (Shōyō's nephew) on 21 Jul 1919. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/雲井浪子
- **Tokyo debut and magazine.** First Tokyo performance at the Imperial Theatre on 26 May 1918. The magazine 『歌劇』 was founded in 1918. Average attendance in 1918: 2,000 a day. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団

### 6c. School, troupes, Grand Theatre and Mon Paris

- **Music school.** The Takarazuka Music and Opera School (宝塚音楽歌劇学校) was authorised as a private school; its date is given as January 1919 (ja.wp) or December 1918 (Nipponica). It had a one-year preparatory course, a one-year main course and a research course, with dormitories.
  - Its pupils' green hakama became "the envy of girls across the country" (Nipponica).
  - A short-lived male course in 1919 had 8 students and closed after 10 months.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚音楽学校 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/宝塚歌劇団-92817 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- **Troupes.** Flower and Moon troupes in 1921; the Snow troupe in 1924. The Paradise and Public Hall theatres burned on 22 Jan 1923. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- **Grand Theatre (宝塚大劇場), opened July 1924.** Capacity is given as 3,000 (宝塚歌劇団), 3,500 (宝塚大劇場) or 4,000 (Family Land article).
  - In 1924 a reserved seat, onsen admission and curry rice each cost 30 sen: "with one yen you can play all day" (「一圓あれば一日遊べる」).
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚大劇場
- **『モン・パリ 〜吾が巴里よ!〜』, 1 Sep 1927**
  - By Kishida Tatsuya (岸田辰彌), choreographed by Shirai Tetsuzō.
  - Japan's first "revue": 16 scenes without a curtain, 250 appearances in all, a 16-step grand staircase, and the first line dance.
  - Afterwards played at the Kabuki-za in Tokyo.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/モン・パリ ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- **Motto.** 「清く正しく美しく」 ("Pure, proper, beautiful"). https://kotobank.jp/word/宝塚歌劇団-92817

### 6d. Shōchiku's girls' revues

- **Osaka**
  - Shōchiku president Shirai Matsujirō saw Takarazuka teacher 楳茂都陸平's dance 『春から秋へ』 in 1921.
  - The 松竹楽劇部生徒養成所 opened in April 1922 at Tengachaya, Osaka, with teachers hired away from Takarazuka.
  - Trial performances at the Nakanoshima Public Hall in 1922. First public performance at the Minami-za, Kyoto, in February 1923 (『アルルの女』).
  - From May 1923 the company was based at the new Osaka Shōchikuza. 『春のおどり』 began in 1926.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/OSK日本歌劇団 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/松竹歌劇団
- **Tokyo**
  - 110 Osaka members played the opening of the Asakusa Shōchikuza on 31 Aug 1928. The Tokyo company (東京松竹楽劇部) was formed in October 1928.
  - In 1930 Mizunoe Takiko (水の江瀧子) became the first girls'-revue performer to cut her hair short, and was called 男装の麗人 ("the beauty in men's clothes").
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/松竹歌劇団

## 7. Asakusa and popular spectacle

### 7a. Asakusa Park and misemono

- **The park.** The Sensō-ji grounds were taken by the state in 1871 and made a public park by the 1873 decree. In September 1883 a pond was dug west of the temple and the sideshow booths moved there from the Okuyama area behind the temple. In January 1884 the park was divided into districts 1–6; District 6 (六区) became the entertainment quarter. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/浅草公園六区
- **Fukuchi Ōchi** managed the park from 1 Apr 1884 until he was dismissed in July 1889. Same page.
- **Main sights**
  - The Tokiwa-za theatre (1887).
  - Egawa's ball-walking troupe (江川の玉乗り) at the Daiseikan, from 1884 until 1923.
  - The Denkikan (1903).
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/浅草公園六区 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/江川マストン
- **Regulation**
  - On 3 Oct 1891, police order no. 15 (観物場取締規則) concentrated Tokyo's sideshows in District 6.
  - Earlier, the Tokyo minor-offences code of Meiji 5/11/8 (= 8 Dec 1872) banned male–female wrestling and "ugly" sights. It followed the Japan Herald editor Black's report of a child shown tearing apart a dead rabbit near Kandabashi.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/見世物小屋
- **Life-size dolls (iki-ningyō, 生人形).** Matsumoto Kisaburō (松本喜三郎, 1825–91) showed lifelike dolls in Asakusa Okuyama from 1855. Yasumoto Kamehachi (安本亀八, 1826–1900) was the other master. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/生人形
- **Asakusa Fuji (富士山縦覧場), 1887.** A climbable model of Mount Fuji, 18 ken (32 m) high, on 1,054 tsubo. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/富士山縦覧場

### 7b. Ryōunkaku (凌雲閣, "Asakusa Jūnikai")

All points from https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/凌雲閣 unless another source is given.

- **Opening.** **11 Nov 1890**. Announced for the 10th and postponed for the guests; Elevator Day, 10 November, keeps the announced date.
- **The building**
  - Height 173 shaku (about 52 m); first reported as 220 shaku.
  - Brick up to the 10th floor, wood for the 11th and 12th.
  - Designed by W. K. Burton. Proposed by the Nagaoka merchant 福原庄七. First company president: the photographer and city councillor Ezaki Reiji.
  - Floors 2–7 held 46 shops selling foreign goods; floor 8 was a rest room; floors 10–12 were viewing rooms with telescopes. Two arc lamps of 5,000 candlepower stood on floor 11.
- **Admission.** 8 sen for adults, 4 sen for children.
- **Japan's first electric lift**
  - Two cars for 10 people, from the ground floor to the 8th.
  - Broke down from opening day and was stopped in May 1891, followed by a lawsuit with Tokyo Electric Light.
  - Restored in 1914.
- **"Tokyo Hyaku Bijin", 1891.** With the lift out of service, the operators hung photographs of about 100 geisha, taken by Ogawa Kazumasa against one backdrop, on the staircase walls. Visitors voted, and the top five were honoured. ja.wp's miss-contest article calls it Japan's first; the Ryōunkaku article says a photo-judging contest is already reported in April 1890. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ミス・コンテスト
  - 枡田屋小つま ("Araigami Otsuma", aged 17) missed the top five but became a postcard and advertising model.
- **Below the tower.** The area at its foot became a district of unlicensed prostitution: "十二階下の女" was slang for a prostitute.
- **End.** The tower broke at the 8th floor in the 1 Sep 1923 earthquake. Of the 12 or 13 visitors at the top, only one survived, caught on a Fukusuke tabi advertising sign. Army engineers blew up the remains on 23 Sep 1923.
- **Kotobank entry.** https://kotobank.jp/word/凌雲閣-659093

### 7c. Panoramas, 1890

- **Two panoramas, May 1890.** The Ueno Panorama-kan opened on **7 May 1890** with the 3rd National Industrial Exhibition. The Nihon Panorama-kan in Asakusa District 6 opened on **22 May 1890**. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本パノラマ館
- **Nihon Panorama-kan: building and backers**
  - Backed by Shibusawa Eiichi, Ōkura Kihachirō and Yasuda Zenjirō. Shibusawa had seen a panorama in Paris on 7 May 1867.
  - A wooden 16-sided drum, 37.15 m across inside and about 28.8 m to the top, with a single central post.
- **Nihon Panorama-kan: the paintings**
  - Opened with the **Battle of Vicksburg** (American Civil War) panorama, bought from San Francisco for ¥100,000.
  - Between 22 May and 17 Jul 1890 it sold 107,262 adult tickets at 10 sen and 19,631 child tickets at 5 sen.
  - Replaced in March 1896 by **the Battle of Pyongyang** of 1894, painted by Koyama Shōtarō, who had been at the war.
- **Nihon Panorama-kan: the visit**
  - Visitors walked a pitch-dark corridor, climbed a spiral stair into the bright viewing platform, and left by a different route.
  - A boy with a whip explained the scene and sold photographs (4 sen) and keys (2 sen).
  - It had an in-house café, the ダイヤモンド珈琲店: coffee 3 sen; omelettes, steak and curry rice 7 sen.
  - Closed in 1909.

### 7d. Asakusa Opera, 1917–1923

All points from https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/浅草オペラ

- **Origins.** Takagi Tokuko (高木徳子), back from vaudeville in America, staged a "world variety" show at the Kinema Club on 27 May 1916.
- **Start of the boom.** After the Imperial Theatre's Western opera section was disbanded in May 1916, Takagi and Iba Takashi staged the opera 『女軍出征』 ("The Women's Army Marches to War") at the Tokiwa-za on **22 Jan 1917**. It was a hit and is taken as the start.
- **Companies**
  - Sassa Kōka's 東京歌劇座 at the Nihonkan, from 23 Oct 1917.
  - Hara Nobuko's company.
  - Shōchiku's company (1919).
  - The Negishi Grand Opera at the Kinryūkan, from 1919 to 1924. Its 『カルメン』 of 20 Mar 1922 was Enoken's debut, in the chorus.
- **The fans**
  - Seats cost 10–20 sen for half a day.
  - Ardent fans were called **ペラゴロ** ("peragoro", perhaps from opera + gorotsuki, "thug", or gigolo). The article itself compares them to the gidayū fans' 堂摺連.
  - Shouts of "Morino!" and "Sumiko!" for the stars Sawa Morino and Kawai Sumiko drowned out the dialogue.
  - Fans included Kon Tōkō, Satō Hachirō and Takada Tamotsu. Miyazawa Kenji, Kawabata Yasunari and Tokugawa Musei were enthusiasts.
- **End.** Killed by the 1923 earthquake; the last show was in October 1925.

## 8. Popular song

### 8a. Enka in the original sense: songs of the political activists

- **Definition.** Enka (演歌) began as "speech-song" (演説の歌). Kurata Kiyohiro (Nipponica and Heibonsha) counts **Oppekepe as the first enka**. He rules out earlier political verses set to old tunes, such as Ueki Emori's 「民権数え歌」 and Yasuoka Michitarō's 「よしや武士」. https://kotobank.jp/word/演歌-170980
- **Street sellers and later songs.** Imitators sang on the street and sold printed songbooks (読売). Other songs: 『ヤッツケロ節』, 『欽慕節』, 『ダイナマイトドン』. Police often suppressed them. Same entry.
- **Dynamite-bushi (ダイナマイト節).** ja.wp calls it "the oldest enka", sung by the 青年倶楽部 and Ezawa Takejirō's street-selling activists. Its refrain was printed in katakana, 「コクリ、ミンプク、ゾウシンシテ ミンリョクキュウヨウセ / もしも成らなきゃ ダイナマイトドン」, said to be a way of avoiding a ban on the leaflets. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ダイナマイト節
- **From politics to romance.** After the Sino-Japanese War the political edge faded. Students playing the gekkin (a moon-lute) sang love songs as "shosei-bushi" (書生節).
  - Students in black kimono, woven hats and light make-up who courted girls became a social problem.
  - Violin-accompanied street singers ("enkashi") followed in the 1910s and spread 『カチューシャの唄』, 『船頭小唄』 and 『籠の鳥』.
  - Source: https://kotobank.jp/word/演歌-170980
- **Soeda Azenbō (添田唖蝉坊)**
  - Meiji 5/11/25 (= 25 Dec 1872) – 8 Feb 1944.
  - Came to the activists' songs in 1890 and wrote his first songs in 1892.
  - His 「ラッパ節」 (1905–06) became, at Sakai Toshihiko's request, the 「社会党喇叭節」.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/添田唖蝉坊

### 8b. Military songs (軍歌)

- **「抜刀隊」.** Words by Toyama Masakazu, music by Charles Leroux. Premiered at a concert at the Rokumeikan (the former Peers' Club in Hibiya) in 1885. Called the first popular song with a Western melody. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/抜刀隊_(軍歌)
- **Sino-Japanese War output.** In the two years of the war, **1,300 military songs and 140 songbooks** were produced (辻田真佐憲). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/軍歌
- **「勇敢なる水兵」 (1895).** By Sasaki Nobutsuna and Oku Yoshiisa, about a dying sailor on the *Matsushima* at the Yellow Sea battle of 17 Sep 1894. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/勇敢なる水兵
- **「雪の進軍」 (1895).** By the bandsman Nagai Kenshi, written in mid-February 1895 at Weihaiwei. It is in the colloquial style, rare then, and full of soldiers' grumbling. Printed by August 1895. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/雪の進軍

### 8c. The Katyusha song (1914) and the record industry

All points in the first three bullets from https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/カチューシャの唄

- **The song.** 「カチューシャの唄」 (also 「復活唱歌」). Words by Shimamura Hōgetsu (verse 1) and Sōma Gyofū (verses 2–5); music by **Nakayama Shinpei**, his first published work.
  - Hōgetsu asked for "something between a school song and a Western art song… something all Japan can sing".
  - The "lala" refrain was Nakayama's idea.
- **The play's run.** 『復活』 opened at the Imperial Theatre in March 1914 to poor houses. It then packed theatres in Osaka and Kyoto from April 1914, and reached more than 440 performances in four years.
- **The record and the sales**
  - Recorded in Kyoto by **Tōyō Chikuonki (Orient Records)** between 23 and 25 Apr 1914. Advertised in early May 1914 (日出新聞, 8 May; 大阪朝日, 11 May).
  - Commonly said to have sold **20,000+** copies, when a few thousand counted as a hit. The Osaka Mainichi of 13 Mar 1915 gave a figure of 2,000, possibly yen rather than copies (Minami Hiroshi; Nagamine Shigetoshi).
  - Sheet music sold **78,000** copies by September 1914, the best since the 「鉄道唱歌」; with cheap songbooks, 140,000–150,000 copies (Yomiuri, 13 Sep 1914).
  - Lyrics were printed in newspapers with musical notation. When they were posted in a theatre corridor, audiences crowded round to copy them and sang together.
  - Schools forbade pupils to see 『復活』. The Third Higher School banned singing the song (Yomiuri, 17 Jun 1914). A Tokyo women's college did the same (Yorozu Chōhō, 11 Aug 1914).
  - No royalties were paid. Hōgetsu played the record at his lectures on tour.
- **Earlier theatre song.** 「どん底の歌」 (from *The Lower Depths*, 1910 and 1913) had been a smaller hit. Same page.
- **The record companies**
  - The Yokohama trader F. W. Horn, with the help of Matsumoto Takeichirō of the record shop Sankōdō, founded 日米蓄音器 in October 1907.
  - **Nippon Chikuonki Shōkai (Nipponophone)** was incorporated on **1 Oct 1910** in Kawasaki, with Horn as first president. It absorbed the manufacturing company in 1912.
  - In 1912 it was sued in the Tōchūken Kumoemon case (unauthorised copies of rōkyoku records made by Sankōdō), a landmark case on record piracy decided by the Great Court of Cassation on 4 Jul 1914.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本コロムビア
- **Danjūrō and Kikugorō on record**
  - The Yomiuri of 22 Jul 1890 reports them about to record lines on Edison's phonograph, then on show at the Hanayashiki.
  - In May 1899 Danjūrō recorded a whole act of 『勧進帳』 at his house.
  - In 1923 Nippon Chikuonki was reported transferring the cylinders; their fate is unknown.
  - Source: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川團十郎_(9代目)

## 9. Fandom economics

- **Patron circles (連中, ren)**
  - Fan societies (also 見連, 組見) gave presents to actors and hung lanterns at the theatre tea-houses.
  - In Meiji, connoisseur circles such as the 六二連 and 水魚連 had authority. As their number grew, so did the abuses.
  - Source: Heibonsha in https://kotobank.jp/word/連中-662140 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/六二連-1440566
- **Gift curtains**
  - Patrons gave stage curtains (引幕). The Siebold–Bälz curtain went to the Shintomi-za. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川團十郎_(9代目)
  - Ayanosuke received back-curtains (後幕) from fans; she in turn sent curtains to her actor cousin Nakamura Shibajaku. Memoir: https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000726/files/45982_26593.html
  - At the Misaki-za's opening in June 1891, offers of curtains from patrons were declined (Yomiuri, 24 Jun 1891, as cited). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/三崎座
- **Fan letters.** One devotee sent Ayanosuke 100-odd letters in six months, written in jōruri style. Memoir: Hasegawa, same URL.
- **Portraits for sale**
  - Woodblock actor prints gave way in Meiji to small (手札) photographs of wrestlers and kabuki actors, later also called "bromides". https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ブロマイド
  - Private postcards were allowed from October 1900. The Russo-Japanese War commemorative cards of 1904–06 caused crowds and injuries at post offices. Beauty and celebrity postcards were part of the boom (Nipponica, Heibonsha). https://kotobank.jp/word/絵葉書-445975
  - Beauty contests by photograph: Ryōunkaku in 1891. The Jiji Shinpō's national contest, with 22 regional papers and 215 finalists, was judged on 29 Feb 1908; the winner was the 16-year-old 末弘ヒロ子 of Kokura. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ミス・コンテスト
- **Bromides proper**
  - The first "promide" was of Onoe Matsunosuke, in 1920.
  - When Shōchiku Kamata opened in 1921, street photographers mass-produced pictures of its actresses. Kurishima Sumiko's sold **4,000 a day**.
  - Marubell-dō was founded in Asakusa on **5 May 1921**. Postwar it published monthly sales rankings.
  - The pronunciation "pu-romaido" was invented in late Taishō to separate the product from the photographic paper.
  - Before the war, "one-star-only" women fans wiped dust off a favourite's photo in the shop window.
  - Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ブロマイド ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/マルベル堂
- **Theatre magazines**
  - 『歌舞伎新報』, February 1879 – March 1897, 1,669 issues: the first to print kabuki scripts. https://kotobank.jp/word/歌舞伎新報-46222
  - 『演芸画報』, January 1907 – 1943, 439 issues, with monthly photographs of the stage. https://kotobank.jp/word/演芸画報-38092
- **Newspapers and theatre**
  - The **Konnichi Shinbun / Miyako Shinbun** was founded on 25 Sep 1884 as Japan's first true evening paper, with Kanagaki Robun as editor, and was strong on theatre, yose and the geisha quarters. As the Miyako Shinbun from 1888–89 it carried drama reports arranged with Fukuchi Ōchi and Morita Kan'ya. Ihara Seiseien wrote its drama criticism for 40 years from 1898. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/都新聞
  - Serialised fiction grew out of the small newspapers' **続き物**: crime and scandal news written up as serials, for example 『鳥追お松の伝』 (1877–78). These were adapted for the stage within months.
  - The first newspaper serial story is dated 1875 (『岩田八十八の話』, 平仮名絵入新聞).
  - Heibonsha: a typical instalment ran 1,200–1,600 characters, for 100–300 days. Writers timed episodes to the season, for example the 『金色夜叉』 karuta scene in the New Year week.
  - Sources: https://kotobank.jp/word/新聞小説-82525 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/鳥追お松-1095616
- **Cross-promotion in practice**
  - The Geijutsuza printed the Katyusha lyrics and score in newspapers. Hōgetsu gave lectures at each stop and played the record before opening night. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/カチューシャの唄
  - Kawakami's war plays followed the news of the front. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/川上音二郎
  - The Sino-Japanese War songs were printed within months of the battles they described. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/雪の進軍

## Telling details

1. **A girl in a student's clothes, on stage in 1877.** Mokuami's 『富士額男女繁山』 (Shintomi-za, April 1877) was taken from a newspaper report of a woman arrested at Kumagaya for wearing men's clothes. Its heroine was raised as a boy, lives in Tokyo as a male live-in student (書生) to a powerful official, is unmasked, raped, becomes the official's mistress, and kills her tormentor. Nipponica notes the play's "perverse eroticism of the woman in male dress". https://kotobank.jp/word/富士額男女繁山-1585971
2. **The "man-woman" idol.** Ayanosuke grew up with a boy's crop and was called 男おんな ("man-woman"). On stage she wore a boy's and then a man's topknot. Her rival Kotosa also wore a man's topknot. Fans from Keiō and Kōgyokusha formed the first organised fan "parties". Memoir: https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000726/files/45982_26593.html ; Nipponica: "boyish style", https://kotobank.jp/word/女義太夫-456030
3. **What the fans did.** Dōsuru-ren beat time with wooden shoe-check tokens. One fan took a policeman's dismissal for his devotion; another began seppuku and his mother went to plead with Ayanosuke. A rejected suitor forged a letter in her name to test her lover. (Hasegawa, same URL.)
4. **Mercury in the teacup.** In 1885 a plot by a jealous rival's camp to put mercury in the ten-year-old's teacup closed a sold-out run. (Hasegawa.)
5. **"If she were a man."** Danjūrō IX is said to have told Takata Sanae that Kumehachi was a fine actor and "if she were a man she'd be my partner". (Hasegawa's novelised portrait: https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000726/files/45974_26599.html.) Kidō thought her Princess Yaegaki better than Danjūrō's, but her voice "thick and muddy" from years of male roles. https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000082/files/49526_42385.html
6. **The great reform that nobody used.** When the police allowed mixed casts in August 1890, Kidō says the papers printed it in ordinary small type and no major theatre hired an actress. Rumours that Kumehachi would partner Danjūrō at the Kabuki-za came to nothing. (Kidō.)
7. **Oppekepe against actor-crushes.** A verse of the Oppekepe song scolds men who take their mistresses to see kabuki actors. A woman who dotes on actors will cheat on her husband, it says; once the Diet opens, "there's no time to moon over actors — guard Japan instead". If she likes men without eyebrows, she should take a leper as a lover; if she likes men who roll their eyes, she should sleep with a raccoon-dog. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/オッペケペー節
8. **The emperor looking for strings.** During Kikugorō's puppet-style dance in 1887, the emperor kept glancing at the ceiling for the strings. Danjūrō could not eat the day before, and lost about 5.6 kg over the four days. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/天覧歌舞伎
9. **The oldest surviving Japanese film shows the great male actor as a woman.** In 『紅葉狩』 Danjūrō plays Princess Sarashina, who turns into a demon. He dropped his fan in the wind, refused a retake, and barred the film's release while he lived. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/紅葉狩_(映画)
10. **Takarazuka began in a failed swimming pool.** The pool was unheated, and men and women were not allowed to swim together. The pool became the auditorium and the changing rooms the stage. The first Momotarō, Takamine Taeko, was hired for singing "do-re-mi" instead of "hi-fu-mi". https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚ファミリーランド ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高峰妙子
11. **Names from a card game.** Takarazuka's first stage names came from the Hyakunin Isshu, the poems every family knew from the New Year card game. https://kotobank.jp/word/宝塚歌劇団-92817
12. **The Ryōunkaku vote.** The lift broke, so the owners pasted 100 geisha photographs up the staircase and let climbers vote: a popularity contest born of a mechanical failure. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/凌雲閣
13. **The masked young lady.** Mizutani Yaeko's 1921 film debut was credited to "the masked young lady" (覆面令嬢) because her Catholic girls' school would not let her name appear. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/水谷八重子_(初代)
14. **"Lowly actress."** In 1916 First Higher School students demanded that Mori Ritsuko leave her brother's sports day; the brother killed himself the next day (reported). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/森律子
15. **The onnagata dandy.** The shinpa onnagata Kitamura Rokurō smoked cigars, wore Western suits and played golf and mahjong. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/喜多村緑郎_(初代)
16. **The Katyusha mania.** A madwoman called "Katyusha" wandered the streets singing "the pain of parting" and was teased by youths (recalled by 南部僑一郎). At cinemas a woman benshi sang the song. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/立花貞二郎
17. **Kikugorō V learns English.** For the balloonist "Spencer" (January 1891) he went to Keiō for English lessons. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/尾上菊五郎_(5代目)
18. **The Hepburn amputation.** The onnagata star Tanosuke III went on performing after Dr Hepburn amputated his leg in 1867, and later lost fingers too. Fashions were sold under his name. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/澤村田之助_(3代目)
19. **Danjūrō's daughters.** Danjūrō IX let his daughters pick their own lovers. The elder became a dancer and actress, Ichikawa Suisen II. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川團十郎_(9代目) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川翠扇
20. **The panorama's prices.** The Asakusa panorama paid ¥100,000 for a second-hand Civil War panorama and sold more than 107,000 adult tickets at 10 sen in under two months. Its café served "steak" for 7 sen. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本パノラマ館
21. **"The Women's Army Marches to War."** The opera that launched Asakusa Opera (22 Jan 1917) was titled 『女軍出征』. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/浅草オペラ
22. **Ii Yōhō's name.** His stage name was said to pun on ii yōbō, "good looks". https://kotobank.jp/word/伊井蓉峰-29795
23. **Gidayū in a frock coat.** In the Rokumeikan year of 1885, Takemoto Kyōshi's company chanted gidayū in Western dress, seated on chairs at a table, to full houses. Memoir: https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000726/files/45982_26593.html
24. **One viewer at a time.** At the Kōbe showing, customers waited in an anteroom and were called one at a time to peer into the box. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/キネトスコープ
25. **The first actress.** Chitose Beiha, the first stage "actress", was a former geisha named by a Confucian scholar (Yoda Gakkai). She later went back to women's kabuki and the yose, and for a time married a politician. https://kotobank.jp/word/千歳米坡-1365680

## Uncertain / conflicting

- **Mixed-sex ban lifted 1890.** Rests mainly on Okamoto Kidō's memoir, which gives "August" of the year he is describing (1890 from context). The exact decree was not found. The same month's revision of the theatre regulations (August 1890) is in ja.wp 三崎座. Treat "August 1890, Metropolitan Police permission" as probable but not documented from the decree text.
- **Musume-gidayū "police crackdowns".** The sources consulted record the 1898 remarks of Education Minister Toyama, the 1900 Yorozu Chōhō campaign, and promoters' self-policing (barring the dressing rooms). I found no specific police order or arrest. The yose regulations (1877) and the 1891 sideshow rules are the relevant police instruments.
- **Kumehachi dates**
  - Joined Danjūrō in 1888 (ja.wp) or 1882 (en.wp).
  - Named 九女八 in 1897 (ja.wp) or 1893 (en.wp).
  - en.wp says she debuted in 1858 as "the first kabuki actress since 1629"; that claim is not in the Japanese sources.
  - Britannica's "弘化3(1848)" is wrong (Kōka 3 = 1846–47).
- **Sadayakko's marriage.** 1891, 1893 (Downer: October 1893) or 1894; the same ja.wp article contradicts itself. Her US debut date (25 May 1899) is from Downer via en.wp only.
- **Kawakami's Sakai launch.** 5 Feb 1891 (Heibonsha), February 1891 (ja.wp Kawakami) or March 1891 (ja.wp shinpa).
- **The first Sino-Japanese War play.** August 1894 (Nipponica) or September 1894, Asakusa-za (ja.wp).
- **Shintomi-za dates**
  - Nipponica's opening date 1872 (明治5) 10月13日 is lunar (= 13 Nov 1872 by my conversion); ja.wp says "1872年9月".
  - Renamed January or March 1875.
  - Bought by Shōchiku in 1909 or 1910.
  - The rebuilt theatre opened 7 Jun 1878 (ja.wp); Britannica says "77年6月類焼のため再建", which seems garbled.
- **First zangiri plays in Kyoto.** Heibonsha says "72年11月": whether this is lunar (≈ December 1872) is unstated.
- **『富士額男女繁山』 premiere.** April 1877 (Nipponica, Nichigai) or March 1877 (ja.wp table).
- **『婦系図』 premiere.** September 1908 (Nipponica, Heibonsha) or October 1908 (Nichigai title dictionary).
- **First staging of 『金色夜叉』.** March 1898 at the Ichimura-za (Nipponica). ja.wp says the Kawakami troupe, 1898. Possibly the same production; not checked.
- **Saibikan reading and Ii Yōhō's name.** さいびかん (ja.wp disambiguation) or せいびかん (Kotobank, ja.wp shinpa). The name is a pun on "good looks" (Daijisen) or a Fuji epithet chosen by Gakkai (Shinsen).
- **Chitose Beiha's death.** 1918 (Shinsen) or 1913 (Nipponica's shinpa entry).
- **Takata Minoru's death.** 1916 (ja.wp Takata) or 1918 (ja.wp shinpa).
- **Ayanosuke's arrival in Tokyo.** 1885, 1886 or 1887. Takemoto Kyōshi's arrival: 1882 or 1883.

Pathé's 1908 fiction films cast the women's-kabuki actress Nakamura Kasen, and geisha were filmed in 1899. Treat "first film actress" as a claim about intent and method, not literally the first woman on film.
- **Tejima's lanterns.** 1873 (Nipponica) or 1874 (Bazzell). Tsurubuchi Hatsuzō is attested here only by Bazzell's conference slides, which spell him "Taurubuchi", with no date. Not verified in a Japanese source.

  - Grand Theatre capacity: 3,000, 3,500 or 4,000.
  - Takamine Taeko born 1899 (ja.wp) or 1900 (Nipponica).

- **Katyusha record sales.** "20,000+" is a later claim. A 1915 newspaper figure of "2,000" (perhaps yen) suggests caution. "Japan's first pop hit record" is a common label, not a measured fact. Earlier rōkyoku records (Tōchūken Kumoemon, 1912) also sold widely.
- **Sumako's "first banned record" (1917) and "270,000 copies".** From 南葉二 (1968) via ja.wp; unverified.
- **Tokyo minor-offences code (違式詿違条例).** ja.wp gives Tokyo, Meiji 5/11/8 (lunar = 8 Dec 1872). The Shōtō Museum exhibition text, via Tokyo Art Beat, says Meiji 6. It probably refers to the national version issued to the other prefectures in 1873. The cross-dressing clause and any actor exemption: not verified from the text.
- **Danjūrō's planing of the Kabuki-za stage after Kawakami (1895).** Given in ja.wp as "said to have" (言われる): anecdote.
- **Hasegawa Shigure's portraits** (Ayanosuke, Kumehachi). Literary essays with invented dialogue. Use their numbers (100+ letters, the mercury plot, the first Keiō fans) as colourful tradition, not fact.
- **The Engeki kairyōkai's supporters.** ja.wp includes Iwasaki Yatarō (d. 1885) among its 1887 backers: an error, probably for Yanosuke.

  - The NDL page on the 1878 Shintomi-za opening, which has moved.
  - Takarazuka's first fan letters.
  - Any 1880s–90s police order specifically against the dōsuru-ren.

No NDL page was reached, because none could be found without a search. "Different source" means a page other than the one the report cites. Where only the cited page could be found, the bullet says so.

1. **Calendar anchors.** The report gives the lunar 1st month of Meiji 5 as beginning on 9 Feb 1872 (it flagged this as unchecked), Meiji 5/10/13 as 13 Nov 1872 and Meiji 5/11/8 as 8 Dec 1872. **CONFIRMED.**

   - Confirmed: en.wp *Morita Kan'ya XII* says "The grand reopening ceremony took place on 7 June 1878" and mentions gas lighting and chairs. Heibonsha (in Kotobank) gives "78年6月", with actors in 燕尾服 and staff in フロック・コート.

## Additional facts found while verifying

- **A woman on the Kabuki-za stage in 1895 (memoir).** Kidō writes that in spring 1895 Kumehachi joined Kawakami Otojirō's shosei troupe at the Ichimura-za. Kawakami refused to take her young pupil Katsura, formerly of the Misaki-za, because she was a young woman in an all-male dressing room. He gave way after a patron's wife cut off her own marumage hair and threw it in front of him. Kumehachi left first. Katsura left "after this run", meaning the troupe's run at the Kabuki-za in May 1895. Kawakami otherwise used only onnagata until he married Sadayakko. This complicates the claim that no theatre hired actresses after 1890. https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000082/files/49526_42385.html
- **The Rokumeikan in 1885.** ja.wp 抜刀隊 says it premiered in 1885 「鹿鳴館（元の日比谷の華族会館）」, meaning the building later used by the Peers' Club. ja.wp 鹿鳴館 says the building passed to the Fifteenth Bank in 1889 and was lent to the 華族会館 only in 1890.
