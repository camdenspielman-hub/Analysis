## 1. Bakumatsu women

### Matsuo Taseko 松尾多勢子 (1811–1894)
- Born 文化8年5月25日 (15 July 1811) in Yamamoto village, Ina district, Shinano. She was the eldest daughter of the wealthy farmer 竹村常盈. Died 明治27年 (1894) 6月10日. Her name was originally (お)多勢; the "‑子" was added in Meiji, following the new custom for women's names. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/松尾多勢子
- At 19 she married 松尾佐次右衛門淳斎 of Tomono village, a sake-brewing headman family. With him she studied waka and Hirata-school kokugaku. They had 10 children, of whom 3 sons and 4 daughters were raised. WP (above). Nipponica says the family business was silk reeling. https://kotobank.jp/word/松尾多勢子-136470
- In 文久2年 (1862) she went to Kyoto alone with her sick husband's permission. WP gives her age as 51; Nipponica and 日本人名大辞典 say 52 (counted in kazoe). Britannica gives the month as 8月: 文久2年8月1日 = 25 Aug 1862 [NAOJ]. She frequented the Shirakawa and Ōhara court houses and mixed with Kusaka Genzui, Shinagawa Yajirō and Fujimoto Tesseki. She became known as a curiosity: an old country woman who wrote poems. WP; KB (above).
- She was implicated in the **Ashikaga statues incident** 足利三代木像梟首事件. On the night of 文久3年2月22日 (9 April 1863), the heads of the wooden statues of the shoguns Takauji, Yoshiakira and Yoshimitsu were taken from Tōjiin and exposed at the Sanjō riverbed with a placard naming them traitors to the Southern Court. This was read as a veiled call to overthrow the shogunate. Taseko fled to the Chōshū mansion and then home. In the Ina valley she sheltered Tenchūgumi and Mito Tengu-tō men, including Tsunoda Tadayuki and Sagara Sōzō. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/足利三代木像梟首事件 ; WP 松尾多勢子
- In 1868 she served in the Iwakura Tomomi household, in charge of the children's education and the housekeeping. She was called "the woman councillor of the Iwakura house" (岩倉家の女参事). She returned home in 1869. In 1903 she was posthumously given senior fifth rank (正五位). Shimazaki Tōson portrayed her in *Yoake mae*. WP; KB 日本人名大辞典.

### Nomura Bōtō-ni 野村望東尼 (1806–1867)
- Born 文化3年9月6日 (17 Oct 1806) in Fukuoka, daughter of the Fukuoka retainer 浦野勝幸. Died 慶応3年11月6日 (1 Dec 1867) at Mitajiri (Hōfu). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/野村望東尼
- She married at 17 (1822) a man 20 years older and was divorced within about six months. In 1829 she married 野村貞貫. In 1845 she retired to the **Hirao mountain retreat** (平尾山荘). She became a nun on her husband's death in 1859. From 1861–62 she spent time in Osaka and Kyoto and heard about the Teradaya incident. She then sheltered loyalists at Hirao, among them Gesshō, **Takasugi Shinsaku**, Hirano Kuniomi and others. WP.
- During the 乙丑の獄 purge she was exiled in 10月 of 慶応元年 (1865) to **Himeshima island**. In 9月 of 慶応2年 (1866) men acting on Takasugi's orders broke her out. She nursed Takasugi on his deathbed. He wrote 「おもしろき 事もなき世に おもしろく」 and she completed the poem with 「住みなすものは 心なりけり」. She was posthumously given 正五位 in 1891. WP.

### Kusumoto Ine 楠本イネ (1827–1903)
- Born 文政10年5月6日 (31 May 1827) in Nagasaki, daughter of **Philipp Franz von Siebold** and 瀧 (お滝). Died 26 Aug 1903 in Azabu, Tokyo. She was the first Japanese woman trained in Western obstetrics. She studied with 二宮敬作, 石井宗謙 (obstetrics), Murata Zōroku (Ōmura Masujirō; Dutch), and from 1859 with **Pompe van Meerdervoort**, then Bauduin. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/楠本イネ ; https://kotobank.jp/word/楠本いね-1071673
- Her daughter by Ishii Sōken, born out of wedlock, was named **タダ ("Tada", "free")**, meaning "given by heaven for nothing". Date Munenari later renamed her 高子. WP.
- In 1871 she opened a practice in Tsukiji. On Fukuzawa's recommendation she became a court appointee (宮内省御用掛) and attended the confinement of the Meiji Emperor's consort 葉室光子, who died after a stillbirth. WP.
- When the medical licensing exam opened to women in 1884 she did not sit it. She was then 57. Uchigami Yukio argues she did not need to, since the exam was for new practitioners. WP.
- The famous nickname **"Oranda Oine" is anachronistic.** Uchigami traces it to the 1970 TBS drama 『オランダおいね』; no source from her lifetime uses it. WP.

### Chiba Sana 千葉さな子 / 佐那 (1838?–1896)
- Born 天保9年3月6日 (31 March 1838) according to WP; 日本人名大辞典 has 1839. WP calls her the 二女 and 日本人名大辞典 the 長女 of **Chiba Sadakichi**, master of the Okechō Chiba dōjō of the Hokushin Ittō-ryū. She held full licence (免許皆伝) in the short sword and was a naginata teacher. Died 15 Oct 1896. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/千葉さな子 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/千葉佐那-1091338
- The Uwajima record 『稿本藍山公記』 says that in 1856, aged 19, she taught swordsmanship to the Date princesses. She beat the future daimyo **Date Munetoshi** (then 27) in a bout. The record says she was the most beautiful of the women in either Date mansion. WP.
- Sakamoto Ryōma described her to his sister Otome as follows: she rode well, was strong with the sword, could use the naginata, was stronger than an ordinary man, and was a bit better-looking than Hirai Kao. WP.
- From 9/1882 to 2/1888 she was **dormitory supervisor (舎監)** at the Gakushūin women's section (華族女学校). She then ran a moxibustion clinic in Senju. Her grave stone in Kōfu reads **「坂本龍馬室」** ("wife of Sakamoto Ryōma"). In 2010 a Meiji newspaper item was found reporting that she married the former Tottori samurai 山口菊次郎 in 1874 and later divorced him. WP.
- She appears in 坂崎紫瀾『汗血千里駒』 (1883; see "Telling details"). WP.

### Nakano Takeko 中野竹子 and the Aizu women's unit 婦女隊 / 娘子隊 / 娘子軍 (1868)
- **Siege of Aizu-Wakamatsu castle**: 慶応4年8月23日 – 明治元年9月22日 = 8 Oct – 6 Nov 1868. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/会津城籠城戦
- **Takeko**: born in the Aizu Edo mansion, eldest daughter of 中野平内. She is given as born 嘉永3年3月 (April 1850) or 1847. She held a near-full licence in the naginata under 黒河内兼規. She was engaged, but returned to Aizu when the war began. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/中野竹子
- **Origin of the unit.** The group was missing the castle when the gates shut on 8/23: they had gone to Aizu-Bange chasing a false report that Princess Teru was there. At first the karō **萱野権兵衛** refused to let them fight, on the ground that "people will mock Aizu for having been reduced to sending women to fight". They threatened suicide, and **町野主水** attached them to the 衝鋒隊 of 古屋佐久左衛門 for the next day. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/婦女隊
- **Members, with ages, from WP 婦女隊**: 中野こう子 (mother, 44); Takeko (20 or 22); 中野優子 (sister, 16); 神保雪子 (widow of Jinbo Shūri, 23); 依田まき子 (35); 依田菊子 (18), and others. A surviving member said that "twenty-odd" had agreed to turn out if needed. WP 婦女隊.
- **Appearance**: hair **cut short to above the shoulders, "in male guise" (男姿)**, white habutae headbands, women's kimono worn with hakama. They carried long and short swords and naginata. WP 婦女隊.
- **Battle at Namidabashi**, 8月25日 = 10 Oct 1868 [NAOJ; also WP]. The Ōgaki and Chōshū troops shouted "Take them alive!" (生け捕れ). Takeko was shot in the forehead (another account says the chest). Her sister Yūko, or a peasant soldier in other accounts, cut off her head so the enemy would not take it. The account by 水島 (依田) 菊子: 「当るを幸ひ斬り捲りましたが、竹子さんは遂に額に弾丸を受けて斃れました…漸くにして其首を介錯されました」. Jinbo Yukiko died the same day; one account says she was captured and killed herself. The unit was then disbanded by Kayano with the words "today's fighting is a contest of cannon and rifles". The women entered the castle to support the siege. WP 婦女隊; WP 中野竹子.
- **The night before**: Kōko and Takeko discussed killing the beautiful 16-year-old Yūko in her sleep so she could not be captured and dishonoured. The Yoda sisters stopped them (Mizushima Kikuko's account). WP 中野竹子.
- **Death poem** tied to her naginata: 「もののふの猛き心にくらぶれば数にも入らぬ我が身ながらも」. The naginata is now at Hōkaiji, Aizu-Bange: shaft 5 尺 3 寸, blade 1 尺 5 寸, tip chipped. WP 中野竹子.
- **Name.** The term 娘子軍 may have been coined by Hiraishi Benzō (『会津戊辰戦史』). WP says the participants themselves wrote of "娘子軍" in later testimony. WP 婦女隊.

### Yamamoto (Niijima) Yae 新島八重 (1845–1932)
- Born 弘化2年11月3日 (1 Dec 1845), daughter of the Aizu gunnery instructor 山本権八 (stipend 22 koku 4-nin buchi). Died 14 June 1932. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/新島八重
- In 1868 she held that the fighting should be done with guns, so she did not join the naginata unit. She **cut her hair and dressed as a man (断髪・男装)** and fought with a **Spencer rifle**. WP adds that the domain had only that one Spencer, with the "hundred rounds" she brought into the castle, so in practice she mostly used Geweer muskets. It is said, without proof, that she wounded 大山巌 (Ōyama Iwao), then a Satsuma artillery commander. WP. Nipponica: 「男装して7連発銃をもって新政府軍に応戦するなどの逸話」. https://kotobank.jp/word/新島八重-679265
- Her first husband 川崎尚之助 was taken prisoner and they were separated. The formal divorce was not until 明治4年 (1871) 12月. In 1871 she went to Kyoto to her brother 山本覚馬. She worked at the Kyoto **Nyokōba** 女紅場, a girls' craft school, and was dismissed after her engagement to **Niijima Jō** (Oct 1875). They married on **3 Jan 1876**. WP.
- Tokutomi Sohō called her a **鵺 (chimera)** for making her husband defer to her and boarding the rickshaw first. Niijima wrote to an American friend that "her way of life is handsome". She was the first woman outside the imperial family to receive a state decoration from the government (勲七等/勲六等宝冠章). WP.

### Ee ja nai ka ええじゃないか (1867)
- Mass dancing, with costumes, after amulets reportedly "fell from heaven", across the Kinki, Shikoku and Tōkai regions. WP dates it from 慶応3年8月 to 12月 and notes it coincided with the return of power (大政奉還) and the Restoration decree. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ええじゃないか
- **First occurrence.** The 国史大辞典 entry gives 慶応3年8月4日 at Goyu post-station in Mikawa as the first recorded case: **1 Sept 1867** [NAOJ]. 『新修名古屋市史』 gives an earlier case: Ise Outer Shrine amulets falling at Ōnishi village, Atsumi district (Toyohashi) on 7月14日 = **13 Aug 1867** [NAOJ], and in Nagoya on 8月28日 = 25 Sept 1867 [NAOJ]. Both are as quoted by the Nagoya City Library reference report: https://www.library.city.nagoya.jp/img/reference/chosahoukoku_29.pdf. Tamura Sadao (1987) puts the start at 7/14 near Muro Hachiman, Toyohashi. WP.
- 『岩倉公実記』 records Kyoto crowds crying 「ヨイジャナイカ、エイジャナイカ、エイジャーナカト」. It says the movement began in late 8th month and **stopped on 12月9日, the day of the Restoration decree** (3 Jan 1868). WP.
- **Sample lyrics** (WP): 「今年は世直りええじゃないか」 (Awaji); 「日本国の世直りはええじゃないか、豊年踊はお目出たい」 (Awa). The sexual 「御かげでよいじゃないか、何んでもよいじゃないか、おまこに紙張れ、へげたら又はれ、よいじゃないか」 (Awaji). The political 「長州がのぼた、物が安うなる、えじゃないか」 (Nishinomiya).
- **Eyewitness**: Ernest Satow in Osaka, about 12–13 Dec 1867: "Crowds of people in holiday garb, dancing and singing 'Ii ja nai ka, ii ja nai ka'… The dresses worn were chiefly red crape, a few blue and purple. Many of the dancers carried red lanterns on their heads. The pretext… was a shower of pieces of paper, bearing the names of the two gods of Isé." *A Diplomat in Japan*, ch. XXIII. https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/43541/pg43541.txt. Fukuchi Gen'ichirō saw it in Osaka on 慶応3年11月29日 (24 Dec 1867 [NAOJ]). He recorded a rumour that the Kyoto faction had staged the amulet showers to stir up the population. WP.
- **Cross-dressing**: en.wikipedia states that "youth groups organized mass dances which included cross-dressing, elaborate costumes, or not wearing clothes at all" (no footnote). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ee_ja_nai_ka. ja.WP only says 民衆が仮装するなどして. **I could not verify the cross-dressing claim in a Japanese primary source. See the last section.**
- Historiography: Hani Gorō saw ee ja nai ka as a smokescreen arranged by Saigō for the Restoration coup; Inoue Kiyoshi disagreed. WP.

## 2. The vocabulary of revolution: 維新 vs 革命

### Ishin 維新
- **Origin**: *Shijing* 大雅・文王: 「周雖旧邦、其命維新」, "Zhou is an old state, but its Mandate is new". *Shujing* 胤征 has 「舊染汚俗咸與惟新」. The earliest Japanese use is in the *Nihon shoki* under Taika 2 (646): 「天人合應厥政惟新」. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/維新 ; KB 維新 https://kotobank.jp/word/維新-432403
- **Bakumatsu use**: Fujita Tōko's diary 『庚寅日録』, 天保元年 (1830) 4月21日: 「中興維新之責実在足下」. Yokoi Shōnan's letter of 嘉永3年 (1850) to Fujita: 「近年来尊藩御維新之御政事」. Even a shogunal letter of 嘉永6年10月15日 (1853) to the Russian chancellor has 「百度維新」. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/明治維新 (section 名称)
- **Why not 革命.** According to Karube Tadashi, as cited by WP, Tamamatsu Misao advised Iwakura (1866 or 1867) to follow 「萬機の維新」. Because power passed by returning the office of shogun to the emperor, and was not a change of dynasty (易姓革命), the word 維新 was chosen rather than 革命, and the new government kept it. WP 明治維新.
- **Official vocabulary 1867–72**: The Restoration decree of 慶応3年12月9日 (3 Jan 1868) says 「百事御一新」. Other phrases: 「政体復古」 (Higashikuze to the foreign ministers, 慶応4年2月14日 = 7 Mar 1868); 「天下一新」 (28 Feb lunar); 「朝政一新」 (the 宸翰 of 3/14 lunar). Kido wrote 「大政一新」 in early 1869, and Iwakura wrote 「大政維新」. The first official 「百度維新」 is the 大教宣布の詔 of 明治3年1月3日 (3 Feb 1870). The people said **御一新 (go-isshin)**. WP 明治維新; KB 維新 (Heibonsha).
- Osatake Takeki held that 「明治維新」 as a set phrase appeared only a few years before the 1890 constitution; before that it was 「王政維新」. WP 明治維新.
- Within the Restoration itself, Chōshū in 1866 formed a unit of outcaste soldiers called **「維新団」**, and in 1864 one called 「一新組」. WP 明治維新.

### Kakumei 革命: classical meaning
- From the *Yijing* 革卦: 「湯武革命、順乎天而應乎人」. It means "the Mandate is changed": a change of dynasty (易姓革命). KB 革命 (大辞泉, NKD, Nipponica) https://kotobank.jp/word/革命-43816
- **辛酉革命 (shin'yū kakumei)**: a weft-text (讖緯) prophecy that a 辛酉 year, once every 60 years, brings a change of Mandate. It formed a set with 甲子革令 and 戊辰革運. Miyoshi Kiyoyuki's 「革命勘文」 led to the **Engi era change of 901**. From then on, era names were changed in 辛酉 and 甲子 years, "with few exceptions", up to the end of the Edo period. Nipponica gives the last 辛酉 change as **文久元年 (1861)**; Yamakawa gives the last 甲子 change as **元治元年 (1864)**. Jimmu's accession in 660 BC (a 辛酉 year) was itself computed on this theory. https://kotobank.jp/word/辛酉革命-82671
- NKD's earliest use of the old sense: 三善清行's letter in 『本朝文粋』 (c. 1060): 「天道革命之運、君臣尅賊之期」. A 1477 gloss reads: 「嘉吉元年辛酉は革命之年ぞ」. KB 革命.
- WP notes that in Edo thought (Yamazaki Ansai; Fujita Tōko's 『弘道館記述義』) the *absence* of 易姓革命 in Japan was treated as proof of Japanese uniqueness. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/革命

### Kakumei = "revolution": when and by whom
- **First attestation in the modern sense (NKD)**: Fukuzawa Yukichi, 『西洋事情』 外編: 「兵乱に由て俄に政府の革まるを革命と云ひ」. NKD dates the whole work 1866–70; 外編 is the 1868 volume (I did not separately confirm the volume's date). NKD's usage note says the Meiji 革命 should be seen as reborn as a translation of *revolution*, and that **Fukuzawa is thought to have chosen it**. KB 革命 (NKD 語誌).
- The JapanKnowledge editors' blog paraphrases the same NKD note: Fukuzawa chose the word, but it was **spread by Nakae Chōmin and the Jiyūtō people's-rights activists**. https://japanknowledge.com/articles/blognihongo/entry.html?entryid=368
- NKD also notes that Edo Confucians had debated whether the Mencian "Tang and Wu" deposition counted as "armed change of rulers", and that this prepared the Meiji sense. KB 革命.
- **"French Revolution" in Meiji book titles**: Mignet's history, translated by **河津祐之, 『仏国革命史』, 1876** (加納久宣; reissued 1878). Also 高木秋浦 (為鎮) 編 『通俗仏国革命史』, 1887. NDL catalogue: https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/books/R100000002-I000000433031 ; https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/books/R100000002-I000000433006
- **Nakae Chōmin**: he sailed with the Iwakura Mission to study in France (1871) and came home in 1874. He was chief editor of 『東洋自由新聞』 (March 1881). He published 『民約訳解』, a kanbun translation of Rousseau's *Social Contract*, in 1882 (仏学塾出版局, vol. 1, Oct 1882), and 『三酔人経綸問答』 in May 1887. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/中江兆民 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/三酔人経綸問答
- **Right of revolution in a draft constitution**: Ueki Emori's 『東洋大日本国国憲按』 (1881, for the 国会期成同盟). Arts. 70–72 let the people disobey a government that violates the constitution, resist oppression by arms, and "overthrow the government and establish a new one". WP calls this 抵抗権・革命権, but those are modern labels. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東洋大日本国国憲按
- **Calling the Restoration a "revolution"**: Lev Mechnikov (in Japan from 1874) wrote in 1881 that it was "the most complete and radical revolution known to history". Takekoshi Yosaburō's 『新日本史』 (1891–92) called it an 「乱世的革命（アナルキカル・レボリューション）」. Tokutomi Sohō and Takekoshi used 「維新革命」. Kita Ikki (1906) wrote 「維新革命の民主主義」 and said the people's-rights movement was its real continuation. The movement itself called itself "the second Restoration" (第二の維新). WP 明治維新.
- On the English side, W. E. Griffis's *The Mikado's Empire* (1876) uses both "restoration" and "a three-fold political revolution". WP 明治維新.

### Takebashi Girls' School 東京女学校 (1872) — girls in men's hakama
- Announced 明治4年12月 (12th lunar month, from 10 Jan 1872 [NAOJ]). Opened as 官立女学校 in March 1872 (lunar 2月) and renamed 東京女学校 in late 1872. It took girls aged 7–15 "from kazoku to commoners". English was taught by three American women. **At its opening, the Council of State ordered girls to wear the samurai men's hakama (士分男子の袴)**, the starting point of schoolgirl dress. Closed on 19 Feb 1877 for lack of money after the Satsuma Rebellion. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東京女学校
- NKD's earliest 「女学生」 is from 『新聞雑誌』 no. 40, April 1872: 「冀は邦内四方の女学生能々注意し勉励ありたし」. https://kotobank.jp/word/女学生-533941

### Tokyo Women's Normal School 東京女子師範学校 (1875)
- Proposed after David Murray's report of Nov 1873. 田中不二麿 submitted the request on 4 Jan 1874; it was approved on 20 Jan; Kido Takayoshi, as education minister, announced it on **13 Mar 1874**. The entrance exam on **20 Oct 1875** took 71 students. Nakamura Masanao became principal on 18 Nov. **The opening ceremony, with the Empress present, was on 29 Nov 1875.** Pupils were aged 14–20; the course was five years. The kindergarten opened in 1876. A higher girls' school was attached in July 1882, the first 高等女学校. From Oct 1886 students were required to wear Western dress. It became the Women's Higher Normal School on 24 Mar 1890. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東京女子高等師範学校 ; Yamakawa: https://kotobank.jp/word/東京女子師範学校-1375726

### The five girls of the Iwakura Mission (1871)
- The mission sailed on 明治4年11月12日 = **23 Dec 1871** on the Pacific Mail steamer *America*, reached San Francisco on 15 Jan 1872, and returned on 13 Sept 1873. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/岩倉使節団
- The scheme came from Kuroda Kiyotaka of the Hokkaido Colonisation Office (開拓使). Terms: ten years, all costs paid **plus $800 a year**. Takahashi Yūko: in 1871 this matched a senior official's salary. All five applicants were accepted; there was a second call because few applied. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/津田梅子
- **The five**, with ages in full years at departure per the Tsuda article: 上田悌子 (Ueda Tei, 14, daughter of the bakushin 上田畯); 吉益亮子 (Yoshimasu Ryō, 14); 山川捨松 (Yamakawa Sutematsu, 11, Aizu karō family); 永井繁子 (Nagai Shige, listed as 8); 津田梅子 (Tsuda Ume, 6). WP 津田梅子. In the Chicago photo of Feb 1872 their kazoe ages are 10, 16, 16, 9, 12 (Shige, Tei, Ryō, Ume, Sutematsu). WP 大山捨松.
- Yoshimasu Ryō developed eye trouble from the snow on the transcontinental crossing (they were snowbound 18 days at Salt Lake). She and Ueda went home in 1872. Ryō later taught English and opened a private English school for women in Kyōbashi in 1885. She died of cholera in autumn 1886, aged 30. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/吉益亮子
- **Tsuda Umeko**: born 元治元年12月3日 = **31 Dec 1864**. Lived with Charles Lanman in Georgetown; returned **21 Nov 1882** (WP Tsuda; WP Sutematsu says 22 Nov). She had forgotten Japanese. Taught at the Peeresses' School (華族女学校) from Sept 1885 at ¥420 a year. Studied biology at **Bryn Mawr 1889–92** (frog embryology, with T. H. Morgan). Founded **女子英学塾 (Joshi Eigaku Juku)**, which opened on **14 Sept 1900 with 10 students** in Kōjimachi; 150 students by 1908. The Philadelphia Committee sent $1,500 in March 1900. WP 津田梅子; Yamakawa https://kotobank.jp/word/女子英学塾-1341260
- **Sutematsu (Ōyama Sutematsu)**: born 安政7年2月24日 = **16 Mar 1860**; childhood name さき. At age 8 in kazoe she carried ammunition inside besieged Aizu castle; the women smothered incoming fire-shells with wet futons (焼玉押さえ). Her mother renamed her **捨松**: "I have thrown you away (捨), but I will wait (松) for your return." Graduated from **Vassar on 14 June 1882, magna cum laude**, the first Japanese woman with a bachelor's degree. Then took two months of nursing at New Haven hospital. Married **Ōyama Iwao**, the Satsuma general who had shelled Aizu, on **8 Nov 1883**; they could talk only in French at first. Reception at the **Rokumeikan**, which had opened on **28 Nov 1883** with 1,200 guests. She was called **「鹿鳴館の花」** (WP, uncited). She organised Japan's first charity bazaar, **12–14 June 1884**. WP gives its proceeds as ¥16,000, from 秋山ひさ 1985; flagged below. It funded the first nursing school, Oct 1886. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/大山捨松 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/鹿鳴館
- **Nagai (Uryū) Shigeko**: born 文久元年3月20日 (29 Apr 1861) per 生田澄江; her brother Masuda Takashi gave 5/7. Graduated from Vassar's music course in June 1881. From 2 Mar 1882 she taught at the 音楽取調掛, **"Japan's first pianist"**. She married naval officer Uryū Sotokichi in a love match. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/瓜生繁子

### Maria Luz incident and the 1872 emancipation edict
- On 明治5年6月5日 (**10 July 1872**) the Peruvian ship *María Luz* (Macao → Peru) put into Yokohama with **231 Chinese coolies**. One, 木麗, escaped to HMS *Iron Duke*. Foreign minister Soejima ordered Kanagawa 権令 **大江卓** to act on 7/1 (4 Aug). The ship was held from 7/4 (7 Aug). Judgment on 7/27 (**30 Aug 1872**): sailing allowed only if the Chinese were freed. A second suit called the labour contracts slave contracts and void. The Chinese left for China on 9/13 (15 Oct). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/マリア・ルス号事件
- The captain's counsel, **F. V. Dickins**, argued that Japan itself allowed traffic in persons, citing courtesans' indenture contracts. Peru repeated the point ("Japan's geisha and prostitutes are slaves like coolies"). Alexander II's arbitration in St Petersburg (Enomoto Takeaki for Japan) found for Japan in **June 1875**. WP.
- **Geishōgi kaihō rei 芸娼妓解放令**: 太政官布告第295号, 明治5年10月2日 = **2 Nov 1872**. Text: 「娼妓藝妓等年季奉公人一切解放可致右ニ付テノ貸借訴訟總テ不取上候事」. It also limited apprenticeships to 7 years and ordinary service to 1 year. https://ja.wikisource.org/wiki/人身賣買ヲ禁シ諸奉公人年限ヲ定メ藝娼妓ヲ解放シ之ニ付テノ貸借訴訟ハ取上ケス ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/芸娼妓解放令
- **Justice Ministry notice 司法省達第22号**, 10月9日 = **9 Nov 1872**: 「娼妓芸妓ハ人身ノ権利ヲ失フ者ニテ牛馬ニ異ナラス」, so debts could not be claimed from them. Hence the nickname **「牛馬切りほどき令」**, the "untie-the-cattle order". Heibonsha (井手文子) and Nipponica in https://kotobank.jp/word/娼妓解放令-78983. The Justice Minister was Etō Shinpei (Mypedia). Tokyo prefectural orders 704 and 707 of Oct 1872 immediately allowed brothel work again if "by the woman's own wish". KB Heibonsha.

### Licensed prostitution after 1872
- Brothels were renamed **貸座敷 (kashi-zashiki, "rental rooms")**. Rules for kashi-zashiki and for prostitutes (貸座敷渡世規則・娼妓渡世規則) followed in **Dec 1873**. In Tokyo from Apr 1882, a woman needed parents or nearest kin to apply for her. **1889**: minimum age 16. **Dec 1891**: daughters of shizoku allowed to register (they had been excluded). **May 1900**: minimum age raised to 18. **Oct 1900**: the national **娼妓取締規則 (Home Ministry Ordinance No. 44)**, 16 articles. It recognised **free cessation (自由廃業)**: in Tokyo **429 women** quit before the year's end. Courts in 1899–1900 repeatedly ruled for prostitutes against brothel-keepers. **Gunma** was the first prefecture whose assembly voted to abolish licensed prostitution. The Christian abolitionist **Kakuseikai 廓清会** was founded in **July 1911**. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/近代公娼制
- **Conflict**: Heibonsha's old edition says the 1900 rules "closed the road" to free cessation (自由廃業の道を閉ざした) while setting the age at 18 (formerly 15–16). WP says they allowed it. https://kotobank.jp/word/娼妓取締規則-1338749. NKD's first 「自由廃業」: 時事新報, 10 Sept 1900, mentioning the Salvation Army. https://kotobank.jp/word/自由廃業-527504

### Concubines and the early criminal codes
- **新律綱領** (明治3年12月20日 = 9 Feb 1871) made **wife and concubine (妾) both relatives of the second degree**: the concubine was officially recognised. Its 闘殴律 made a wife who struck her husband and caused serious illness liable to death, while "the reverse was no crime" (WP's summary). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/新律綱領 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/妾
- The **Penal Code of 17 July 1880** (in force 1 Jan 1882) dropped the 妾 provisions. The Home Ministry ruled that concubines already registered stayed as they were. The word 妾 disappeared from family registers with the 1898 Family Registration Law. WP 妾.

## 4. Women's labour

### Tomioka silk mill 富岡製糸場 (1872)
- Recruitment notice 明治5年2月12日 = **20 Mar 1872**. Rumours that **"the Westerners will drink your blood"** (red wine) kept applicants away. Manager **Odaka Atsutada** enrolled his own daughter **勇 (Yū)** as the first worker. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/富岡製糸場
- Began work on 明治5年10月4日 = **4 Nov 1872**, with only about 210 women running half the 300 basins. There were 404 workers by January 1873, "mainly daughters of former samurai", and 556 in April 1873, including Wada (Yokota) Ei. Conditions in the government period: Sunday rest, 10 days' holiday at New Year and in summer, about 8 hours a day, board, lodging and medical care free, uniform supplied. Grades were 1–3 and ungraded at first, then 8 grades from 1873. Silk reeled by first-grade women including Odaka Yū won a "second-class progress medal" at the **Vienna Exposition, May 1873**. By the Mitsui period (1893–) daily hours were 11 h 55 m in June and 8 h 55 m in December. Sold to Mitsui on 10 Sept 1893 for **¥121,460**. WP. Four French forewomen came with Brunat; one, **Marie Charet**, was 19. WP.
- **Wada Ei 和田英 (née Yokota)**: born 安政4年8月21日 = **8 Oct 1857**, Matsushiro; died 26 Sept 1929. She entered Tomioka in 1873 with 15 others from Matsushiro and became a first-grade reeler. In 1874 she helped found **Rokkōsha** 六工社, the first private machine filature. Married Wada Morishige (an army lieutenant) in 1880. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/和田英 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/和田英-1121608
- **Her own memoir (『富岡日記』)**, full text: https://cruel.org/books/tomioka/tomioka.html
  - The prefecture ordered each ward to send women aged **13 to 25**. People saw it as offering a human sacrifice (人身御供) and spoke of girls having their blood taken and their fat pressed out (「血をとられるのあぶらをしぼられる」). Neighbours said the ward head's refusal to send his own daughter "proved" it, so her father sent her. Her grandfather told her: 「たとい女子たりとも、天下の御為になることなら参るが宜しい」.
  - **Her travelling clothes**: her father's **black wool narrow-sleeved coat from the 1868 war**, made for a man weighing 20 kan, worn unaltered. Over it a **"Yoshitsune hakama"** of damask edged with purple crepe, a red Scotch knitted undershirt, and a haori recut from a Sanada-crested uchikake. She said she would look like a cartoon today. On the day she entered the mill she lost her nerve and changed into an ordinary kimono.
  - Room: five girls in a 6-mat room. Work began at the first whistle; nobody could step outside the dormitory door before it.
  - **Visit of the Empress Dowager and Empress** ("June, I think", 1873): the workers were issued navy kasuri dress with **Kokura red-striped hakama**. The day before, the girls laughed at the court ladies' huge chignons and white make-up and were warned they would be punished if they laughed again. Ei "thought them nothing other than gods". The mill had "over six hundred" women, though she also says 300.
- Ei's memoir was composed **1905** (WP), **1907–1913** (Nipponica), or completed **1913** (20世紀日本人名事典): conflicting.

### Amemiya silk-mill strike 雨宮製糸争議, Kōfu (June 1886)
- In Feb 1886 the Yamanashi raw-silk merchants' and reelers' guild adopted rules binding women workers to their factory (no moving between mills) and lengthening hours. At the Amemiya mill hours went from **14 to 14½**. On **14 June 1886** more than 100 women walked out and **shut themselves in a nearby temple**. The strike ended on **16 June** with the guild rules relaxed. Britannica gives about 200 (or 115) strikers; Nipponica and Yamakawa give "over 100". Similar strikes followed in other Kōfu mills. It is called **Japan's first factory strike**. https://kotobank.jp/word/雨宮製糸争議-27432 ; Nipponica in https://kotobank.jp/word/女性運動-1175556

### Mill-girl numbers and wages (1890s–1912)
- **Silk reeling: women workers** (全国製糸工場調査). 1895: **172,901** (of whom 124,441 in machine filatures); 1899: **175,906** (117,861); 1904: 229,802 (126,077); 1907: 190,802 (159,460); **1910: 221,770** (191,855). Caveat: the 1895, 1899 and 1904 figures count reeling women only; from 1907 they include re-reeling and other women. 工場統計表, all silk operatives: **1909: 191,561**; 1914: 224,287. 農商務統計表, all silk operatives: 1900: 119,358. Hitotsubashi LTES Textiles Table 58 (data file LTES_J11-58.xlsx): https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2019589
- **Cotton spinning: women's labour input** in thousand person-days, total of the groups: 1890: 2,762; 1895: 10,070; **1900: 16,117**; 1905: 21,064; **1910: 27,504**; 1912: 30,816. These are person-days, not head-counts. LTES Table 23: https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2019554. Cotton spinning employed over 50,000 workers by 1897 and **16% of all factory workers (factories of 10+) in 1900** (Heibonsha). https://kotobank.jp/word/紡績業-869003
- **Wages**: silk women's daily wage (yen/day): 1885 **0.11**; 1890 0.12; 1895 0.13; **1900 0.20**; 1905 0.22; **1910 0.31**; 1912 0.31. LTES Table 59: https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2019590. Cotton-spinning women's average daily wage: 1890 0.08; 1895 0.11; **1900 0.19**; 1905 0.24; **1910 0.30**; 1912 0.32. LTES Table 41: https://d-repo.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/records/2019572
- Britannica: women factory labour emerged in textiles around 1890. https://kotobank.jp/word/女子労働-80422. WP 働く女性 states that around 1890 about 90% of factory workers in textiles were women. Mypedia: before about 1877 mill girls were mostly samurai daughters, afterwards mostly daughters of poor farmers. https://kotobank.jp/word/女工-534481

### Shokkō jijō and the Factory Act
- **『職工事情』**, 5 vols., Ministry of Agriculture and Commerce, **March 1903**. The survey office was set up in 1900 under 窪田静太郎, with 横山源之助 among the investigators. It records **recruitment amounting to abduction, and 15+ hour days** (Mypedia). Heibonsha's 「生糸」 entry cites it: Suwa hours in 1901 were 「決シテ十三四時間ヲ降ルコトナク長キハ十七八時間ニ達セル」. https://kotobank.jp/word/職工事情-80501
- **Factory Act 工場法**: Law No. 46, promulgated **29 Mar 1911**, in force **1 Sept 1916**. It applied to factories with **15+ workers** or dangerous trades. It banned employing children **under 12** (10+ allowed by permit or if already employed). For **women and those under 15**: maximum **12 hours a day** and **no night work 10 p.m.–4 a.m.** But two-shift day-and-night working was allowed for **15 years** after enforcement, at the textile industry's demand. An earlier 1909 bill was withdrawn on 26 Feb 1910 because of opposition to banning women's night work. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/工場法_(日本). Nipponica gives the protected age as "under 16"; that is the 1923 amendment's figure. https://kotobank.jp/word/工場法-62333

## 5. Women in the Freedom and People's Rights movement

### Kusunose Kita 楠瀬喜多, the "people's-rights granny" (民権ばあさん)
- Born 天保7年9月9日 = **18 Oct 1836**; died 18 Oct 1920. Daughter of the Kōchi haulage boss **袈沙丸儀平** (some sources say a rice dealer). She married the Tosa sword-instructor 楠瀬実 in 1854 (or 1857) and learned sword, naginata and sickle-and-chain. **Widowed in 1874 (WP, 日外) or 1872 (Heibonsha)**; childless, she became the **female household head (女戸主)**. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/楠瀬喜多 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/楠瀬喜多-830723
- In the **1878** Kōchi ward-assembly election she was refused a vote as a woman although she paid taxes. She withheld her taxes and, when sent a demand, petitioned the prefecture: "If I cannot vote, I will not pay; rights and duties go together." Refused, she appealed to the **Home Ministry**. WP; KB (Heibonsha, Mypedia).
- Result: under the 区町村会法 of 1880, which let ward and village assemblies make their own electoral rules, the **Kamimachi (上町) town assembly in Kōchi became the first in Japan to give women householders the vote** (sources also say eligibility for office). The neighbouring **小高坂村** followed. **In 1884 a government amendment took the rule-making power back and limited the franchise to men.** WP. Ueki Emori praised these Tosa assemblies as pioneers of equal rights. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/植木枝盛
- She joined Risshisha speaking tours in Awa and Sanuki and lodged Tōyama Mitsuru, Kōno Hironaka and Sugita Teiichi. She watched the Diet from the gallery four times through Kōno. A monument 「婦人参政権発祥之地」 stands at Kōchi City No. 4 Elementary School. WP.

### Kishida Toshiko 岸田俊子 (Nakajima Shōen 中島湘煙)
- Born 文久3年12月5日 = **13 Jan 1864**, Kyoto, to the kimono merchants 小松屋. Sekiguchi gives 1861 and the 367日 dictionary 1861; most sources give 1863/64. She entered the Kyoto women's normal school in 1877 and left ill. **Court lecturer in Chinese classics to the Empress (文事御用掛)** from 1879 (or 1878/1880). Resigned in autumn 1881 and toured; met Sakazaki Shiran and Miyazaki Muryū in Kōchi. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/中島湘煙 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/岸田俊子-155442
- **April 1882**: first speech, 「婦女の道」, at the Rikken Seitō meeting in Osaka. Then a lecture tour until 1884. In **May 1882 at Okayama** her speech converted the 16-year-old Kageyama Hideko. WP 中島湘煙; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/福田英子
- **The "Hakoiri musume" (箱入娘 / 函入娘) speech, Ōtsu, 12 Oct 1883.** She gave it at a meeting registered as a *scholarly* lecture. She was **arrested after the meeting ended, not stopped mid-speech, and taken the same night to the unconvicted-prisoners' gaol**, charged with breaching the Assembly Ordinance and **insulting an official** (官吏侮辱). The evidence was the **attending policeman's transcript**. She admitted the gist in court but disputed the details. The Ōtsu police inspector's report (届書) read the speech as a hidden political allegory: 「暗ニ、現政府ノ集会条例新聞条例出版条例等ヲ以テ函ニ比喩シ、父母ヲ政府ニ、人民ヲ娘ニ喩ヘ、徹頭徹尾治者被治者ノ関係ヲ論シタリ」. The "manservants and maids" whom parents send to catch an eloping daughter were taken to mean the police, and that was the official-insult charge. She denied saying 「娘の駆け落ちしたるとき下男下女をして捕へしむる」 in that form. While she was still in custody a booklet 『函入娘・婚姻之不完全』 "by Kishida Toshi" (騒々堂本店) was printed, dated **18 Oct**. Sekiguchi argues its text is not her speech. She wrote 「獄ノ奇談」 about her imprisonment. 関口すみ子「岸田俊『函入娘』考」『法学志林』109-2 (2011). PDF: https://hosei.ecats-library.jp/da/repository/00007680/law109-2_sekiguchi.pdf (doi:10.15002/00007673)
- **Penalty**: Heibonsha says she was **fined** for breaching the Assembly Ordinance. Yamakawa says she was **imprisoned (投獄)** on suspicion of official insult and breach of the ordinance. en.wikipedia: "arrested, tried, and fined for having made a political speech without a permit". I found **no source for the amount**. KB; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshiko_Kishida
- In May–June 1884 (issues 2–32) she serialised 「**同胞姉妹に告ぐ**」 in ten parts in 『自由燈』 (correction from a later check: Iwasaki Minoru, 「近代女性の原像」, http://www.iwasakiminoru.com/kindaijyosei.pdf). Heibonsha summarises it: male supremacy springs from barbaric greed; men and women should relate through love and compassion. **Marriage to Nakajima Nobuyuki**, Jiyūtō vice-president, later first Speaker of the House of Representatives: **1884** (Nipponica, Heibonsha, Britannica), or **marriage registered Aug 1885** (WP, 日本人名大辞典). Obunsha calls it a love match (恋愛結婚). Baptised 1886; taught at Ferris; went to Italy in 1892 when he became minister there. She died of tuberculosis at Ōiso on **25 May 1901** (WP, Yamakawa, Britannica; Nipponica says 12 May). Her diaries 『湘烟日記』 were published in 1902/03. KB; WP.

### Fukuda (Kageyama) Hideko 福田英子 (1865–1927)
- Born 慶応元年10月5日 = **22 Nov 1865** [NAOJ], Okayama, daughter of the low-ranking samurai 景山確. Nipponica says 三女 (third daughter), Heibonsha 次女 (second). At 15 she became an assistant teacher at ¥3 a month. https://kotobank.jp/word/福田英子-124086 ; WP 福田英子
- **She dressed as a boy until 16.** In her own words (『妾の半生涯』): 「十六歳までは髪を剪りて前部を左右に分け、衣服まで悉く男生の如くに装い、加も学校へは女生と伴うて通いにき」. Local boys jeered 「『マガイ』が通る」, *magai* being horse-hoof passed off as tortoiseshell, i.e. "a fake, neither boy nor girl". She grew her hair and took up the new 束髪 style at 17 out of embarrassment. Aozora: https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/000057/files/43276_18829.html
- Kishida's 1882 Okayama visit led her to join the Okayama Women's Friendship Society (女子親睦会). In 1883 she and her mother opened the girls' school **蒸紅学舎**, with evening classes. It was closed under the Assembly Ordinance in 1884. Itagaki had her studies funded by Dosho Shōzaburō and made **Sakazaki Shiran her guardian in Tokyo**. She entered Shin'ei Girls' School around Nov 1884. WP; KB Heibonsha.
- **Osaka Incident 大阪事件 (1885)**: Ōi Kentarō, Kobayashi Kusuo, Isoyama Seibei and others planned to send armed shishi to Korea to overthrow the pro-Qing government and spark revolution at home. Exposed in Nov 1885 when Isoyama defected; **139 arrested**. Sentences: Ōi and Arai **9 years' heavy penal servitude (重懲役)**; Kobayashi and Isoyama 6 years' light imprisonment (軽禁獄) per WP. Heibonsha instead gives Kobayashi 9 years with Ōi and Arai. Hideko had raised funds and **carried the explosive chemicals in a Chinese trunk as "a woman's luggage" to avoid notice**. She was arrested in Nagasaki and held in Osaka (Nakanoshima). As the only woman she was famous as the 紅一点 and called **「東洋のジャンヌ・ダルク」**. https://kotobank.jp/word/大阪事件-39191 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/大阪事件 ; 『妾の半生涯』
- Her trial opened on **25 May 1887**. She had lost all her hair in prison illness. She was sentenced to **"over a year" of light imprisonment (一年有余の軽禁錮)**, elsewhere in the memoir "a year and a half". In remand she acted as head of the women's cell and taught young prisoners. Released in the **constitution amnesty of Feb 1889**. 『妾の半生涯』 (Aozora).
- She had a child with Ōi, who deserted her. In 1892 she married Fukuda Yūsaku, who died in 1900 leaving three children. She opened 角筈女子工芸学校 in 1901. **『妾の半生涯』 published Oct 1904**; its preface line 「妾が過ぎ来し方は蹉跌の上の蹉跌なりき。されど妾は常に戦えり」. She founded **『世界婦人』 in 1907**, which ran to no. 38 in July 1909. She petitioned the Diet to amend Article 5 of the Public Peace Police Law and supported the Yanaka villagers in the Ashio pollution case. Died 2 May 1927. KB; WP.

### Shimizu Toyoko (Shikin) 清水紫琴 and "Koware yubiwa"
- Born late 12th month of 慶応3年, registered Jan 1868 (WP). Kotobank (20世紀日本人名事典) gives 慶応4年1月11日 = 4 Feb 1868 [NAOJ]. Raised in Kyoto; graduated from the Kyoto prefectural girls' school. **Married the lawyer and activist 岡崎晴正 in 1885**, who kept a concubine from before the marriage. Divorced in **1889** (WP; 20世紀日本人名事典) or about 1887 (Winston, per en.wikipedia). In 1887 she and Okazaki drew **2,000 people** to a theatre in Nara. Met Ueki Emori in 1888. Became a reporter on 『女学雑誌』 in 1890 and its chief writer from Nov 1890. **Had a son, 清水家邦 (b. 1891), by Ōi Kentarō.** Married the agronomist 古在由直 (later president of Tokyo Imperial University) in 1892. Stopped writing after 『移民学園』 (1899), a novel about buraku people. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/清水紫琴 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/清水紫琴-1081149 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/女学雑誌
- **「こわれ指環」 ("The Broken Ring")**, 『女学雑誌』, **1 Jan 1891**. A woman explains why she still wears a ring **whose stone has fallen out**. (Correction from review, October 2026: in the story she pulls the stone out herself, deliberately; see note 14, line 188.) She married at 18 in an arranged match, could never love her husband, and left him. She keeps the broken ring as a memorial, hoping that "its giver might one day make it whole again". Full text, Aozora: https://www.aozora.gr.jp/cards/001146/files/43515_16581.html
- Her protests against the 1890 law include 「何故に女子は政談集会に参聴することを許されざる乎」 and 「泣いて愛する姉妹に告ぐ」 (listed with sources at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimizu_Shikin).

### Laws closing politics to women
- **Assembly Ordinance 集会条例**, **5 Apr 1880**: political meetings and associations needed police permission; police could dissolve meetings; soldiers, police, teachers and pupils were barred. It was not yet aimed at women. Tightened in Dec 1880 and **3 June 1882**. In 1882 alone there were 86 violators and 351 dissolutions (Heibonsha). https://kotobank.jp/word/集会条例-76769
- **Municipal code 1888**: women excluded from town and village assembly elections. **House election law of 11 Feb 1889**: voters were men aged 25+ paying ¥15 direct tax; candidates were men 30+. Heibonsha in https://kotobank.jp/word/集会及政社法-1335370 ; Britannica https://kotobank.jp/word/衆議院議員選挙法-76825
- **集会及政社法 (Law on Assembly and Political Associations)**, promulgated **25 July 1890**. It **barred women from attending or organising political meetings and from joining political associations**. Repealing it became a main aim of the women's movement. Nipponica in KB 集会条例; Heibonsha https://kotobank.jp/word/集会及政社法-1335370 ; WP 日本の女性解放運動 (adds: women could not even go to hear political speeches). The Tokyo Women's Reform Society (Kyōfūkai, founded 1886 under Yajima Kajiko) campaigned against the law and petitioned for monogamy and against the trafficking of women abroad. It argued that adultery should be punishable for both sexes. https://kotobank.jp/word/婦人矯風会-124740 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/矢島楫子-21848

## 6. The Civil Code

- **Boissonade** (1825–1910) came to Japan in 1873. He taught at the Justice Ministry law school and drafted the Penal Code, the Code of Criminal Instruction and the property books of the Civil Code. In 1875 he urged the abolition of judicial torture, apparently the only foreign adviser to do so. He left in 1895. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ギュスターヴ・エミール・ボアソナード
- **"Old Civil Code" (旧民法)**: property books (Law 28) promulgated **21 Apr 1890**; **family and succession books (Law 98) promulgated 7 Oct 1890**; both due in force on **1 Jan 1893**. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/民法_(日本). The family part (人事編) was drafted by **Japanese commissioners**: Kumano Binzō (main drafter of 人事編), Isobe Shirō (succession), Kōmyōji Saburō (adoption), Kuroda Tsunahiko (戸主及び家族), Takano Shinson, Inoue Shōichi and others. It was not Boissonade's work. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/民法典論争 ; Heibonsha in https://kotobank.jp/word/民法典論争-140173
- **The controversy (民法典論争, 1889–92), timeline (WP)**: May 1889, the Tokyo University law graduates' society (法学士会) opinion starts it. Dec 1890, the First Diet postpones the Commercial Code. Feb 1891, the House of Peers resolution on the codes. Apr 1891, Hozumi Yatsuka's 「国家的民法」. **Aug 1891, Hozumi Yatsuka 「民法出デテ忠孝亡ブ」 in 『法学新報』 no. 5.** **May 1892, the Third Diet passes the postponement bill** (Peers on 28 May). **The emperor sanctioned it on 24 Nov 1892.** The Code Investigation Committee started in May 1894. Books 1–3 of the new code passed in **Mar 1896** (Law 89). **Books 4–5 (family, succession) passed June 1898 as Law 9, dated 21 June 1898; the whole code took effect on 16 July 1898.** WP 民法典論争; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/穂積八束 ; WP 民法 (日本)
- **Revisionist point (WP 民法典論争)**: the 戸主 power and single-heir household succession were already in the 1890 code (人事編 ch. 13 戸主及び家族; 家督相続). So the family law changed little in substance. WP calls the view that the 1898 code was far more conservative an "old orthodoxy" (旧通説), and cites Hoshino Tōru that Hozumi's essay mattered more for its catchy title than for deciding the outcome.
- **Parental consent to marry**: under the **1890 code (人事編 art. 38) a child of any age needed parental consent**. The **1898 code art. 772** required consent of the parents in the household **"but not after a man reaches 30 or a woman 25"** (「但男が満30歳女が満25歳に達したる後は此限りに在らず」). This was *more* liberal than 1890. The drafters' original text had required consent only for minors. WP 民法典論争; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/家制度
- **Marriage age**: first fixed by the 1898 code at **17 for men, 15 for women**. Majority was 20 since an 1876 Council of State notice. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/婚姻適齢. Marriage took legal effect **by registration only**, with no ceremony required; the 1890 code had also required a customary ceremony. WP 民法典論争.
- **The ie (家) system, 1898–1947**, in force 16 July 1898 – 2 May 1947. The **戸主 (household head)**: consent to members' marriage and adoption (art. 750); power to fix their residence (art. 749); power to strike members off the register (離籍) if they disobeyed. A marriage without his consent was still valid, at the cost of being struck off. **Household succession (家督相続)** to one heir, ranked by degree of kinship, **male before female**, legitimate before illegitimate, elder before younger. But a legitimate daughter came before an illegitimate son. **Women heads (女戸主)** could exist. When a man married into a woman head's house (入夫婚姻), he became head unless they declared otherwise. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/家制度. Ume Kenjirō, one of the drafters, wrote in the Yomiuri on 5 Jan 1900 that "the househead has become almost unnecessary". WP 家制度.
- **The wife's legal incapacity (妻の無能力)**: art. 14 of the Meiji code. A wife needed her **husband's permission** for the acts listed in art. 12 (borrowing, real-estate dealings, litigation and so on), to accept or refuse gifts and bequests, and **to make contracts binding her person** (身体ニ羈絆ヲ受クヘキ契約). Acts without permission were voidable, not void. The rule applied only to married women. The drafters said it was inherited from the 1890 code (人事編 art. 68), following Franco-Italian practice. Kumano and Boissonade were both critical of the French rule. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/行為能力 ; WP 民法典論争
- **Divorce (1898 code)**: divorce by agreement, by simple registration (art. 808). Under 25, the consent of those who consented to the marriage was needed (809). **Custody went to the father** unless agreed otherwise (812). Judicial divorce only on **ten listed grounds** (813). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/離婚. A wife's right to sue for divorce first came with the **太政官布告 of 15 May 1873**, which required her to be accompanied by her father or brothers. Heibonsha in https://kotobank.jp/word/離婚-148650. WP 離婚 misprints this as "1883年（明治6年）".
- **Adultery in the penal codes**:
  - **1880 Penal Code** (in force 1 Jan 1882), **art. 353**: 「有夫ノ婦姦通シタル者ハ六月以上二年以下ノ重禁錮ニ處ス其相姦スル者亦同シ」. Prosecution only on the **husband's** complaint, and void if he had condoned it. **Art. 311**: 「本夫其妻ノ姦通ヲ覺知シ姦所ニ於テ直チニ姦夫又ハ姦婦ヲ殺傷シタル者ハ其罪ヲ宥恕ス」: a husband who killed or wounded his wife or her lover caught in the act was excused. Art. 354: bigamy. https://ja.wikisource.org/wiki/刑法_(明治13年太政官布告第36号)
  - **1907 Penal Code** (Law 45, promulgated **24 Apr 1907**, in force **1 Oct 1908**), **art. 183**: 「有夫ノ婦姦通シタルトキハ二年以下ノ懲役ニ處ス其相姦シタル者亦同シ」, again on the husband's complaint. A married man's affair with an unmarried woman was no crime. Abolished on 26 Oct 1947. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/姦通罪

## 7. The Imperial House Law (1889) and the female-succession drafts

- **Imperial House Law**, sanctioned **11 Feb 1889**, not published in the Official Gazette (treated as house law). **Art. 1**: 「大日本國皇位ハ祖宗ノ皇統ニシテ男系ノ男子之ヲ繼承ス」. Art. 2: 「皇位ハ皇長子ニ傳フ」. Art. 4: legitimate before illegitimate lines. **Regency** (art. 21): princes, then the Empress, the Empress Dowager, the Grand Empress Dowager, and princesses. **Art. 23: a female regent must be unmarried** (「皇族女子ノ攝政ニ任スルハ其ノ配偶アラサル者ニ限ル」). https://ja.wikisource.org/wiki/皇室典範_(明治二十二年二月十一日) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/皇室典範_(1889年)
- The Constitution's art. 2 originally read 「皇子孫之ヲ継承ス」. At Itō's Takanawa villa meeting on 27 Jan 1889 it was changed to **「皇男子孫」**. Footnote in 山田敏之, below.
- **Main source for the drafts**: 山田敏之「旧皇室典範における男系男子による皇位継承制と永世皇族制の確立」『レファレンス』808 (NDL, May 2018), pp. 8–12. https://dl.ndl.go.jp/view/download/digidepo_11095235_po_080801.pdf?contentNo=1
  - **Genrōin drafts**: imperial order of 7 Sept 1876 to draft a constitution "taking account of the laws of foreign states".
    - **1876 first draft 「日本国憲按」**, art. 2: 「…同族ニ於テハ男ハ女ニ先チ…」 (within the same degree, males before females). This implies women could succeed. Art. 4: 「**女主入テ嗣クトキハ其夫ハ決シテ帝国ノ政治ニ干與スル コト無カル可シ**」. Both are almost copies of arts. 50 and 54 of the **Spanish constitution of 1845**.
    - **Dec 1877 revision**: female succession dropped.
    - **July 1880 「国憲按」**: male-line succession, but 「**若シ止ムコトヲ得サルトキハ女統入テ嗣クコトヲ得**」. Councillor 河田景與 objected that children of a princess and her husband are of another surname, so their succession would break the unbroken line. Vice-president 佐々木高行 and others asked for deletion. Presented in Dec 1880 by President Ōki Takatō and **rejected by Iwakura and Itō** as a patchwork of foreign constitutions ignoring Japan's 国体.
  - **Private draft constitutions (c. 1881)** split five ways. Some allowed women if no male in the direct or collateral lines (Chikuzen Kyōaikai 1880; Chiba Takusaburō's Itsukaichi draft 1881; the Ōmeisha draft, and others). Some allowed princesses but not their line. Some allowed both, including **Ueki Emori's draft** and Ono Azusa's. Many barred the empress's husband from politics.
  - **Ōmeisha public debate 「女帝ヲ立ルノ可否」**, serialised in 『東京横浜毎日新聞』 **14 Mar – 4 Apr 1882**. **Shimada Saburō**, against: past empresses were stand-ins (摂位) until a crown prince grew up and stayed single, which "is against human nature today"; no suitable consort exists, since a foreign prince is unthinkable and a subject would lower the throne. And: **「我が国の現状では男尊女卑のため皇婿を立てると国民は…女帝の上に一つの尊位を占める人があるように思い、女帝の威徳を損する」**; a consort would also steer the empress behind the scenes. **Numa Morikazu**, against: people would not see an empress's son as of the unbroken line. **Kōzuka Ryū**, for: if only female-line royals remained, would you go outside the dynasty? The chairman Takahashi Shōemon ruled 「女帝を立つべからず」. WP 女性天皇: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/女性天皇
  - **「皇室制規」**, drafted in the imperial household (制度取調局, under Itō) late 1885 – early 1886: art. 1, 「**皇位ハ男系ヲ以テ継承スルモノトス若シ皇族中男系絶ユルトキハ皇族中女系ヲ以テ継承ス**」; art. 13, 「**女帝ノ夫ハ皇胤ニシテ臣籍ニ入リタル者ノ内皇統ニ近キ者ヲ迎フヘシ**」. Author unknown.
  - **Inoue Kowashi**, then 図書頭, opposed it in 「謹具意見」, quoting Shimada and Numa. He argued: the son of an empress and a "Minamoto so-and-so" would be a Minamoto, i.e. a change of surname (易姓); some European states also bar women; there are other ways to keep the line fertile. The **1886 「帝室典則」** draft accordingly dropped all female and female-line provisions.
  - **From 1887, Yanagihara Sakimitsu (柳原前光, head of the decorations bureau) and Inoue drafted the Imperial House Law under Itō.** On 20 Mar 1887 Itō, Yanagihara, Inoue and Itō Miyoji met on the drafts. **None of the later drafts provided for female succession, and it was not debated again.** Yamada; WP 皇室典範 (1889年).
  - WP adds that Itō struck out a draft clause allowing abdication, which Inoue had supported. WP 皇室典範 (1889年).
- **Yanagihara Sakimitsu** (1850–1894) was the brother of **柳原愛子, the Meiji Emperor's consort and mother of the Taishō Emperor**, and father of the poet Byakuren. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/柳原前光
- **The eight earlier reigning empresses (八方十代, ten reigns)**: Suiko 推古 (33rd; 593–628), Kōgyoku 皇極 (35th; 642–645) reigning again as Saimei 斉明 (37th; 655–661), Jitō 持統 (41st; 690–697), Genmei 元明 (43rd; 707–715), Genshō 元正 (44th; 715–724), Kōken 孝謙 (46th; 749–758) again as Shōtoku 称徳 (48th; 764–770), **Meishō 明正 (109th; 1629–1643)**, **Go-Sakuramachi 後桜町 (117th; 1762–1771)**. After Shōtoku (the Dōkyō affair) there was none for over 850 years. Sennyūji holds portraits of 12 of the 14 Edo emperors; the two women are the missing ones. Go-Sakuramachi did not attend Shihōhai or Niiname-sai, because menstrual "pollution" (御障り) kept her from rites. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/女性天皇

## 8. Ideology and education, 1890–1912

- **Imperial Rescript on Education**, **30 Oct 1890**, circulated by Education Minister Yoshikawa on 31 Oct: 「爾臣民父母ニ孝ニ兄弟ニ友ニ**夫婦相和シ**朋友相信シ…一旦緩急アレハ義勇公ニ奉シ」. https://ja.wikisource.org/wiki/教育ニ関スル勅語 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/教育勅語-52408. Heibonsha: from the 1880s Confucian views of women dominated; the Rescript (1890) and the 1898 Civil Code settled the 良妻賢母 ideology in schools. https://kotobank.jp/word/良妻賢母-149966
- **Ryōsai kenbo 良妻賢母**: NKD's earliest citation is Tokutomi Roka 『思出の記』 (1900–01): 「其よりも良妻賢母の必要は一般に渉っての事ですよ」. Britannica says the phrase began in early Meiji. KB 良妻賢母. **The official formula in 1899 was Education Minister Kabayama Sukenori's**: the girls' high school's aim is 「**賢母良妻タラシムルノ素養ヲ為スニ在リ**、故ニ優美高尚ノ気風、温良貞淑ノ資性ヲ涵養スルト倶ニ中人以上ノ生活ニ必須ナル学術技芸ヲ知得セシメンコトヲ要ス」. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高等女学校. Kikuchi Dairoku was Education Minister 1901–03, but I could not source the "Kikuchi 1902" attribution (see below). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/菊池大麓
- **高等女学校令 (Higher Girls' School Order)**: imperial ordinance No. 31, **7 Feb 1899** (Gazette 8 Feb), in force 1 Apr 1899, 20 articles. Every prefecture had to have one. Normally 4 years (3 or 5 allowed); entry after 2 years of higher primary school (age 12+); public schools had to charge fees. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高等女学校令. Purpose: 「中等以上ノ社会ニ於ケル女子」に「須要ナル高等普通教育ヲ為ス」 (art. 1). The emphasis was housework, sewing and accomplishments (Nipponica). There were **193 higher girls' schools by 1910**. Under 5% of girls went on to them in 1905, 9% in 1920. https://kotobank.jp/word/高等女学校-62898 ; WP 高等女学校
- **Women's higher education**:
  - **女子英学塾**, Tsuda: 14 Sept 1900, 10 students (above).
  - **東京女医学校**: founded **5 Dec 1900** in a room of the Shisei clinic by **Yoshioka Yayoi** (b. 明治4年3月10日 = 29 Apr 1871), after the Saiseigakusha medical school stopped admitting women. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東京女子医科大学 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/吉岡彌生
  - **日本女子大学校**: authorised 1900, **opened 20 Apr 1901**. Founded by Naruse Jinzō, whose 『女子教育』 appeared in 1896, with Ōkuma Shigenobu as committee chair. Mitsui gave 5,520 tsubo at Mejiro, through Hirooka Asako. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/日本女子大学
  - Earlier, **Ogino Ginko** had become the first state-licensed woman physician. She passed the first part of the exam in Sept 1884, the only one of four women, and the second in Mar 1885. She opened her practice in Yushima in May 1885. She had chosen medicine after being treated, humiliatingly, by male doctors for gonorrhoea caught from her husband (divorced 1870). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/荻野吟子
- **Aikoku Fujinkai 愛国婦人会**: founded **24 Feb 1901** (inaugural meeting 2 Mar) by **Okumura Ioko** after she visited the Boxer front. Its prospectus was drafted by Shimoda Utako; first president 岩倉久子. Slogan: save "one half-collar" (半衿一掛) of spending to give to soldiers' families. **460,000 members by 1905**, 700,000 in 1907 (WP). https://kotobank.jp/word/愛国婦人会-23672 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/愛国婦人会
- **Public Peace Police Law 治安警察法**: Law No. 36, signed 9 Mar 1900, Gazette **10 Mar 1900**. **Art. 5**: those who may not join political associations are active soldiers, police, Shinto priests, Buddhist priests and other clergy, teachers and students, 「**五 女子**」, minors, and those deprived of rights. And: 「**女子及未成年者ハ公衆ヲ會同スル政談集會ニ會同シ若ハ其ノ發起人タルコトヲ得ス**」. https://ja.wikisource.org/wiki/治安警察法
  - **Petitions to amend art. 5**: Fukuda Hideko and the Heiminsha circle petitioned (Heibonsha). **On 25 Mar 1908 the House of Representatives passed an amendment bill based on Fukuda's petition; the House of Peers rejected it on 26 Mar.** https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/治安警察法 ; KB 福田英子 (Heibonsha). Only **in 1922** were women allowed to attend and organise political meetings; they were still barred from parties. WP 治安警察法.
- **Kanno Suga 管野スガ**: born 7 June 1881, Osaka; pen name 幽月 / 須賀子. Unhappily married at 19 and divorced. Reporter on 『大阪朝報』 from 1902; baptised Nov 1903. She first denounced prostitutes as 「醜業婦」, then joined the Kyōfūkai's abolitionism, then socialism. Lived with Arahata Kanson. Acquitted in the 1908 Red Flag Incident. Later lived with Kōtoku Shūsui, attacked in the press as "bigamy". **Arrested at Yugawara with Kōtoku on 1 June 1910.** In the High Treason trial (opened 10 Dec 1910, closed to the public) **24 were sentenced to death on 18 Jan 1911**, 12 of them commuted to life. **Eleven men were hanged on 24 Jan 1911, and Kanno, the only woman, on 25 Jan 1911.** Her death poem: 「くろがねの 窓にさしいる 日の影の 移るを守り けふも暮らしぬ」. **Her gravestone at Shōshunji, Yoyogi, reads on its back 「革命の先駆者管野スガここにねむる」.** Prison memoir 「死出の道艸」. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/管野スガ ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/大逆事件 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/大逆事件-90983
- **Seitō 青鞜**: first issue **Sept 1911**, 1,000 copies; 52 issues to Feb 1916. Founders: Hiratsuka Haru (Raichō), Yasumochi Yoshiko, Nakano Hatsuko, Kiuchi Teiko, Mozume Kazuko, mostly graduates of Japan Women's University. 18 members and 7 supporters, including Yosano Akiko and Mori Shige. Cover by Naganuma (Takamura) Chieko. Opened with Yosano's poem 「そぞろごと」 (「山の動く日来る」). Hiratsuka's manifesto 「元始、女性は実に太陽であった」 was the first use of the name らいてう. Funded partly from Hiratsuka's mother's savings for her wedding. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/青鞜 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/平塚らいてう ; https://kotobank.jp/word/青鞜-86272

## 9. People who crossed gender lines in public life, and how they were treated

- **Okumura Ioko 奥村五百子** (1845–1907; born 弘化2年5月3日 = 7 June 1845), founder of the Aikoku Fujinkai. **In 1862 she carried secret messages to Chōshū disguised as a man** (男装). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/奥村五百子 ; Heibonsha https://kotobank.jp/word/奥村五百子-451839 (「男装で萩藩に使いし」)
- **The Aizu women** of 1868 cut their hair to the shoulders and went "in male guise"; Yamamoto Yae cut her hair and dressed as a man (section 1).
- **Wada Ei** entered Tomioka in 1873 dressed in her father's wartime wool coat and a man's "Yoshitsune hakama" (section 4).
- **Kageyama (Fukuda) Hideko** wore boys' clothes and cropped hair until 16 and was jeered as a "magai", a fake (section 5).
- **The 1871 haircut law and women**: the 散髪脱刀令 (明治4年8月9日 = **23 Sept 1871**) made the topknot and the sword optional for men. Some women "misread" it and cut their hair short, so **Tokyo prefecture issued a ban on women's short hair (女子断髪禁止) on 明治5年4月5日 = 11 May 1872**. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/散髪脱刀令 ; Nipponica: Tokyo's 1872 「婦女子散髪禁止」 告諭, https://kotobank.jp/word/散髪脱刀令-514608. Heibonsha (旧版): because the edict did not specify men, "women at the front of fashion cut their hair", which **the 違式詿違条例 then banned**. Britannica: "women's haircuts were banned the following year". https://kotobank.jp/word/断髪-95419
- **The Misdemeanour Ordinances (違式詿違条例)**: Tokyo version, 明治5年11月 (Justice Ministry notice; 11月 = from 1 Dec 1872 [NAOJ]); national 地方違式詿違条例, 1873. They were absorbed into the 1880 Penal Code's police offences (違警罪). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/警察犯処罰令. The 2022 Shōtō Museum exhibition 「装いの力―異性装の日本史」, as reported, says they **included a ban on cross-dressing, in force about eight years**. It also says that **newspapers often reported people punished under them**, feeding public disgust. Exhibit cited there: **『東京日々新聞』 no. 813 (Oct 1874)**, a nishiki-e news print by Ochiai Yoshiiku. A "wife" was found to be male when papers were requested under the 1871 Family Registration Law. **The marriage, which the husband had knowingly kept up for three years, was forcibly annulled**, and the wife's hair was cut to the men's zangiri style. https://www.tokyoartbeat.com/articles/-/the-power-of-clothing-history-of-cross-dressing-in-japan-report-2022-09
- **Schoolgirls in men's hakama**: at the 1872 Takebashi school the state ordered girls into **samurai men's hakama** (section 3). The later women's hakama (女袴, andon-bakama), designed by Shimoda Utako at the Peeresses' School (opened Sept 1885), was a pleated garment without divided legs. Fukunaga Misao dates the general schoolgirl hakama to the later 1890s. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/袴 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/華族女学校
- **First recorded use of 「男装」 (NKD)**: **Kimura Akebono's 『婦女の鑑』 (1889), ch. 9**: 「身は男装のよそほひなし鏡に向ひて」. https://kotobank.jp/word/男装-564337. 『婦女の鑑』 was serialised in the **Yomiuri, 3–28 Jan 1889**. Its heroine quarrels with her father, runs away, graduates from the women's section at Cambridge, works as a factory girl in New York, and returns to build a factory and teach the poor. Kimura (real name 岡本栄子, daughter of the beef-restaurant king Kimura Shōhei) wrote it between shifts at the family restaurant. She died in 1890 aged 18 (or 20). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/木村曙
- **Love between women, Meiji to 1912**: WP says women's double suicides were reported from the 19th century, but **the 1911 suicide of two schoolgirls in Niigata** began the press framing of schoolgirl intimacy as "homosexuality". The girls' slang 「お目」, with an older girl sending a purple or red ribbon through friends to a younger one, is quoted. It became 「エス」 in the 1910s. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/エス_(文化). The 1910s report of the custom is undated in WP. The criminal code's "love suicide" category (相対死) assumed a man and a woman, so same-sex double suicides were recorded as "unnatural deaths" (変死). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/心中
- **Hiratsuka Raichō and Otake Kōkichi** (尾竹紅吉, 1893–1966) were lovers in 1912 (Meiji 45). Kōkichi's "five-coloured cocktail" and her visit to the Yoshiwara were made into press scandals about the "New Woman". She left Seitō in Oct 1912, and stones were thrown at the Hiratsuka house. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/富本一枝 ; WP 平塚らいてう
- **Takahashi Oden**: I found **no evidence** that she wore men's clothing. She is relevant as a press creation instead. She was sentenced and **beheaded on 31 Jan 1879**. The 『東京曙新聞』 ran 「毒婦高橋お伝」 from 1 to 7 Feb 1879, and Kanagaki Robun's 『高橋阿伝夜刃譚』 followed. The "last woman beheaded in Japan" claim is false: 7 women in 1880 and 7 in 1881 were beheaded, and decapitation ended on 1 Jan 1882. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高橋お伝

## 10. Divorce, marriage age, miai and elopement

- **Divorce rate**: about **3.38 per 1,000 in 1883**; WP gives 3.38 in one paragraph and 3.39 in another. WP's explanation: in Meiji women were valued for labour rather than virginity, there was little stigma on remarriage, and many divorces were wives being driven out or running away. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/離婚
- **After the 1898 code** (IPSS 人口統計資料集 2024, Table 6‑2): **1900: 63,828 divorces, 1.46‰** (99.7% by agreement, 196 by court); **1910: 59,432, 1.21‰**. https://www.ipss.go.jp/syoushika/tohkei/Data/Popular2024/T06-02.files/sheet001.htm
- **Marriages** (Table 6‑1): 1900: 346,528 (7.9‰); 1910: 441,222 (9.0‰). The share of first marriages for wives was 85.0% in 1900 and 88.5% in 1910. https://www.ipss.go.jp/syoushika/tohkei/Data/Popular2024/T06-01.files/sheet001.htm
- **Mean age at marriage** (Table 6‑12). All marriages: 1899, husband **27.6**, wife **23.0**; 1900: 27.7 / 23.1; 1905: 28.6 / 24.0. **First marriages, 1910: husband 27.0, wife 23.0.** https://www.ipss.go.jp/syoushika/tohkei/Data/Popular2024/T06-12.files/sheet001.htm. Caveat, my inference: these are registered marriages, and registration often lagged the actual wedding.
- **Miai 見合い**: in samurai custom the couple first saw each other on the wedding night. Commoners arranged a glimpse, with the man calling on a pretext such as "looking at a horse". **From mid-Meiji in the cities, miai meetings were held at theatres and restaurants** and the couple could talk (Nipponica). In the older form the decision was usually made before the miai (Heibonsha). https://kotobank.jp/word/見合い-138185. NKD's first 「見合結婚」 is Masamune Hakuchō 『泥人形』 (1911): 「目隠しして偶然相触れた男と女が契を結ぶやうな見合結婚」. https://kotobank.jp/word/見合結婚-636985
- **Elopement and runaways**:
  - Kishida's 1883 Ōtsu speech, as recorded by police, mentioned parents sending servants to catch an eloping daughter (Sekiguchi, above).
  - Kimura Akebono's 1889 heroine runs away from her father (above).
  - **Hiratsuka Haru and Morita Sōhei, 21–23 Mar 1908**: Haru's family filed a missing-person report on 21 Mar. Police found the pair on the 23rd near the Obizumi pass above **Shiobara**, after a failed love-suicide in the snow. The papers headlined it 「**自然主義の高潮 紳士淑女の情死未遂 情夫は文学士、小説家 情婦は女子大学卒業生**」. Morita turned it into 『煤煙』 for the Asahi (Jan–May 1909) at Sōseki's suggestion. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/平塚らいてう ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/煤煙_(小説)
  - Kotobank 駆落ち: the core sense is lovers forbidden to marry fleeing to another place; written 欠け落ち, it also meant (in the early modern period) fleeing one's home district because of heavy taxes, poverty or crime. https://kotobank.jp/word/駆落ち-461048
- **Love matches**: the Iwakura girls Shigeko (with Uryū) and Sutematsu (with Ōyama) married for love, as WP describes them. Kishida and Nakajima are called a love match (Obunsha). These are notable precisely as exceptions. WP 瓜生繁子; WP 大山捨松; KB 岸田俊子.

## Telling details

- **The "box" speech was read as a political code.** The police report on Kishida's 「函入娘」 (Ōtsu, 12 Oct 1883) said she likened the government's press and assembly laws to the box, the government to the parents and the people to the daughter. The servants sent after the eloping daughter were taken to be the police in the hall. A pirated booklet of the speech was on sale (dated 18 Oct) while she was still in gaol. (Sekiguchi 2011)
- **Fukuda Hideko**, the "Joan of Arc of the East", wore boys' clothes and cropped hair until 16, and children shouted "here comes the fake" (マガイ). In 1885 she carried the bomb chemicals in a Chinese trunk because a woman's luggage would not be searched. She lost all her hair in prison before her trial on 25 May 1887. (『妾の半生涯』)
- **The ring with the missing stone.** Shimizu Shikin's first story (1 Jan 1891) is told by a divorced woman who keeps wearing her marriage ring with its stone gone, as a memorial, wishing "its giver" could make it whole again.
- **Wada Ei** went to the model silk mill in 1873 dressed in her father's Boshin-war wool coat and a man's Yoshitsune hakama trimmed with purple crepe. The empresses' visit gave the mill girls a uniform of navy kasuri with **red-striped hakama**.
- **Rumours of blood-drinking** foreigners (the red wine) emptied the recruitment for Tomioka. The manager put his own daughter in the first row of basins to disprove them.
- **Chiba Sana beat a future daimyo** in a bout in 1856. Her Kōfu gravestone calls her "wife of Sakamoto Ryōma", though they never married and she may have married someone else in 1874.
- **A female regent had to be unmarried** under art. 23 of the 1889 Imperial House Law. This echoes Shimada Saburō's 1882 fear that an empress's husband would rule from behind her.
- **The 1890 Civil Code required parental consent to marry at any age**; the "conservative" 1898 code freed men at 30 and women at 25.
- **The 1880 Penal Code excused a husband who killed his wife and her lover caught in the act** (art. 311).
- **Concubines were legally second-degree relatives** like wives from 1871 until 1882.
- **"Untie the cattle"**: the 1872 Justice Ministry notice freed prostitutes by declaring them "no different from oxen and horses", from whom no debt can be demanded.
- **Oranda Oine** is a 1970 TV coinage; Ine named her own illegitimate daughter "Tada" ("free").
- **Sutematsu's name**: "thrown away (捨) but waited for (松)". She later married the artillery commander who had shelled her childhood castle, and they first courted in French because neither could understand the other's dialect.
- **Ee ja nai ka** began, on the earliest dating, on 13 Aug 1867 at a village outside Toyohashi. Satow saw Osaka dancers in red crape with red lanterns on their heads. Iwakura's biography says the dancing stopped on the very day of the Restoration decree (3 Jan 1868).
- **The Engi era change of 901** and every 辛酉 change down to 1861 were made to ward off a "kakumei". The modern sense of the word was then supplied by Fukuzawa's 『西洋事情』.
- **Kanno Suga's gravestone** reads "Here sleeps Kanno Suga, forerunner of the revolution". She was hanged on 25 Jan 1911, the day after the eleven men.
- **Kusunose Kita refused to pay her taxes** until she could vote, and won the vote for women householders in one Kōchi ward (1880–84). In old age she watched the Diet four times.
- **The first newspaper serial biography of Ryōma**, Sakazaki Shiran's 「天下無双人傑海南第一伝奇 汗血千里の駒」, ran in the Kōchi *Doyō shinbun* from **24 Jan to 27 Sept 1883** in 64 episodes (68 instalments). It paused from 30 Mar to 10 July while the author served a sentence for lèse-majesté: 3 months' heavy imprisonment and a ¥20 fine. A pirate edition of the first 15 episodes appeared in May 1883, while the serial was still running. Shunyōdō reissued it in one volume in 1885. Sakazaki also acted as Fukuda Hideko's Tokyo guardian and had met Kishida in Kōchi. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/汗血千里駒
- **Kimura Akebono's 1889 heroine** crosses the world, studies at Cambridge, works in a New York factory and comes home to build a factory. NKD cites this novel for the first recorded "男装" in Japanese.
- **The Sokuhatsu (束髪) hair reform of 1885** was led by doctors, who called Japanese hairdressing unhygienic. By the 1890s Kimuraya in Ginza sold a "sokuhatsu bun" shaped like the chignon. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/束髪
- **Sutematsu's charity bazaar at the Rokumeikan (June 1884)** paid for Japan's first nursing school (Oct 1886).
- **Women's wage in a silk mill was ¥0.11 a day in 1885 and ¥0.31 in 1910** (LTES Table 59).

## Uncertain / conflicting

- **Ee ja nai ka cross-dressing.** Only en.wikipedia states it ("cross-dressing, elaborate costumes, or not wearing clothes at all"), with no footnote. Satow's eyewitness passage mentions red crape, not cross-dressing. ja.WP says only 仮装. I could not reach a Japanese primary or scholarly source before the search quota ran out. Treat it as plausible but unverified.
- **The 1890 ban on women in the Diet gallery and its reversal**: **not verified**. None of the sources I opened mention it (Kotobank, WP 女性参政権, WP 日本の女性解放運動, WP 矢島楫子, WP 清水紫琴, en.WP Feminism in Japan and Shimizu Shikin). Kusunose Kita did watch the Diet four times later (WP), so any ban did not last. Check the House of Representatives' 傍聴人規則 of Nov 1890 or a women's-history survey before using it.
- **Kishida's penalty in 1883**: fined (Heibonsha, en.WP) or imprisoned (Yamakawa: 投獄). The amount and length were not found. The figure of "¥5" sometimes repeated is **unverified**. Her marriage date is 1884 (Nipponica, Heibonsha, Britannica) or Aug 1885 (WP: registration), and her death date 25 May 1901 or 12 May (Nipponica). Her birth year is 1863/64; Sekiguchi gives 1861. The OCR of Sekiguchi's PDF reads 「一八八一二年」; context and every other source give 1883.
- **The 1905–1909 socialist women's petitions on art. 5**: only the 25 March 1908 House of Representatives vote and the 26 March House of Peers rejection are sourced. The often-cited first petition of Jan 1905, organised by 今井歌子 and others with a few hundred signatures, is **unverified here**.
- **"Ryōsai kenbo" and Kikuchi Dairoku 1902**: not found in WP or Kotobank. The 1899 Kabayama formula 「賢母良妻」 is sourced (WP 高等女学校). NKD's earliest 良妻賢母 is 1900–01, from Roka.
- **Kamimachi women's vote**: WP says the 区町村会法 was issued on **20 Sept 1880**. The date I recall for that law is 8 Apr 1880 (太政官布告第18号), but I could not verify it; 20 Sept may be the date of Kamimachi's own rule. One web summary also says women could stand for office as well as vote, which WP does not say. Kusunose's husband died in 1874 (WP, 日外) or 1872 (Heibonsha); they married in 1854 or 1857.
- **Osaka Incident sentences**: WP gives Kobayashi Kusuo 6 years' light imprisonment; Heibonsha gives 9 years' heavy penal servitude, the same as Ōi and Arai.
- **Fukuda Hideko's sentence**: her memoir says 「一年有余の軽禁錮」 in one place and 「刑期は一年半」 in another. Third daughter (Nipponica) or second daughter (Heibonsha).
- **Shimizu Shikin**: born late 慶応3年 12th month (WP) or 慶応4年1月11日 (20世紀日本人名事典); divorced from Okazaki in 1889 (WP, Kotobank) or about 1887 (Winston, cited on en.WP); third daughter (Nipponica) or eldest (Heibonsha).
- **Tokyo Women's Normal School**: founded 1874 (decision) and opened 1875 (ceremony 29 Nov 1875). Yamakawa says "設立 1874".
- **Takebashi school**: WP's chronology says it opened in March 1872, but the same article's site section says "1873年3月の開校". Probably a typo; 1872 is consistent with everything else.
- **Nagai Shige's age in 1871**: "満8歳" (WP 津田梅子, from the Kaitakushi papers) against a birth date of 29 Apr 1861 (WP 瓜生繁子, after 生田澄江), which would make her 10. The return date of Tsuda and Sutematsu is 21 Nov 1882 (WP Tsuda) or 22 Nov (WP Sutematsu).
- **Sutematsu's "Rokumeikan no hana" and the ¥16,000 bazaar takings** come from WP, citing 秋山ひさ 1985. "Rokumeikan no hana" carries no footnote. The WP editors themselves cut a story about a ¥5 note as anachronistic.
- **Chiba Sana**: born 1838 (WP) or 1839 (日本人名大辞典); second daughter (WP) or eldest (Kotobank). The 1874 marriage rests on one newspaper item found in 2010.
- **Matsuo Taseko**: aged 51 (WP) or 52 (Nipponica, kazoe) when she went to Kyoto.
- **Nakano Takeko**: born 1850 or 1847; shot in the forehead or the chest; beheaded by her sister, her mother or a peasant soldier (WP gives all three). The term 娘子軍 may be a post-war coinage by Hiraishi.
- **Yamamoto Yae**: the claim that she wounded Ōyama Iwao is "said to be" (と言われている) in WP. WP Sutematsu says Ōyama was wounded on the first day and evacuated, and was at Wakamatsu for only two days.
- **Wada Ei's 『富岡日記』**: written 1905 (WP), 1907–1913 (Nipponica), or completed 1913 (20世紀日本人名事典). Within the diary she says both "300" and "over 600" women were at the mill.
- **Amemiya strike numbers**: about 200 (Britannica, which also gives "115"), or "over 100" (Nipponica, Yamakawa).
- **The 1900 娼妓取締規則** allowed free cessation (WP), or "closed the road" to it (Heibonsha, old edition).
- **Factory Act protected age**: under 15 in the 1911 text (WP); Nipponica gives "under 16", which is the 1923 amendment.
- **Imperial House Law drafting**: the user asked about "Iwakura, Itō, Yanagihara". Sourced (Yamada 2018): **Iwakura and Itō rejected the Genrōin's 1880 「国憲按」**, which had a female-line clause. **Inoue Kowashi's 「謹具意見」 killed the 1885–86 「皇室制規」 female-line clause.** Yanagihara and Inoue drafted the final law from 1887 with no female clause. I found no source for a separate Yanagihara position on female succession.
- **"Kakumei" first attestation**: NKD cites 『西洋事情』 外編 and dates the whole work 1866–70. I believe 外編 is the 1868 volume but did not confirm it. Whether any pre-1868 Dutch-learning text already used 革命 for "revolution" was not checked. NKD and the JapanKnowledge blog say Fukuzawa "is thought to have" chosen the word, which is a hedge.
- **The 1883 divorce rate** is given as 3.38 and as 3.39 in the same WP article. WP's statement that wives gained the right to sue for divorce in "1883 (Meiji 6)" is an error for **1873** (Heibonsha: 15 May 1873).
- **Mean marriage ages** come from registered marriages. They may overstate the real age at which couples began living together; that point is my inference.
- **Mill-girl head-counts**: there is no single clean national series of "women factory workers" for 1900 and 1910 in the sources I could open. The silk series (172,901 in 1895, 221,770 in 1910) mixes definitions (see the Table 58 note). The cotton series is in person-days.
- **Misdemeanour ordinances (違式詿違条例)**: I could not open the original text with the cross-dressing clause or its article number. The ban rests on the museum report, and the women's-haircut ban on Heibonsha. Dating: Tokyo 明治5年11月 (WP 警察犯処罰令) against "明治6年" (the museum report, which probably means the national 地方違式詿違条例 of 1873).
