## 1. Takarazuka and the girls' revues

### Founding and the first performance (1913–1914)
- **The founder and the model.** Kobayashi Ichizō 小林一三, founder of the Minoo-Arima Electric Railway (later Hankyū), modelled the company on the Mitsukoshi boys' band and the Shirokiya girls' band. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団 ; Nipponica, https://kotobank.jp/word/宝塚歌劇団
- **First names.** It began in **July 1913** as the 宝塚唱歌隊 and was renamed 宝塚少女歌劇養成会 in **December 1913**. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚音楽学校 (citing 『90年史』 p.179)
- **Pay.** The girls had only elementary schooling, but they were paid as much as university graduates. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- **The first all-girls revue was not Takarazuka.** It was the Shirokiya department store's company, Nihonbashi, which staged the opera 《羽子板》 in **January 1912** to draw shoppers. Nihon Kokugo Daijiten and Daijisen both date the start of 少女歌劇 to this. https://kotobank.jp/word/少女歌劇
- **First performance.** It opened on **1 April 1914**, as an entertainment at the 「婚禮博覧会」 (wedding exhibition), in the Paradise Theatre, a converted indoor pool in the Takarazuka hot-spring resort. The run lasted to 31 May.
  - Programme: the opera 『ドンブラコ』 (Momotarō, music by Kitamura Suehiru 北村季晴), 『浮れ達磨』, the dance 『胡蝶』, an orchestral piece and choruses.
  - Takamine Taeko 高峰妙子 played **Momotarō**. A woman played the male lead from the very first show.
  - Sources: Kitamura Taku, 「宝塚歌劇におけるフランスのイメージ」, 『比較日本学教育研究センター研究年報』10 (2014), https://teapot.lib.ocha.ac.jp/record/40851/files/p.47-56.pdf ; Yoshida Yayoi, 「小林一三の歌舞伎観」, 『文京学院大学…紀要』12 (2012), https://www.bgu.ac.jp/library/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2022/08/cbec833bfa08ef3f73c6f21c442e61d9.pdf
- **The first cohort.** There were **16 performers**, among them Takamine Taeko (1900–1980) and Kumoi Namiko 雲井浪子 (1901–2003). At Kobayashi's suggestion their stage names were taken from the **Ogura Hyakunin Isshu** anthology. Nipponica (向井爽也), https://kotobank.jp/word/宝塚歌劇団 ; Sekai Daihyakka, https://kotobank.jp/word/少女歌劇
- **Attendance.** Average daily attendance was **1,100 in 1914** (entry was free with a spa ticket) and **2,000 in 1918**. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団

### Early repertoire, 1914–1927
- **Kobayashi's aim** was a 「国民劇」: kabuki's strengths set to Western music. He argued this in the first issues of the house magazine *Kageki* 『歌劇』:
  - 「國劇と歌劇との関係」, no. 1, August 1918;
  - 「国劇と歌劇との関係」, no. 2, November 1918.
  - Tsubouchi Shōyō endorsed the company as 「愛らしき少女隊」 in 『宝塚少女歌劇集』 no. 1 (1916). He wrote that having girls perform fairy-tale operas, to lead children to a taste for opera, was 「頗る適当な方法」.
  - Kitamura 2014 (above); Yoshida 2012 (above).
- **Kabuki pieces turned into 歌劇, 1914–1927**, as identified by Yoshida from *Kageki* and the Ikeda Bunko scripts:

  | Date | Work |

  | Jul 1916 | 「松風村雨」 |
  | Jul 1917 | 「大江山」, 「女曾我」 |
  | Jul 1917/18 | 「石童丸」 (Yoshida prints 大正7年 (1917), which is internally inconsistent) |
  | Jan 1920 | 「お夏笠物狂」 |

  - Yoshida notes that early adaptations favoured **female protagonists**, and that musume-yaku art then mattered more than it does now.
  - *Chūshingura* entered with the comic opera 『ハラキリの稽古』 (June 1926), and the *Gikeiki* world with 『鞍馬山』 (Sept 1926).
  - Yoshida 2012, table, pp. 132–133.
- **Other early works.**
  - 『クレオパトラ』 (1918): Kumoi Namiko and Shinohara Asaji 篠原淺茅 alternated the title role and became the first stars. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
  - The early signature pieces were Hisamatsu Issei's 『お夏笠物狂』 (1920) and Umemoto Rikuhei's dance 『春から秋へ』 (1921). Sekai Daihyakka, https://kotobank.jp/word/少女歌劇

### The music school, the theatres, and the first revues
- **The music school (1919).** In **January 1919** the training society was dissolved and the private **宝塚音楽歌劇学校** founded. Its pupils and graduates formed the 宝塚少女歌劇団, so school and company were one body.
  - Course: preparatory year, main year, and a "research course" (研究科). Performers counted their seniority by research-course year: 「研○」.
  - Entry age was set at 13–19 (counted the old way) in 1923.
  - School and company separated in December 1939, when the school became the 宝塚音楽舞踊学校.
  - It was renamed **宝塚音楽学校** in April 1946.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚音楽学校
  - Nipponica dates the school to December 1918 and says it ran as a boarding school. It adds that 「生徒たちの緑の袴姿は全国の少女たちのあこがれの的となった」. https://kotobank.jp/word/宝塚歌劇団
- **The motto** is 「清く 正しく 美しく」. Kobayashi's last instruction (遺訓) added 「朗らかに」 in front of it. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚音楽学校
- **Men refused, twice.**
  - In **1919** a men's course admitted **8 male students** (including the future directors Shirai Tetsuzō and Kishida Tatsuya). It was disbanded after 10 months.
  - In 1945–47 a postwar men's section trained **13 men** for three years each. None ever appeared in a main performance.
  - Performers, their parents and fans objected both times.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団 ; Baraniak-Hirata (below), citing 渡辺裕 1999
  - Kotake Satoshi's 2023 book on Taishō Takarazuka, drawn from *Kageki*'s reader letters, says fans' resistance shaped the decision. https://www.kinokuniya.co.jp/f/dsg-01-9784797674248 (WebFetch summary)
- **Troupes.**
  - Flower and Moon troupes: 1921.
  - Snow troupe: **July 1924**, with the Grand Theatre.
  - Star troupe: 1933 (abolished 1939, revived 1948).
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- **Fire and rebuilding.** On **22 January 1923** a fire destroyed the Paradise and Kōkaidō theatres. A middle-sized theatre was built at once. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- **The Grand Theatre (宝塚大劇場), July 1924.**
  - It was a whole-day resort: a reserved seat, a spa ticket and a plate of **curry rice cost 30 sen each**, so 「一圓あれば一日遊べる」.
  - From 1925 there were 12 main runs a year.
  - It first had a kabuki-style *hanamichi*, later replaced by the *ginkyō* (silver bridge, first used in 『ローズ・パリ』, 1931).
  - It burned on **25 January 1935** and reopened on 1 April 1935.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚大劇場
  - Capacity: see Uncertain §1.
- **Fans and Onoe Kikugorō (1923).** In November 1923 Kobayashi announced he would lend the Grand Theatre to the kabuki star **Onoe Kikugorō VI**. Fans protested fiercely, and in the end he lent the middle-sized theatre instead. Yoshida 2012, note 12.
- ***Mon Paris* (1927).** 『モン・パリ 〜吾が巴里よ!〜』, by Kishida Tatsuya 岸田辰彌 with choreography by Shirai Tetsuzō, was **Japan's first revue**. It opened on **1 September 1927** (Flower Troupe).
  - 250 performers in all and 16 scenes with no curtain. The story is a Japanese man's world tour, modelled on Kishida's own trip.
  - It introduced the line dance and a 16-step grand staircase (50 steps by the 1957 revival).
  - After two months at Takarazuka it moved to the Kabukiza in Tokyo.
  - In 1929 two record companies issued the theme song (Columbia 25722A; Orient 4218–19), and both were big hits.
  - 1 September became 「レビュー記念日」.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/モン・パリ ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- ***Parisette* (1930).** Shirai Tetsuzō's 『パリゼット』 opened in **August 1930**. Its songs 「すみれの花咲く頃」 and 「おゝ宝塚」 became the company's anthems.
  - The first song was the German "Wenn der weiße Flieder wieder blüht", which Shirai had heard as a French chanson.
  - The comic double act of Tachibana Kaoru and Miura Tokiko became stars.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団 ; Sekai Daihyakka, https://kotobank.jp/word/少女歌劇
  - ja.wp has **no separate article** on *Parisette*: the raw URL returns 404.
- ***Hana shishū* (1933).** 『花詩集』, the 20th-anniversary revue, was staged in 1933 and reprised to open Tokyo. By 1933 the company had about **300 members**. In **1936** it had 366 performers, and **612** counting the music school. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団 ; Sekai Daihyakka, https://kotobank.jp/word/少女歌劇
- **The Tokyo Takarazuka Theatre (Hibiya/Yūrakuchō).**
  - Built in 1 year 5 months and completed on 31 December 1933.
  - It opened on **1 January 1934** with the Moon Troupe: 「宝三番叟」, the operetta 「巴里のアパッシュ」 and 『花詩集』.
  - Seating was **2,778** (later 2,476). Its proscenium was 5 *shaku* higher than the Grand Theatre's, then the largest in Japan, and it was the first theatre built with modern stage lighting.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東京宝塚劇場

### *Otokoyaku*: the word and the first stars
- **The word.** Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, sense ②, defines 男役 as 「演劇で男として登場する役。女優が男性に扮する場合に多くいう」. Its **earliest citation is 1886**: Toyama Masakazu 外山正一, 『演劇改良論私考』: 「男役を男が勤むる上は」. So the word predates Takarazuka. The date the theatre world first applied it to Takarazuka's women was **not found**. https://kotobank.jp/word/男役
- **The look.** Players began to dress as men in 1924, with the Grand Theatre and the revue format. At first they pinned up their long hair under oversized hats, which made their heads look big. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/男役
- **First short hair.**
  - At Shōchiku, Mizunoe Takiko cut her hair in September 1930 (or May 1931; see §1 SKD below).
  - At Takarazuka, **Kadota Ashiko 門田芦子** and **Saho Miyoko 佐保美代子** cropped theirs during rehearsals of 『ブーケ・ダムール』 (**August 1932**), and Kamiyo Nishiki 神代錦 and others followed.
  - **Kasugano Yachiyo** 春日野八千代 was the first at Takarazuka to wear false eyelashes and permed hair.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/男役 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- **First stars.**
  - Amatsu Otome 天津乙女 (1905–80), a Japanese-dance specialist, joined after the 1918 Tokyo debut.
  - In the 1930s the male-role pairs were Ashihara Kuniko 葦原邦子 with Sakura Hisako 桜緋沙子, then Sayo Fukuko 小夜福子 with Nara Miyako 奈良美也子.
  - Ashihara and Sayo were the top 二枚目男役 pairing of 1933.
  - Sekai Daihyakka; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- **Kobayashi on the *otokoyaku*** (『宝塚漫筆』, 1955): 「女から見た男役というものは男以上のものである。いわゆる男性美を一番よく知っている者は女である。その女が工夫して演ずる男役は、女から見たら実物以上の惚れ惚れする男性が演ぜられているわけだ」. He compared it explicitly with the kabuki *onnagata*, "a woman better than a woman". Yoshida 2012, p. 130.
- **Height today.** In 2019 the average *otokoyaku* was over 170 cm, and an applicant under 166 cm will rarely be cast as one. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/男役

### Fans, fan clubs and fan letters
- **The reader column.** *Kageki* 『歌劇』, founded **August 1918**, ran a reader-letters section. A review of Kotake's book calls it 「高声低声」 (**unverified**). The letters were blunt about audience manners, theatre manners and ticketing. Kotake Satoshi, 『宝塚少女歌劇、はじまりの夢』 (Shūeisha International, March 2023), https://www.kinokuniya.co.jp/f/dsg-01-9784797674248 (WebFetch summary)
- **The opera critic.** Aoyagi Yūbi 青柳有美, an opera authority, sent many letters to *Kageki* criticising the music. Readers turned against him. Yoshida 2012, p. 133.
- **Who the fans were.** Tsuganesawa Toshihiro's study of *Kageki* readers (1999) found that until the prewar years **male readers were by far the majority**. The audience's sex ratio **reversed in the late 1930s**. Baraniak-Hirata links the rise in women fans to the male role that took shape around 1930. Zuzanna Baraniak-Hirata, 「ポップカルチャーの中のタカラヅカ」, 『人間文化創成科学論叢』21 (2018), p. 232, https://teapot.lib.ocha.ac.jp/record/42510/files/24%20NINGENBUNKA-SOSEIKAGAKU-RONSO-21%2Cp231-240.pdf
- **Press warnings.** Robertson (1998, Japanese translation p. 194) found many popular-press articles warning that the all-female revue made girls "pathological", "fall into abnormality" (= same-sex love), and perhaps commit suicide. Baraniak-Hirata 2018, p. 232. Robertson herself was **not opened** (Project MUSE blocked the request).
- **The first official club.** The 宝塚女子友の会 was founded in **December 1934**; today it is the 宝塚友の会. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- **Private clubs.**
  - Until the early 1980s fans could hand food to performers, and performers sometimes invited fans to a café.
  - Private per-star clubs (「会」) organised from the **late 1970s**, to protect stars after the *Berubara* boom. Some fans had torn performers' clothes or taken their hair (Stickland 2008).
  - Club rituals: 入り待ち/出待ち (waiting at the stage door going in and out) and the uniformed 「ガード」 escort.
  - On **4 February 2026** the company officially recognised these clubs.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団 ; Baraniak-Hirata 2018, p. 233
- **Fan behaviour at the rival revue** (see SKD below): an 8,000-strong crowd at a Kyoto station (1933), and paper streamers thrown on stage.

### War and reopening
- **Wartime titles and organisation.**
  - War titles began in 1934 (『太平洋行進曲』).
  - The Star troupe was abolished in 1939.
  - In 1940 every member had to join the Dai Nippon Kokubō Fujinkai branch, foreign-language titles disappeared, and in **October 1940** the company became the 宝塚歌劇団, dropping 少女.
- **Wartime work.** From 1942 the company performed in Manchukuo. Members were also drafted into the 女子挺身隊 at the Kawanishi aircraft works.
- **Closure.** Theatres closed after **4 March 1944**. The last show was 『翼の決戦』 (with 『勧進帳』), and police drew swords to control the queue. The Grand Theatre became Navy barracks for *Kaiten* (suicide-torpedo) crews.
- **Reopening.** The Grand Theatre reopened on **22 April 1946** with 『カルメン』 and 『春のおどり』. Three classes, 69 girls who had joined during the war, danced the line dance. The Tokyo theatre was requisitioned as the Ernie Pyle Theatre until 1955 (reopened 15 April 1955 with 『虞美人』).
- Sources: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東京宝塚劇場 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚大劇場

### *The Rose of Versailles* on stage (1974)
- **The slump.** Around 1970 Takarazuka ran deficits and weekday houses were empty. The press joked that Takarazuka and the Braves baseball club were "Hankyū's two big burdens". In 1972 a retirement age of 57 was introduced to cut costs. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- **How the play came about.**
  - Hasegawa Kazuo had wanted his second Takarazuka production to be a Western piece. The playwright **Ueda Shinji** had heard from a fan about six months earlier that *Berubara* would suit Takarazuka.
  - Hasegawa at first objected: 「描かれているのは王妃の浮気の話。清く正しく美しく（が方針）の宝塚ではやったらあかん」.
  - Management resisted, because no manga had ever been staged successfully. Ikeda, a Takarazuka lover, agreed for 「安めの原作料」.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ベルサイユのばら_(宝塚歌劇)
- **The backlash before opening.** Once the casting was announced, fans of the manga wrote that a Japanese could not play the eight-heads-tall Oscar. **Ueda repeatedly received threatening letters containing razor blades.** Same page.
- **Hasegawa's direction.**
  - Hasegawa was a former kabuki *onnagata* turned film star. He worked out how to put **stars in the actors' eyes**: he found the second-balcony seat numbers where the light would catch the pupils and drilled the cast to send their gaze there.
  - He taught love scenes by playing both sexes himself.
  - Same page.
- **Opening and cast.** It opened on **29 August 1974** (Moon Troupe), paired with a Japanese piece, 『秋扇抄』.
  - Cast: Ōtaki Ko 大滝子 (Fersen), Hatsukaze Jun 初風諄 (Marie Antoinette), **Haruna Yuri 榛名由梨 (Oscar)**, Asō Kaoru 麻生薫 (André).
  - The 60th-anniversary flagship that year was not *Berubara* but 『虞美人』.
  - Kitamura 2014, pp. 52–53.
- **Audience.**
  - 1974–76: **about 1.4 million** (ja.wp), or **1.6 million** (en.wp, citing Cavaye & Senda 2004).
  - 1,500 performances by 9 January 2006; 4 million on 17 March 2006; **5 million on 27 June 2014**.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ベルサイユのばら_(宝塚歌劇) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rose_of_Versailles
- **Effects on the company.**
  - Applications to the music school went from about **5 to about 20 per place**, and the phrase 「東の東大、西のタカラヅカ」 appeared.
  - The 「ベルばら四強」 were Haruna, Ōtori Ran, Anna Jun and Migiwa Natsuko.
  - The run won the 1974 Arts Festival prize and the 1976 Kikuta Kazuo special prize.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
  - *Berubara* also began the fixed **top-star system**, which became formal after the 1989 revival. Baraniak-Hirata 2018, p. 233.
- **First Takarazuka-themed girls' manga.** 『ばらよ美しく咲け―宝塚スター物語』 (story by Ueda Shinji, art by Nagumo Keiko), *Weekly Margaret*, 1977. Baraniak-Hirata 2018, p. 233.

### The rival: Shōchiku's girls' revue (SKD) and Mizunoe Takiko
- **Osaka origins.** Shōchiku's Shirai Matsujirō saw Umemoto Rikuhei's dance 『春から秋へ』 at Takarazuka in 1921. In **April 1922** he founded the 松竹楽劇部生徒養成所 in Osaka, hiring Umemoto and the composer Harada Jun away from Takarazuka. Its 『春のおどり』 (April 1926) made it profitable. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/松竹歌劇団
- **Tokyo.** The Tokyo company was founded on **12 October 1928** with **14 pupils**, including Mizunoe Takiko (first intake).
  - 『東京踊り』 (April 1930) ran 32 days, and 「桜咲く国」 became its anthem.
  - By late 1930 there were over 100 members. In October 1930 the company split into two troupes and **the police issued directives against "suggestive" staging**.
  - Its magazine 『楽劇』 started in January 1931.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/松竹歌劇団
- **Mizunoe Takiko 水の江瀧子, "Tākī".**
  - Born Miura Umeko, 20 February 1915, Otaru; died 16 November 2009.
  - First short-haired male-role star: September 1930 (SKD's history) or 31 May 1931 (Nakayama Chinatsu).
  - Her private supporters' club, the 水の江会, began in **autumn 1931**. Its first pamphlet (1 January 1932) lists among its supporters the poet **Saijō Yaso**, the novelist Kawaguchi Matsutarō and the actress Yamada Isuzu.
  - The club grew to **about 20,000 members**, including in Korea and Taiwan. On 13 October 1935 it staged its own 「第3回タアキイ祭り」 on the scale of a company production.
  - Its pamphlet was later sold commercially as the fan magazine 『タアキイ』.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/水の江瀧子
- **The strike (桃色争議, 1933).**
  - It surfaced on **10 June 1933**. The company closed the theatre on 16 June and threatened to replace the revue with *naniwa-bushi* concerts.
  - Mizunoe was fired on 1 July.
  - On **12 July** police raided the strike headquarters on suspicion of *thought crime* and arrested 46, including Mizunoe; 35 were released the same day.
  - It was settled on **15 July** (SKD page) or **17 July** (Mizunoe page). The workers won almost every demand except menstrual leave. Mizunoe and 18 others were suspended for two months.
  - Mizunoe then gave a one-woman show at **Hibiya Kōkaidō on 20 September 1933**. The company had to take her back.
  - Her return vehicle, 『タンゴ・ローザ』, ran **160 performances**, then the record for a girls' revue.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/水の江瀧子 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/松竹歌劇団
- **Rivalry and fan frenzy.** In October 1932 Takarazuka played the Shinbashi Enbujō and Shōchiku answered with 『らぶ・ぱれいど』 across the Tsukiji River. The press framed it as 「踊る松竹、歌う宝塚」.
  - A Kyoto paper reported **"some 8,000 fans"** packing Shijō station and the Minamiza to see Mizunoe, Orie Tsusaka and Saijō Eriko arrive. Inside, girls shrieked "Tākī!" whenever she appeared. 京都日日新聞, quoted from 『近代歌舞伎年表京都篇』 p. 492.
  - Private clubs competed to throw paper streamers and bouquets on stage; 「劇場内は異常な興奮状態を示していた」.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/水の江瀧子 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/松竹歌劇団
- **Advertising.** Mizunoe advertised Kikkoman, Asahi beer, Meiji chocolate, Tombow pencils, Datsun (1936) and Canon. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/水の江瀧子
- **A critic's verdict.** Aochi Shin 青地晨 (1955): 「少女歌劇の過去、現在を通じて、タアキイほどのダイナミックな人気の所有者はなかった…いわば彼女は少女歌劇の象徴みたいな存在である」. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/水の江瀧子
- **Breakdown and wartime.** In 1939 she developed a fear of fans' eyes: she began seeing door-pulls as eyes, and 「アクセントのあるものは全て人の目に見える」. She went to the United States to recover. On her return, the authorities had **forbidden male dress**, so she played women. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/水の江瀧子 (citing her 1988 memoir)
- **The Kokusai Gekijō.** Shōchiku's **3,600-seat** Kokusai Gekijō opened in Asakusa in **July 1937**. Writers at the time said it was too big for the star-to-fan intimacy the revue had lived on.
  - The company disbanded on **31 March 1944**, and the theatre was turned into a **balloon-bomb factory**.
  - It restarted as SKD on 29 October 1945. **2.65 million** people attended in **1957**, its peak.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/松竹歌劇団

## 2. *Princess Knight* (『リボンの騎士』) and Tezuka's classics

- **Runs.** All four were published by Kōdansha. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/リボンの騎士

  | Version | Magazine | Dates |
  |---|---|---|
  | *Shōjo Club* | 『少女クラブ』 | **January 1953 – January 1956** |
  | Sequel (later titled 『双子の騎士』) | 『なかよし』 | January 1958 – June 1959 |
  | *Nakayoshi* remake | 『なかよし』 | **January 1963 – October 1966** |
  | *Shōjo Friend* (concept only; art by Kitano Hideaki) | 『少女フレンド』 | 1967, issues 24–29 |

- **Television.** Mushi Production / Fuji TV, **2 April 1967 – 7 April 1968**, 52 colour episodes, music by Tomita Isao.
  - The sole sponsor, **Sunstar**, pulled out after episode 13 because ratings missed the 20% target. Mushi Pro kept going rather than pay damages to stationery licensees promised a year-long run.
  - It was the **second Japanese TV anime with a girl protagonist**, after *Mahōtsukai Sarī*.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/リボンの騎士
- **Tezuka's own words.** He called the work his 「宝塚体験の総決算」. Takarazuka City Tezuka Osamu Memorial Museum, exhibition 『リボンの騎士 ３人のサファイアの物語』, 4 March – 25 June 2023, https://www.museum.or.jp/event/110980 (WebFetch summary)
- **The model for Sapphire.** Sapphire was modelled on the Takarazuka *musume-yaku* **Awashima Chikage 淡島千景**. Tezuka, a great fan of hers, saw her for once play a male role (probably Portia in *The Merchant of Venice*). He said so on TBS 『テレビ探偵団』, 10 January 1988.
  - Sapphire's TV voice, Ōta Yoshiko 太田淑子, was also ex-Takarazuka.
  - **Takarazuka itself has never staged it.**
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/リボンの騎士
- **Tezuka's Takarazuka childhood.** In 1933, aged 5, he moved to Takarazuka. His mother took him to the revue. Next door lived the top *otokoyaku* **Amatsu Otome** and her sisters. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/手塚治虫
- **Precedents.**
  - Matsumoto Katsuji's 『？（なぞ）のクローバー』 (**Shōjo no tomo, April 1934 supplement**), a masked girl swordfighter.
  - Ishida Eisuke's 『カナリヤ王子さま』 (*Shōjo Salon*, 1951), an earlier story manga for girls.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/リボンの騎士 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/松本かつぢ
- **Love between women in the series.**
  - In 『双子の騎士』 a cross-dressed girl already attracts women.
  - The *Nakayoshi* version has a **wedding between two women**, though there the attraction ends when the cross-dressed girl is revealed.
  - In *Berubara*, by contrast, love between women is presented as one possible form of love.
  - 押山美知子『少女マンガジェンダー表象論』 (2007), via https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/百合_(ジャンル)
- **Influence.** Before *Princess Knight*, girls' magazines were mainly text. Baraniak-Hirata describes 1950s–60s shōjo heroines as visibly modelled on Takarazuka *musume-yaku* and *otokoyaku* (for example Takahashi Macoto). Until *Berubara* the influence ran **one way**, from Takarazuka to manga. Baraniak-Hirata 2018, p. 232.
- **Tezuka's literary adaptations.**
  - 『ファウスト』: a tankōbon, Fuji Shobō, **15 January 1950**. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faust_(manga)
  - 『罪と罰』: Tōkōdō, **5 November 1953**. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_and_Punishment_(manga). The ja.wp Tezuka article puts it in **1952** (see Uncertain).
  - Tezuka read Goethe and Dostoevsky "dozens of times" from his family's Shinchōsha world-literature set. He adapted *Faust* **three times**: 『ファウスト』 (1950), the Japan-set 『百物語』, and the unfinished 『ネオ・ファウスト』 (1988).
  - He was a theatre-mad student. Around 1950 he joined the 関西民衆劇場 and played a **house-painter in a stage *Crime and Punishment***.
  - Natsume Fusanosuke sees Takarazuka in Tezuka's exaggerated poses and stage-set backgrounds.
  - In 1949 『拳銃天使』 had the first kiss in a children's manga.
  - Tezuka's last 描き下ろし tankōbon were 『バンビ』 and 『罪と罰』. After that, magazine supplements (付録) took over.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/手塚治虫

## 3. *The Rose of Versailles* (『ベルサイユのばら』)

- **Run.** *Weekly Margaret* (Shūeisha), **1972 no. 21 (21 May) to 1973 no. 52 (23 December), 82 instalments.** Kitamura 2014, p. 52 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ベルサイユのばら
- **Source.** Ikeda read Stefan Zweig's *Marie Antoinette* (1932) in the summer of her second year of high school.
  - She settled the title 『ベルサイユのばら』 while still in high school.
  - She took most of the historical matter from the Japanese translation (Misuzu Shobō Zweig zenshū vols. 11–12, 1962), which she lists first in her bibliography in vol. 9.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/池田理代子 ; https://bunshun.jp/articles/-/57215 (WebFetch summary) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ベルサイユのばら
- **Ikeda's background.**
  - Born 1947, Osaka. She dropped out of philosophy at Tokyo University of Education, joined the Communist-aligned Minsei (民青) and the student movement, and drew manga for rent.
  - She once lived three days on two 5-yen cakes of *fu*.
  - She debuted in 1967 (ja.wp) or 1968 (nippon.com).
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/池田理代子 ; https://www.nippon.com/ja/japan-topics/g02080/
- **Editors' resistance.** The all-male editorial staff objected: 「歴史物？ そんな高尚な題材が少女マンガの読者に理解されるはずがない」. Ikeda's reply was 「絶対に当てる。当たらなかったらすぐにやめる」. Women artists were paid about **half** what men were. https://www.nippon.com/ja/japan-topics/g02080/ (WebFetch summary)
- **How Oscar was made.**
  - The historical commander of the French Guards sided with the people on 14 July 1789. Ikeda had never been to France and had few sources, and 「隊長は女性に変えた方が自由に描けるなと考えました」 (bunshun). ja.wp gives a variant: she could not understand male psychology, so a cross-dressed woman was 「苦肉の策」.
  - Her face was based on **Björn Andrésen** (*Death in Venice*).
  - Oscar began as a **supporting character**. Reader response made her the protagonist (Shamoon 2012).
  - In a 2013 *Fujin Kōron* interview Ikeda linked Oscar to her anger at a society that did not recognise women's personhood and abilities (nippon.com).
  - Sources: https://bunshun.jp/articles/-/57215 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ベルサイユのばら ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rose_of_Versailles ; https://www.nippon.com/ja/japan-topics/g02080/
- **Readers.**
  - It was **first in the reader poll from the first instalment** (nippon.com).
  - An **Oscar fan club** formed during serialisation and issued its own bulletin, 『ばらベルサイユ』; parts are reproduced in 『ベルサイユのばら大事典』 (2002).
  - After Oscar and André died, sales fell. The *Margaret* of **4 November 1973** printed an editors' note saying they were flooded with letters begging that the two be brought back to life (Shamoon 2012, p. 127).
  - The editors limited the post-Oscar story to **10 instalments**, fearing a slump.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ベルサイユのばら ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rose_of_Versailles
- **Sales.**
  - 15 million copies (nippon.com, undated).
  - **Over 20 million** by December 2013 (ja.wp).
  - **Over 23 million worldwide** by 2022 (en.wp).
  - Hagio Moto credits its success with making tankōbon publication routine for serialised manga (en.wp).
- **TV anime (1979).** Nippon TV, **10 October 1979 – 3 September 1980**, Wednesdays 19:00, 40 episodes plus a recap. Shown in France and Italy as ***Lady Oscar***.
  - The first director, **Nagahama Tadao**, left after 13 episodes, after Oscar's voice actress Tajima Reiko objected to his overwrought direction. Episodes 14–18 had **no director**.
  - **Dezaki Osamu** 出崎統 took over and told the character designer Araki Shingo, 「これは美少女モノじゃない」. The art changed from episode 19.
  - For the love scene (episode 37) the staff took three weeks instead of one. The scriptwriter Sugie Keiko invented a **fireflies** scene to replace the manga's version.
  - Ikeda never watched the videos through, saying it tired her eyes.
  - Ikuhara Kunihiko names **Dezaki** (and Oshii Mamoru) as the directors who influenced him.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ベルサイユのばら ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/幾原邦彦
- **Lupin III crossover.** Oscar appeared in *Lupin III* ep. 101, 「ベルサイユは愛に燃えた」 (17 September 1979). The script came from a fan-submission contest for the 100th episode. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ベルサイユのばら
- **The live-action film (1979).**
  - Released **3 March 1979** by Tōhō. Director **Jacques Demy**, music Michel Legrand, Oscar played by the British newcomer **Catriona MacColl**, in English. Ikeda wrote the Japanese subtitles.
  - Producer Yamamoto Mataichirō raised **1 billion yen** from Shiseidō, Nippon TV and Tōhō. Shiseidō tied its spring-1979 cosmetics campaign to Oscar.
  - It earned **930 million yen** in distribution revenue, 7th among 1979 Japanese films, below expectations.
  - Satō Tadao (*Kinema Junpō*) said it was directed so "sheepishly" that Japanese could not enjoy it.
  - Kishi Keiko's 14-year-old daughter Delphine-Mayuko Ciampi refused the role.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ベルサイユのばら ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ベルサイユのばら_(映画)
- **Later honours.** Ikeda received the Légion d'honneur on 11 March 2009. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/池田理代子

## 4. Terayama Shūji and Tenjō Sajiki (1967–83)

- **Terayama Shūji 寺山修司.** Born **10 December 1935**, Hirosaki; died **4 May 1983** (sepsis, after cirrhosis and peritonitis). His father was a *Tokkō* political-police detective.
  - From the mid-1960s he was a 「青少年のカリスマ」: author of 『家出のすすめ』 (1963) and 『書を捨てよ、町へ出よう』 (1967).
  - He **judged high-school students' poems for Gakken's 『高三コース』** and **edited Shinshokan's girls' poetry imprint 「フォアレディース」**. One of his books is 『ひとりぼっちのあなたに』 (1965).
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/寺山修司
- **Founding of Tenjō Sajiki (天井桟敷).** Founded **1 January 1967** by Terayama, Yokoo Tadanori, Higashi Yutaka and Kujō Eiko. The name comes from Carné's *Les Enfants du Paradis*.
  - The recruiting ad read 「怪優奇優侏儒巨人美少女等募集」. Most early members were dropouts and runaways.
  - Debut: 『青森県のせむし男』, **18–20 April 1967**, Sōgetsu Kaikan, with Maruyama (Miwa) Akihiro. It proclaimed 「見世物の復権」.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/天井桟敷_(劇団)
- **Key productions.** All are Terayama's unless noted. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/天井桟敷_(劇団) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/毛皮のマリー

  | Year | Work |
  |---|---|
  | 1967 | 『大山デブコの犯罪』 (Shinjuku Suehirotei) |
  | 1967 | **『毛皮のマリー』**, a 女装劇 for Maruyama Akihiro. It opens with Snow White's mirror line, used four times; the title is from the chanson "La Marie-Vision". |
  | 1968 | **『星の王子さま（男装劇）』**: Saint-Exupéry as a *dansō* play. 『新宿版千一夜物語』; 『青ひげ』; 『「瞼の母」における愛の研究』 |
  | 1969 | 『時代はサーカスの象にのって』; 『犬神』 |
  | 1971 | 『邪宗門』 |
  | 1975 | 『ノック』 (30-hour street play); 『疫病流行記』 |
  | 1976 | 『阿呆船』 |
  | 1977 | 『中国の不思議な役人』 |
  | 1978 | **『奴婢訓』** (from Swift's *Directions to Servants*); **『身毒丸』**; 『観客席』 |
  | 1979 | 『レミング』; 『青ひげ公の城』 |
  | 1981 | 『百年の孤独』 (from García Márquez; later the film 『さらば箱舟』) |
  | 1982 | 『奴婢訓』 at the Palais de Chaillot, the last tour |

- **Tenjō Sajikikan.** **Tenjō Sajikikan**, Japan's first underground-only theatre (design by Awazu Kiyoshi), opened at Namikibashi, Shibuya, on **15 March 1969**. The first foreign tour followed: Frankfurt, **4–5 June 1969**. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/天井桟敷_(劇団)
- **Street theatre.**
  - 『人力飛行機ソロモン』 played across Takadanobaba and Shinjuku, **20–30 November 1970**.
  - The 30-hour 『ノック』 ran **from 3 p.m. on 19 April to 9 p.m. on 20 April 1975**. It used Suginami bathhouses, empty lots, private houses and the ward office as stages. Tickets were **maps** marked with times. Complaints brought the police.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/天井桟敷_(劇団)
- **A funeral for a manga character.** Rikiishi Tōru of 『あしたのジョー』 died in the 15 February 1970 *Shōnen Magazine* (on sale 30 January). After a flood of readers' letters, Terayama, as overall director, held a public funeral at Kōdansha's 6th-floor auditorium on **24 March 1970**, 3–5 p.m.
  - About **800 mourners** came, from schoolchildren to office workers.
  - The creators attended, and a Sōtō Zen priest presided.
  - It was reported as the first funeral ever held for a manga character.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/天井桟敷_(劇団)
- **A feud with Kara Jūrō.** In December 1969 Terayama sent a funeral wreath to the opening of Kara Jūrō's tent show. Kara's company raided Tenjō Sajikikan on the night of **12 December**, and **9** members of the two troupes were arrested. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/天井桟敷_(劇団)
- **Reworking old stories: *Shintokumaru* 『身毒丸』.** Co-written with Kishida Rio 岸田理生 and directed with J.A. Seazer.
  - It opened at Kinokuniya Hall, **22–26 June 1978**, with Shinjō Keiko and others.
  - It draws on the *sekkyō-bushi* 『しんとく丸』 and 『愛護若』: a father buys a new mother at a **「母を売る店」**, and the boy and stepmother end in forbidden love.
  - Behind it lies the Shuntokumaru legend: the Nō 『弱法師』, the jōruri 『摂州合邦辻』 (1773), and Orikuchi Shinobu's story 「身毒丸」 (1917).
  - Ninagawa Yukio restaged Kishida's revision in 1995 (Yomiuri Theatre Award). The 15-year-old Fujiwara Tatsuya played it in **London in 1997**.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/身毒丸_(舞台作品) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/俊徳丸
- **Films from older texts.** 『草迷宮』 (shot 1979 for the French omnibus *Collections privées*; released in Japan 11 November 1983) is from **Izumi Kyōka**. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/草迷宮_(映画)
- **End of the troupe.** It dissolved on **31 July 1983**. Its last show was 『レミング―壁抜け男』. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/天井桟敷_(劇団)
- **J.A. Seazer** (寺原孝明):
  - Born **6 October 1948**, Miyazaki; raised in Shizuoka. He came to Tokyo in **1969** and dropped out of Tokyo Designer Gakuin (where Takada Junji was a classmate).
  - He joined Tenjō Sajiki, and **from the early 1970s** was Terayama's right hand in music and direction. He scored 『書を捨てよ町へ出よう』 (1971), 『田園に死す』 (1974) and 『草迷宮』 (1979), and led the band 「シーザーと悪魔の家」.
  - In **1983** he founded 演劇実験室◉万有引力, and most members moved there. His later works include 『地下幻燈劇画 少女椿』 (1992).
  - He wrote the music for *Utena*'s soundtrack albums (1997–98).
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/J・A・シーザー ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/天井桟敷_(劇団)
- **Ikuhara and Tenjō Sajiki.** As a student Ikuhara was devoted to Tenjō Sajiki (天井桟敷に傾倒), which is how he came to know Seazer. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/幾原邦彦
- **A bridge to shōjo manga.** Kishida Rio, Terayama's co-writer, also wrote the screenplay of 『1999年の夏休み』 (1988), the film of Hagio's *Tōma* in which **girls play the four boys** (see §6). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/トーマの心臓

## 5. Nakahara Jun'ichi 中原淳一

- **Life.** Born **16 February 1913**, Kagawa; died **19 April 1983**, two weeks before Terayama. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/中原淳一
- **Discovery.** As a teenager he made French-style dolls. A show at **Ginza Matsuya (March 1932)** led to his debut as *Shōjo no tomo*'s house artist with the **June 1932** issue (covers, frontispieces, illustrations and supplements). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/中原淳一 ; Nipponica, https://kotobank.jp/word/中原淳一
- **Style.** He was influenced by Takehisa Yumeji. Girls'-magazine art was then mostly in a Japanese-painting, ukiyo-e-derived style, and his large-eyed, doll-like girls stood out. Ikeda Riyoko praised his unfocused eyes, with white showing beneath the iris: 「どこを見ているのかわからないような神秘的な魅力」. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/中原淳一
- **Work at *Shōjo no tomo*.**
  - Fashion page 「女学生服装帖」, May 1937 – May 1940.
  - Illustrations for Kawabata's 『乙女の港』 (1937–38).
  - The 1939 Jitsugyō no Nihon-sha edition of 『花物語』.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女の友
- **Removal (1940).** Under **military pressure** he left *Shōjo no tomo* after the **May 1940** issue. His girls were attacked as 「頽廃的で不健康」. Tanabe Seiko recalled schoolgirls hunting second-hand back issues with his covers 「タカラモノを発見したように」. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女の友 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/中原淳一
- **1940: shop and marriage.**
  - January: he opened a goods shop, **「ヒマワリ」**, in Kōjimachi.
  - June: he issued 『きものノ絵本』 by mail order.
  - **November: he married Ashihara Kuniko 葦原邦子**, Takarazuka's top male-role star. They had met through his covers for the Takarazuka magazine 『宝塚をとめ』, and his heroines of the period resemble her.
  - He was drafted in March 1945 and demobilised in August 1945.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/中原淳一
- ***Soleil* (『それいゆ』).** First issue **August 1946** (as *Soleil*, then ソレイユ, and それいゆ from no. 9). Quarterly, later bimonthly; **63 issues** to August 1960.
  - Early covers were by others, including **Fujita Tsuguharu** (no. 2). Nakahara drew nos. 3, 7 and 9–60.
  - Slogan: 「女性のくらしを新しく美しくする」.
  - In the first issue he wrote, roughly: a rose means nothing to a hungry dog, but we are human, so read this as you would put a flower in a window. This line is in a commented-out passage of the ja.wp source.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/それいゆ ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/中原淳一
- ***Himawari* (『ひまわり』).** January 1947 – December 1952, **67 issues**. It was a girls' magazine meant to lead into *Soleil*, started after the cartoonist Sugiura Yukio asked why he had no girls' magazine.
  - Serials included **Kawabata's 『歌劇学校』 (June 1949 – July 1950, illustrated by Nakahara)**, a Takarazuka-school novel, and Kawabata's 『万葉姉妹』 (1951).
  - It also ran Muraoka Hanako's translation of *The Secret Garden*.
  - It closed after circulation fell during Nakahara's year in Paris (1951–52).
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ひまわり_(少女雑誌) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ジュニアそれいゆ
- ***Junior Soleil* (『ジュニアそれいゆ』).** Trial issues in March and August 1953, then **July 1954 – October 1960, 34 issues**. Slogan: 「十代のひとの美しい心と暮らしを育てる」.
  - It chose a reader **「ミスター＆ミス・ジュニアそれいゆ」** every year, a way into show business.
  - Naitō Rune drew the covers from September 1959.
  - It closed after Nakahara's 1960 heart attack, when doctors ordered five years' rest.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ジュニアそれいゆ
- **Last years.** He launched 『女の部屋』 in 1970, but it lasted only 5 issues. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/中原淳一

## 6. Girls' manga and its revolutions

### The magazines
- **『少女倶楽部』** (Kōdansha), **January 1923**; renamed 少女クラブ in 1946; closed **1962** and succeeded by *Shōjo Friend*.
  - Noma Seiji wanted it to instil 「the inner modesty and fortitude of samurai women」. Unlike *Shōjo no tomo* and *Shōjo gahō*, it **never featured film or revue stars**, which won parents over.
  - Circulation: **67,000 in 1923 and 492,000 in 1937**, the largest of the girls' magazines (今田絵里香『「少女」の社会史』 2007).
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女クラブ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shōjo_Club
- **『少女の友』** (Jitsugyō no Nihon-sha), February 1908 – June 1955.
  - Its quality rose after **Uchiyama Motoi 内山基** became editor in **1931** (Nipponica).
  - It featured Takarazuka and foreign film stars.
  - Its letters page used a 「キミ・ボク言葉」 register, and some provincial girls' schools treated writing to it as delinquency.
  - The letters page created a readers' community. From the 1930s it held **readers' conventions**, and about **400** attended in **1938**.
  - Through the letters pages, readers' conventions, pen-pal letters and *dōjinshi*, *esu* friendships sometimes lasted beyond marriage (ja.wp エス).
  - Sources: https://kotobank.jp/word/少女の友 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女の友 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/エス_(文化)
- **『なかよし』** (Kōdansha): **December 1954** (January 1955 issue), **110 yen**, with supplements from the first issue.
  - *Candy Candy* (late 1970s) pushed it past **1.8 million**.
  - It reached **2.05 million in 1993** with Sailor Moon.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/なかよし ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/美少女戦士セーラームーン
- **『りぼん』** (Shūeisha): **3 August 1955**, **100 yen**, as the sister of 『少女ブック』. It was mostly manga by about 1958.
  - Circulation: **150,000 in 1955**; 1.35 million claimed in 1978; 2 million in 1986; **2.55 million around 1993–94**, the all-time record for a girls' manga magazine.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/りぼん
- **『週刊マーガレット』** (Shūeisha): **April 1963**. **650,000** copies of the first issue were given away free as publicity.
  - It ran 『アタックNo.1』 (1968) and 『ベルサイユのばら』.
  - Kōdansha had already launched the weekly 『少女フレンド』 (1962).
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/マーガレット_(雑誌) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女フレンド
- **『少女コミック』** (Shōgakukan): **April 1968**, monthly; twice a month in 1969; weekly from 1970. Editor **Yamamoto Jun'ya 山本順也** built it around the new artists (below). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女コミック ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/24年組

### Yamagishi Ryōko, 『白い部屋のふたり』 (1971)
- **Publication.** A one-shot in ***Ribon Comic*, February 1971**, a Ribon sister magazine for girls 16 and over that favoured "social issue" stories. It was printed as a cover feature, with its first page in full colour. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/白い部屋のふたり
- **Plot.**
  - At a French Catholic girls' boarding school, the gentle blonde Resine is paired with the rebellious dark-haired Simone. Simone recites **Rilke**'s love poems to her before the class.
  - For the school's 50th-anniversary play, ***Romeo and Juliet***, Simone (Romeo) kisses Resine (Juliet) passionately **on stage**.
  - Rumours drive Resine away. Simone provokes a man and is stabbed to death. Resine vows never to love again.
  - Same page.
- **Why girls rather than boys.** Yamagishi was drawn to Mori Mari's *tanbi* stories of men. She judged that a story of **love between girls would be easier for Ribon Comic's teenage readers to accept** (Yamagishi 2016, p. 151). Same page.
- **Context.** Similar stories of the time:
  - Watanabe Masako 『ガラスの城』 (1969–70);
  - Yashiro Masako 『シークレット・ラブ』 (1970), which some scholars call the first *yuri*;
  - Ikeda 『ふたりぽっち』 (1971);
  - Ichijō Yukari 『摩耶の葬列』 (1972);
  - Satonaka Machiko 『アリエスの乙女たち』 (1973–75).
  - Fujimoto Yukari argues it became the archetype ("the typical *yuri* story"). Almost all such 1970s stories end in **separation or death**, and *Yuri Shimai* later called the 1970s–80s the genre's "dark age".
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/白い部屋のふたり ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_(genre) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/百合_(ジャンル)

### The Year 24 Group (24年組)
- **Members.** Women born around Shōwa 24 (1949) who remade shōjo manga in the 1970s: Hagio Moto (1949), Takemiya Keiko (1950), Ōshima Yumiko (1947), Yamagishi Ryōko (1947), Aoike Yasuko, Kihara Toshie, Kimura Minori, Sasaya Nanae, Yamada Mineko and others.
  - The name began among artists and the critics' circle 「迷宮」, the founders of Comiket.
  - Its first appearance in print was 『鈴木光明の少女まんが入門』 (Hakusensha, 1979).
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/24年組
- **The Ōizumi salon.** Yamamoto Jun'ya, deputy editor of *Shōjo Comic*, went to Tokushima to recruit Takemiya. Hagio's rejected *Nakayoshi* manuscripts reached him through Takemiya.
  - From 1970 the two women shared a flat in Minami-Ōizumi, Nerima, arranged by Masuyama Norie 増山法恵. Yamamoto opposed it as "unheard of".
  - Satonaka Machiko (2004): without Yamamoto 「日本の少女漫画の発展は10年は遅れた」.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/24年組 ; https://bunshun.jp/articles/-/725 (WebFetch summary)
- **Hagio Moto, 『トーマの心臓』.** *Weekly Shōjo Comic* **1974 nos. 19–52**, 33 instalments, set in a German *Gymnasium*.
  - It was prompted by the French film ***Les amitiés particulières*** (『悲しみの天使』): Hagio was angry at its ending, in which one boy kills himself and the other merely regrets.
  - The first instalment came **last in the reader poll**, and the editor asked her to stop. Then the first printing of a *Poe Clan* volume, **30,000 copies, sold out in 3 days**. The series' poll rank rose to 5th–6th, and it ran to the end.
  - A precursor was 『11月のギムナジウム』 (1971).
  - Film: 『1999年の夏休み』 (dir. Kaneko Shūsuke, **26 March 1988**), with the four boys **played by girls** and voiced by voice actors; script by Kishida Rio.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/トーマの心臓
- **Takemiya Keiko, 『風と木の詩』.** *Weekly Shōjo Comic* **1976 no. 10**, moving to *Petit Flower* until June 1984. It won the Shōgakukan Manga Award for 1979.
  - It took **seven years** from conception (c. 1969–70) to publication, because editors kept refusing it.
  - The story: the Laconblade academy, a boarding *lycée* in Arles; the boy Gilbert, abused by his uncle **Auguste**, who is secretly his father; boy-boy sex, rape and incest.
  - Takemiya: 「当時はベッドで男女の足が絡まっているのを描いただけで作者が警察に呼び出されていましたが、私は作品を描く上で愛やセックスもきちんと描きたかったの。男×女がダメなら男×男でいけばイイと思ったの」.
  - In 1972 she, Masuyama, Hagio and Yamagishi made a research trip to Europe.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/風と木の詩
- **Masuyama's role.** Masuyama Norie promoted **「クナーベン・リーベ」**. She gave Takemiya and Hagio Hesse (*Beneath the Wheel*, *Demian*) and **Inagaki Taruho's 『少年愛の美学』 (1968)**, and she urged Takemiya to be 「少女漫画界で少年愛ものを描く最初の人」.
  - The result was Takemiya's 『サンルームにて』 (1970), the first *shōnen-ai* manga, which readers loved.
  - Masuyama said that when she first met them, the two had not known the word 少年愛.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少年愛_(少女漫画) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/風と木の詩
- ***JUNE*.** First issued as 『Comic Jun』 in **October 1978** by Sun Shuppan, from a plan by the student part-timer **Sagawa Toshihiko** for "mild porn for women", at **380 yen**. Its first editor had edited the gay magazine 『さぶ』.
  - It was renamed ***JUNE*** with the February 1979 issue, after the fashion brand JUN complained; an "E" was added to the already-printing title.
  - Over-printing (100,000+) caused heavy returns, and it was suspended after August 1979. Readers wrote that **"we'd pay up to 1,000 yen"**.
  - It relaunched in **October 1981** at **760 yen** with half the print run.
  - Takemiya joined to provide 「援護射撃」 for *Kaze to ki no uta*. Takemiya and Nakajima Azusa (Kurimoto Kaoru) ran critique columns that trained a generation of writers.
  - Peak circulation was about **80,000**; it closed with no. 85, **November 1995**.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/JUNE_(雑誌)
- **The word *yaoi*.** It dates from **1979**, from Sakata Yasuko's 坂田靖子 dōjin circle 「らっぽり」: 「ヤマなし・オチなし・イミなし」. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/やおい ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boys'_love

### How scholars explain the rise of *shōnen-ai* / BL
- **Creators' accounts.** Takemiya, quoted above: sex between a man and a woman got artists called in by police, so she moved it to two males. Yamagishi made the mirror-image choice in 1971 (girls, as more acceptable to teens). Masuyama wanted a "Knabenliebe" of high literary quality. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/風と木の詩 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/白い部屋のふたり ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少年愛_(少女漫画)
- **Early analyses: escape from gender.** These began with **Nakajima Azusa**, 『コミュニケーション不全症候群』. Women, they argued, are driven to it by misogyny: a 「女性が女性という性であること自体に対しての強い不適応感」, and the need for others' approval.
  - **Ueno Chizuko** called it a 「ジェンダーレス・ワールドの〈愛〉の実験」 (in 『発情装置』, 1998).
  - Japanese critics generally read it as a way to **keep sex away from one's own female body**.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/やおい ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boys'_love
- **Later analyses: gender as play.** From Nagakubo Yōko's 『やおい小説論』 (2005), the stress shifted from escape from oppression to **women playing with gender**. Sedgwick's "homosocial" theory became standard.
  - **Mizoguchi Akiko** (2000) criticised the genre's homophobia; she later (2007) traced its change and called it a "lesbian-feminist genre".
  - Gravett (2004) summed up four theories: boredom with male-female stories; *bishōnen* as a backlash against men's fantasies of girls; a safe space where the reader chooses whom to identify with; and the boys being read as girls.
  - Sources as above; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boys'_love
- **Why so tragic.** Fujimoto attributes the tragic 1970s *yuri* endings to patriarchy. Welker calls them "lesbian panic". Maser argues the end of Class S removed the only context in which such relationships were thinkable. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_(genre)

### *Yuri*: the word and the genre
- **The magazine.** 『薔薇族』, Japan's first commercial gay magazine, was founded in **July 1971**. Editor **Itō Bungaku 伊藤文學**, himself not gay, made it monthly in 1974. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/薔薇族 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/伊藤文學
- **The column.** Itō coined **百合族** as the counterpart of 薔薇族 and opened a women readers' column, **「百合族の部屋」**. It ran **irregularly from issue 46**. In **issue 50** Itō stated that 百合族 meant lesbians, but the column printed letters from women readers of all kinds. en.wp dates the column to **1976**.
  - Another explanation: the white lily answered the gay men's red rose, after the saying 「歩く姿は百合の花」.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/百合_(ジャンル) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_(genre)
- **Spread.**
  - **July 1983**: the magazine *Allan* began 「百合通信」, personal ads for "lesbiennes".
  - **1983**: the Nikkatsu Roman Porno film 『セーラー服 百合族』 made 百合 a general word for lesbians rather than a slang term.
  - **1990s**: 百合 was linked to lesbian porn manga (for example *Misuto*, 1996–99).
  - **Mid-2000s**: it became the genre name, after 『百合姉妹』 (2003) and 『コミック百合姫』 (2005).
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_(genre) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/百合_(ジャンル)
- **1990s works.** The tone brightened: Akisato Wakuni 『10回目の十戒』 (1991) and Ichijō Yukari 『だから僕はため息をつく』 (1993). Above all, **Sailor Moon's Haruka and Michiru** drew men into *yuri* fandom and prompted the first wave of *yuri* dōjinshi. Fujimoto: 「女」 had stopped being a minus sign. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/百合_(ジャンル)
- ***Maria-sama ga miteru* 『マリア様がみてる』** (Konno Oyuki 今野緒雪).
  - A short story in *Cobalt* (1997); vol. 1 in Cobalt Bunko on **24 April 1998**; the series ran 1998–2012.
  - The setting is the Catholic Lillian Girls' Academy (founded "1901" for daughters of the nobility). Pupils have *soeur* "sisters" sealed with a **rosary**. The student council officers are titled after **roses** (Rosa Chinensis, Rosa Gigantea, Rosa Foetida) and meet in the 「薔薇の館」.
  - Volume 1 hinges on a school-festival ***Cinderella*** in which the prince is to be played by a boys'-school council president.
  - Over **80% of readers are said to be men**. By 2010, **5.4 million** copies were in print.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/マリア様がみてる ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_(genre)

## 7. Class S and girls' fiction

- **The word and the custom.** *Esu* (エス, from "sister") named intense attachments between schoolgirls, or between a girl and a woman teacher, from the 1910s. Its rules were one-to-one, exclusive, pure and "mirror-like". https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/エス_(文化) ; details in 18-twentieth-century.md §2.
- **Yoshiya Nobuko 吉屋信子, 『花物語』.**
  - **Origin.** Yoshiya was then a children's-story writer. She **submitted (投稿)** the first story, 「鈴蘭」, to *Shōjo gahō*, and it appeared in the **July 1916** issue. She had earlier tried *Shōjo no tomo*, whose editor sent the manuscript back with a letter asking her to stop. Editor-in-chief Wada Koe 和田古江 promised a series.
  - **Planned as 7 stories.** Readers asked for more after the very first one, and kept asking after the seventh.
  - **The frame.** At first, girls gathered in a Western-style house each confess a sad story about a flower. Later stories took months each and became novelistic.
  - **Run.** Intermittently in *Shōjo gahō* **1916–1924**, then three stories in *Shōjo kurabu* (July 1925 – March 1926), illustrated by Nakahara. **54 stories**, usually counted as 52.
  - **Readers.** The future novelist **Sumii Sue** wrote to the readers' column about it as a schoolgirl. Critics have noted that its ornate prose resembles the style girls used in letters pages and pen-pal letters.
  - **Criticism.** Later stories of breakups, suicide and double suicide drew educators' criticism. The teenage Matsuda Keiko wrote that she was 「あきれもし、又なさけなくなった」.
  - **Films.** 『釣鐘草』 was filmed in **1935** (Shinkō Kinema, starring the Takarazuka actress **Kiritachi Noboru 霧立のぼる**) and in **1940** (Tōhō, Takamine Hideko). 『福寿草』 was filmed in 1935.
  - **Riffs.** **Tsuboi Sakae**'s 『私の花物語』 (early 1950s, in 『家庭朝日』, 『平凡』 and elsewhere) was a deliberate reworking: flower titles, but factory and shop workers instead of schoolgirls. Tanabe Seiko hunted the books in second-hand shops in the 1940s.
  - **Books.** Rakuyōdō edition 1920 (3 postcard-size volumes in a sepia case, with gold-dust lily-of-the-valley covers); Kōransha 1923–26 (5 palm-size volumes).
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/花物語_(吉屋信子)
- **『乙女の港』, Kawabata Yasunari and Nakazato Tsuneko.**
  - Published under Kawabata's name in *Shōjo no tomo*, **June 1937 – March 1938**, 10 chapters, illustrated by Nakahara. The book (Jitsugyō no Nihon-sha, **1 April 1938**, 330 pp.) reached its **47th printing within five years**.
  - The setting is a Yokohama mission girls' school (implied). The plot is an *esu* triangle among Michiko, Yōko and Katsuko.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/乙女の港
- **Nakazato's part.**
  - **Nakazato Tsuneko** 中里恒子 (1909–1987) had attended the Yokohama Catholic school 横浜紅蘭女学校. She wrote the **drafts**, and Kawabata corrected and rewrote them.
  - The proof is their letters, published in 『川端康成全集 補巻2』. Some drafts were found after her death, in 1989, and are held at the Kanagawa Museum of Modern Literature.
  - Kawabata, **14 September 1937**: the prose was getting rough, so please keep the opening tone. 「受けてゐる様子ゆゑ、なるべく続けていただきたい」, and a little more trouble in the triangle would help.
  - Kawabata, **16 October 1937**: 「戦争は入れないこととし、戦前のつもりにしたいと思ひますがいかがですか」.
  - Nakazato herself won the **Akutagawa Prize in February 1939**, the first woman to do so.
  - Critics disagree on the credit: Uchida Shizue holds it is not ghost-writing; Koyano Atsushi says it should be credited to Nakazato.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/乙女の港 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/中里恒子
- **Other Kawabata girls' stories.** In *Shōjo no tomo*: 『花日記』 (1938–39, which Nakazato also drafted), 『美しい旅』 (1939–41) and others. In *Shōjo kurabu*, nine short stories (1932–36). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女の友 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女クラブ
- **Censorship and the end of Class S.**
  - In **October 1938** the Home Ministry Police Bureau's book section issued the **「児童読物改善ニ関スル指示要綱」**. It told publishers to avoid "all vulgar expressions **and depictions of romantic love**", to cut back on manga, and to drop 「卑猥俗悪ナル漫画及ビ用語」.
  - Shamoon dates the decline of Class S to state censorship after 1937.
  - Fujimoto adds two postwar causes: **coeducation** ended the closed world of the girls' school, the prewar 「結婚までの避難所」, and free heterosexual courtship became normal.
  - Sources: https://artsandculture.google.com/story/is-reading-manga-a-bad-thing-to-do-ministry-of-economy-trade-and-industry-meti/LAVBJq82bdR_JA (WebFetch summary) ; search snippet citing 浅岡靖央 and 佐藤広美 (not opened) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_(genre) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/百合_(ジャンル)

## 8. *Sailor Moon*, Ikuhara Kunihiko, and the magical-girl genre

- **The first magical girl: 『魔法使いサリー』.** Yokoyama Mitsuteru's manga ran in ***Ribon*, July 1966 – October 1967**. It was first titled 『魔法使い**サニー**』 but was renamed after five instalments, because **Sony** held the trademarks on similar names and refused permission (Nissan's "Sunny" was licensed from Sony).
  - The TV anime (Tōei Dōga) ran on NET, Mondays 19:00, **5 December 1966 – 30 December 1968**, **109 episodes**. It was the **first Japanese TV anime with a girl protagonist** and the first of Tōei's 魔女っ子 series.
  - It was prompted by the hit of ***Bewitched*** (『奥さまは魔女』). Tōei's Watanabe Yoshinori persuaded Yokoyama.
  - The heroine is a princess of the magic kingdom living as a fifth-grader.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/魔法使いサリー
- **Sailor Moon: the manga.** Takeuchi Naoko's 『美少女戦士セーラームーン』 grew from her one-shot 『コードネームはセーラーV』, which arose when her editor Osano Fumio suggested "girls in sailor uniforms who fight" in early 1991. Takeuchi cited 『美少女仮面ポワトリン』 and the Super Sentai shows.
  - It ran in *Nakayoshi* from the **February 1992** issue to the **March 1997** issue. It was **first in the reader poll from the first instalment**, and first printings of the volumes passed **500,000**.
  - It won the 17th Kōdansha Manga Award (1993).
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/美少女戦士セーラームーン
- **The anime (TV Asahi / Tōei Dōga).** **7 March 1992 – 8 February 1997**, 200 episodes plus one special.

  | Season | Dates | Episodes |
  |---|---|---|
  | First | 7 Mar 1992 – 27 Feb 1993 | 46 |
  | R | 6 Mar 1993 – 12 Mar 1994 | 43 |
  | **S** | **19 Mar 1994 – 25 Feb 1995** | 38 |
  | SuperS | 4 Mar 1995 – 2 Mar 1996 | 39 |
  | Sailor Stars | 9 Mar 1996 – 8 Feb 1997 | 34 |

  - Average rating 11.6%; peaks of 16.3% (Kantō) and 21.7% (Kansai).
  - By March 2001, 2.8 million transformation toys had been sold.
  - In the first season the villains **Kunzite and Zoisite are a gay couple**. The deaths of all the heroines in episodes 45–46 led to newspaper letters saying shocked children had fallen ill.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/美少女戦士セーラームーン_(テレビアニメ)
- **Ikuhara's part.**
  - **Ikuhara Kunihiko** (b. **21 December 1964**) graduated from Kyoto College of Art (junior college) in 1985 and joined Tōei Dōga as a trainee in 1986, under Satō Jun'ichi.
  - He directed episodes from 1992 and became **series director from episode 60** of *R*. He directed *S* and *SuperS*, and the film 『劇場版美少女戦士セーラームーンR』 (1993).
  - He left Tōei after *SuperS* (1996) and founded **Be-Papas** with the series writer Enokido Yōji, his high-school friend.
  - The TV page says the series is full of people who love their own sex or close kin, owing to Ikuhara's 「シビアで寓話的な演出」.
  - He was in a **kendo club** at school. He cites Dezaki Osamu and Oshii Mamoru as influences. Anno Hideaki credited Ikuhara's advice for the theatrical turn in *Evangelion*.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/幾原邦彦 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/美少女戦士セーラームーン_(テレビアニメ)
- **Haruka (Sailor Uranus) and Michiru (Sailor Neptune).** They first appear in the manga's arc 3 and in anime *S*.
  - In the anime Haruka is a **「男装の麗人」**: first person 「僕」, flirtatious, calls girls 「子猫ちゃん」. In the manga she moves between male and female form.
  - They live together; she is a racer and pianist, Michiru a violinist.
  - The **hints of a sexual relationship came from Takeuchi**, whose first plan made Haruka the light and Michiru the shadow. Ikuhara added an episode of their first meeting.
  - Usagi and Minako fall for Haruka before learning she is a girl.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/天王はるか
- **Takeuchi on Takarazuka.** Haruka was originally to have been a **Takarazuka *otokoyaku***. Takeuchi called Takarazuka "the maximum level of feminine emancipation… I was inspired by them to create Haruka". She described the character as "the female best friend and the fairy tale prince in one".
  - Sources: Takeuchi, *Materials Collection* (Kodansha, Oct 1999); *Kappa* (Italy) Sept 1996; *AnimaniA* 30 (1999). All via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sailor_Uranus; the quotations are translations of translations.
- **Reception.**
  - Haruka was first in *Animage*'s 1995 favourite female character poll.
  - DiC's North American dub made the two **"cousins"**.
  - In Bandai's stage musicals (1993–2005), actresses played Haruka **in a boys' school uniform**.
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sailor_Uranus ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/天王はるか

## Context: the moral panics that fixed what girls' media could do

These real campaigns show what a "girl prince" classic would have had to survive.
- **1938.** The Home Ministry directive on children's reading (see §7). Over 50 manga books were reportedly banned by February 1939. This number comes from a search snippet and was **not opened**.
- **1955: the 悪書追放運動.** Prime Minister Hatoyama, in his policy speech of **22 January 1955**, called for the 「絶滅」 of harmful publications.
  - From May 1955 the Tokyo Federation of Mothers' Associations, whose police links were later acknowledged, ran a 「見ない、読まない、買わない」 campaign. It ended in **burning children's manga magazines** in schoolyards. Tezuka's work was among those burned.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/悪書追放運動 ; Google Arts & Culture / METI (above)
- **1970.** Nagai Gō's 『ハレンチ学園』 and the sex scene in Tezuka's 『アポロの歌』 were attacked; 『アポロの歌』 was banned in Fukuoka. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/有害コミック騒動
- **1990–92: the 有害コミック騒動.** In 1991 the Diet adopted petitions for regulation and Tokyo passed a resolution on harmful books. Publishers introduced the **「成年コミック」 mark** (first used on 30 January 1991). Ishinomori Shōtarō headed the 「コミック表現の自由を守る会」 (1992). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/有害コミック騒動

## Telling details

- **Momotarō was a girl from the first night.** At Takarazuka's first performance (1 April 1914, at a "wedding exhibition"), Takamine Taeko (born 1900, so 13 or 14) played Momotarō. The 16 girls' stage names were taken from the *Hyakunin Isshu* anthology. (Yoshida 2012; Nipponica)
- **"Otokoyaku" is older than Takarazuka.** The dictionary's first example of 男役 as a stage term is from 1886, in Toyama Masakazu's pamphlet on theatre reform. (Nihon Kokugo Daijiten)
- **One yen for a day out.** In 1924 a Takarazuka reserved seat, a spa ticket and a plate of curry rice cost 30 sen each. (ja.wp 宝塚歌劇団)
- **Fans versus the kabuki star.** In 1923 Takarazuka's fans forced Kobayashi to break a promise to lend the new Grand Theatre to Onoe Kikugorō VI. Fans also helped kill the plan to add male actors. (Yoshida 2012; Kotake 2023)
- **A man's audience until the late 1930s.** Readers of Takarazuka's own magazine were mostly men until the late 1930s, when women overtook them. The change came with the cropped-hair male lead. (Tsuganesawa 1999, via Baraniak-Hirata)
- **The scissors in rehearsal.** Takarazuka's first short-haired *otokoyaku*, Kadota Ashiko and Saho Miyoko, cut their hair during rehearsals in 1932, two years after their Shōchiku rival. (ja.wp 男役)
- **"I'm Mizunoe Tākī!"** In 1933 about 8,000 fans jammed a Kyoto station to see her arrive. By 1939 she saw eyes in the door-pulls of her house and fled to America. (ja.wp 水の江瀧子)
- **A strike by girls, raided as thought crime.** On 12 July 1933 police arrested 46 striking revue performers, the 18-year-old Mizunoe among them, on suspicion of thought crime. Ten weeks later she sold out Hibiya Kōkaidō alone, and the company had to take her back. (ja.wp 水の江瀧子)
- **A revue house turned bomb factory.** Shōchiku's 3,600-seat Kokusai Gekijō, built for the girls' revue, made paper balloon bombs in 1944–45. (ja.wp 松竹歌劇団)
- **Razor blades for *The Rose of Versailles*.** Before the 1974 opening, the playwright Ueda Shinji repeatedly received letters with razor blades from manga fans who said no Japanese could play Oscar. (ja.wp ベルサイユのばら (宝塚歌劇))
- **Stars in the eyes by seat number.** Hasegawa Kazuo, a former kabuki *onnagata*, found the balcony seats where stage light would make the actresses' eyes sparkle like manga eyes. He made them aim their gaze at those seat numbers. (same)
- **"Bring them back to life."** Two weeks after Oscar died, the *Margaret* of 4 November 1973 printed an editors' note about the flood of letters begging for her and André's resurrection. Readers had their own Oscar fan club and bulletin. (en.wp; ja.wp)
- **A real funeral for a fictional boxer.** On 24 March 1970 about 800 people, schoolchildren to salarymen, attended Terayama's funeral for the manga boxer Rikiishi Tōru at Kōdansha. A Zen priest presided. (ja.wp 天井桟敷)
- **A play staged across a whole ward.** In April 1975 Terayama ran a 30-hour play across Suginami: in bathhouses, empty lots, private homes and the ward office. The tickets were maps, and the police came. (same)
- **The Little Prince in drag, 1968.** Terayama staged *The Little Prince* as a 男装劇 (cross-dressed play), and *Kegawa no Marie* opens with Snow White's mirror. (ja.wp 天井桟敷, 毛皮のマリー)
- **A mother bought in a shop.** In *Shintokumaru* (1978) the father buys a stepmother at a "shop that sells mothers". The tale is a medieval *sekkyō* ballad already retold by Nō, puppet theatre and Orikuchi Shinobu. (ja.wp 身毒丸)
- **The painter married the leading man.** Nakahara Jun'ichi, banned from *Shōjo no tomo* in 1940 for "decadent" big-eyed girls, married the Takarazuka male-role star Ashihara Kuniko that November. They had met through his covers for a Takarazuka magazine. (ja.wp 中原淳一)
- **Kawabata wrote a Takarazuka-school novel.** 『歌劇学校』 ran in Nakahara's *Himawari*, 1949–50. (ja.wp ひまわり)
- **"Leave the war out."** In October 1937 Kawabata told his ghost-writer Nakazato Tsuneko to keep the war out of 『乙女の港』 and set it "before the war". He also advised that the girls' love triangle should "get a little more tangled", because it was "going over well". (ja.wp 乙女の港)
- **A wedding between two women in 1960s *Nakayoshi*.** Tezuka's 1963–66 *Princess Knight* has a wedding between two women. Its 1958–59 sequel already shows a cross-dressed girl attracting women. (押山 2007, via ja.wp 百合)
- **A masked girl swordfighter in 1934.** Matsumoto Katsuji's 『？のクローバー』 was a *Shōjo no tomo* supplement nearly 20 years before *Princess Knight*. (ja.wp 松本かつぢ)
- **Romeo and Juliet, both girls, 1971.** In the founding *yuri* manga, the heroines play Romeo and Juliet in a school play. Romeo's real kiss on stage starts the rumours that destroy them. (ja.wp 白い部屋のふたり)
- **Last in the poll.** The first instalment of 『トーマの心臓』 came last among readers. It was saved because 30,000 copies of the author's vampire book sold out in three days. (ja.wp トーマの心臓)
- **"If man and woman is banned, then man and man."** Takemiya Keiko on why she drew boys: artists were called in by the police for drawing a man's and a woman's legs tangled in bed. (ja.wp 風と木の詩)
- **"We'd pay 1,000 yen."** When *JUNE* folded in 1979, readers wrote that they would pay up to 1,000 yen. It came back at double the price, 760 yen. (ja.wp JUNE)
- **Lily against rose.** The word *yuri* was coined by the straight editor of a gay men's magazine, for a women's letters column called "The Lily Tribe's Room". A 1983 porn film, 『セーラー服 百合族』, then made it common usage. (ja.wp 百合)
- **Sony stopped "Sunny".** The first magical girl was renamed Sally after five instalments, because Sony held the trademark on "Sunny". (ja.wp 魔法使いサリー)
- **Haruka was meant to be a Takarazuka star.** Takeuchi's first idea for Sailor Uranus was a Takarazuka *otokoyaku*, "the female best friend and the fairy tale prince in one". (en.wp Sailor Uranus)
- **The director of *Utena* was a Tenjō Sajiki devotee and a kendo boy.** Ikuhara was devoted to Terayama's troupe as a student and was in his school's kendo club. He names Dezaki Osamu, who took over the *Rose of Versailles* anime in mid-run, as an influence. (ja.wp 幾原邦彦)
- **Two deaths in 1983.** Nakahara Jun'ichi died on 19 April 1983 and Terayama Shūji on 4 May 1983, two weeks apart.

## Uncertain / conflicting

1. **Grand Theatre capacity, 1924.** Sources give three figures:
   - **3,000**: ja.wp 宝塚歌劇団; Sekai Daihyakka.
   - **3,500 seats**: ja.wp 宝塚大劇場.
   - **4,000**: Kitamura 2014.
   - The opening month is July 1924 per the ja.wp chronology. The exact day was not found.
2. **Tokyo Takarazuka Theatre capacity.** **2,778** seats (ja.wp table, later 2,476) vs "3,000" (Kitamura 2014, who also misprints the year as 「1934（大正9）年」).
3. **The word *otokoyaku* at Takarazuka.** The 1886 dictionary citation is certain. When Takarazuka or the press first applied 男役 to its actresses was **not found**.
4. **The *Kageki* readers' column named 「高声低声」.** This comes only from a WebFetch summary of a bookseller page reporting a review. Kotake's book itself was not opened.
5. **Takarazuka's first performance date.** ja.wp says 1 April – 31 May 1914 (prose) but "March 1914" (chronology). Kitamura and Yoshida both say April 1914. A search snippet gives the end date as 30 May.
6. **The music school's founding month.** January 1919 (ja.wp, citing 『90年史』) vs December 1918 (Nipponica).
7. **Amatsu Otome's entry.** Sekai Daihyakka says she joined after the 1918 Tokyo debut. Her exact entry year and her status as the "first *otokoyaku* star" were not checked.
8. **The 1933 Shōchiku strike settlement.** 15 July (ja.wp 松竹歌劇団) vs 17 July (ja.wp 水の江瀧子, citing Nakayama 1993). The Kyoto "8,000 fans" report is dated only 「二十八日」, during the 『タンゴ・ローザ』 run at the Minamiza (late 1933). The month was not established.
9. **The wartime ban on male dress** (Mizunoe's memoir) is still unverified: the issuing authority, date and scope are unknown (as in file 18).
10. **Tezuka's 『罪と罰』.** 5 November 1953, Tōkōdō (en.wp; a search snippet of Kōdansha's collected-edition data) vs **1952** (ja.wp 手塚治虫, which pairs it with 『バンビ』). ja.wp has no separate article on it, and Tezuka's official site page was not found. 1953 is the better-supported date.
11. ***Rose of Versailles* figures.**
    - Sales: 15 million (nippon.com), 20 million by December 2013 (ja.wp), 23 million worldwide by 2022 (en.wp).
    - Takarazuka audience 1974–76: 1.4 million (ja.wp) vs 1.6 million (en.wp, Cavaye & Senda).
    - Ikeda's debut: 1967 (ja.wp, 『バラ屋敷の少女』) vs 1968 (nippon.com).
    - Reason for making Oscar a woman: "to draw freely" (bunshun 2022 interview) vs "I couldn't understand male psychology" (ja.wp). These are compatible.
12. **The 1979 film's credits.** ja.wp's infobox lists **Agnès Varda** as 製作総指揮 (executive producer). This was not checked against another source and should be treated with care.
13. ***Ribon*'s peak.** 2.55 million is given for both "end of 1993" and "1994" on the same ja.wp page. The industry estimates (出版指標年報) are lower: 2.28 million (1993) and 2.30 million (1994).
14. **The *yuri* column.** en.wp dates 「百合族の部屋」 to 1976. ja.wp gives only "from issue 46, irregular; meaning declared in issue 50", without dates. *Barazoku* began in July 1971 as a bimonthly and went monthly in 1974, so issue 46 falling in 1976 is consistent but **inferred**. Whether this was the first use of 百合 for lesbians is uncertain (en.wp says so too).
15. ***Hana monogatari* "began as a reader submission".** It was a 投稿 (unsolicited submission) by Yoshiya, who was already publishing children's stories. Nothing shows it came through a readers' letters column as such. The count of stories (54 vs 52) and the end date (1924, or 1926 counting *Shōjo kurabu*) depend on the edition.
16. **Nakajima Azusa's 『コミュニケーション不全症候群』.** ja.wp gives 1995. The first edition is commonly dated **1991** (Chikuma Shobō), which was not checked.
17. **Seazer's join date.** He came to Tokyo in 1969 (ja.wp J・A・シーザー). ja.wp 天井桟敷 says he was Terayama's right hand "from the early 1970s". The exact year he joined was not pinned down.
18. **Takeuchi's Takarazuka quote about Haruka** is an English rendering, apparently via the Italian *Kappa* magazine and a German interview. The Japanese original in the *Materials Collection* was not seen.
19. **1938 directive details.** "Over 50 manga banned by February 1939" comes only from a search-result snippet citing Japanese papers, which were not opened (the TMU PDF would not render). The "romantic love" clause is from the Google Arts & Culture / METI page.
20. **Robertson's *Takarazuka* (1998)** was cited only through Baraniak-Hirata 2018; Project MUSE blocked the request. A 2024 IAFOR paper on Takarazuka fan letters of the 1920s–40s could not be fetched (Cloudflare); per its search snippet, fans did not divide favourites by male or female roles.
