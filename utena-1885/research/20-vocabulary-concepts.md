# 20 — Key words and concepts in Japanese, 1850–1930 (real history only)

Purpose: to judge how an 1880s Japanese readership would understand the elements of a *Revolutionary Girl Utena*–type story. Real history only; no alternate-history speculation.

**Method and limits.** I opened every page cited here. Most dictionary data comes from Kotobank, which reproduces *Seisen-ban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten* (精選版 日本国語大辞典, "NKD" below), *Digital Daijisen*, *Nipponica* (日本大百科全書), *Sekai Daihyakka* (世界大百科事典) and others. NKD's 初出の実例 (earliest example) is the earliest example the dictionary found. It is not proof of first use, but it is the best single guide to when a word appears. Bibliographic data for 1880s titles comes from NDL Search (国立国会図書館サーチ) catalogue records. Partway through, the shared web-search budget for this run ran out. After that I could only open pages whose addresses I already knew (Kotobank entries, ja.wikipedia articles, repository PDFs). A few items therefore stay unverified, and they are listed under "Uncertain / conflicting."

**Date convention.** Before 1 Jan 1873 (= Meiji 5/12/3), dates are given as the Japanese lunar date plus the Gregorian date, where the source supplies both.

## 1. "Shōjo" 少女 and its rivals (otome, musume, jogakusei); the shōnen/shōjo split

### Classical and Edo usage
- **少女 (せうぢょ, shōjo), general sense.** NKD defines it as "a young female; otome; musume; also the younger of sisters." The earliest example is 日本三代実録 (*Nihon sandai jitsuroku*), Jōgan 9/10/4 (867): 「僕有一少女。願令薦枕席」 ("I have a young daughter; I wish her to serve your pillow"). Next is 名語記 (1275): 「少女のひなあそびとて、人形をつくりすへたる」, about girls' doll play. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%B0%91%E5%A5%B3-531947
- **少女 as a legal age-grade under the ritsuryō codes.** It meant women aged 17 to 20 (later 21). NKD quotes the Taihō 2 (702) household register of 御野国 (Mino), 味蜂間郡春部里: 「次牟依売 年十九少女 次真依売 年十七少女 次床嶋売 年十五小女」. A 15-year-old was 小女, a 17–19-year-old was 少女. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%B0%91%E5%A5%B3-531947 ; also ja.wikipedia 少女 (citing 大辞泉) https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女
- **少女 was often read をとめ (otome).** The NKD entry おとめ【少女・乙女】 (same Kotobank page) gives these senses:
  - (1) an unmarried woman of marriageable age. Later the word widened to "unmarried girls from about 10 to adulthood." Examples: 古事記 (712) 「阿那邇夜志愛袁登売袁」; 南総里見八犬伝 (1814–42) 「未通女(ヲトメ)」.
  - (2) a little girl (色葉字類抄, 1177–81).
  - (3) a celestial maiden (Henjō, 古今集 905–914: 「をとめの姿しばしとどめん」).
  - (4) a Gosechi dancer (源氏物語).
  - It is also the title of *Genji* chapter 21, 「少女 (Otome)」.
  - NKD's 語誌 (usage notes): をと is cognate with をつ ("to rejuvenate"). Anciently をとこ/をとめ were a pair. When をとこ came to mean "man" in general, をとめ came to be written 少女 and to mean celestial maidens and shrine maidens. The 乙 in 乙女 is an *ateji* (a character borrowed for its sound), used once the お/を distinction was lost. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%B0%91%E5%A5%B3-531947 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E4%B9%99%E5%A5%B3-453512
- **少年 (shōnen) was not gendered.** NKD gives 少年 ① as "young person… used of both sexes, but now usually of boys" (田氏家集 c.892). As late as Shimazaki Tōson's 破戒 (1906), the phrase was 「男女の少年は今、小学校を指して急ぐのであった」 ("young people of both sexes"). NKD ③ is Edo usage: "a boy who is the partner in male–male love; *wakashu*" (haikai 新花摘, 1784: 「少年の矢数問寄る念者ぶり」). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%B0%91%E5%B9%B4-532874
- **処女 (shojo), "a woman who stays at home."** It meant an unmarried woman, a virgin, read おとめ or きむすめ: 文華秀麗集 (818); 談義本 艶道通鑑 (1715) 「媒なくて処女(ショヂョ)を犯は…」. 処女作, 処女航海 and 処女地 are later calques from European languages (処女演説 attested 1908). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%87%A6%E5%A5%B3-534653
- **娘 (musume).** Its senses are "one's daughter" (日本書紀 720) and "unmarried young woman" (枕草子). In Edo, in the Fukagawa pleasure quarter, 娘 also meant women who received customers and handled courtesans (洒落本 辰巳之園, 1770). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A8%98-530512

### Early-Meiji rivals
- **女学生 (jogakusei).** NKD's earliest example is *Shinbun zasshi* (新聞雑誌) no. 40, "明治五年（1872）四月": 「冀は邦内四方の女学生能々注意し勉励ありたし」. That is lunar Meiji 5/4, roughly May–June 1872 Gregorian. So "jogakusei" is a Meiji coinage that was current from the very first girls' schools. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A5%B3%E5%AD%A6%E7%94%9F-533941
- **The first government girls' school was 東京女学校, called 竹橋女学校.** The Ministry of Education announced it in January 1872 (lunar Meiji 4/12) and opened it in March 1872 (lunar Meiji 5/2). It admitted girls from 7 to 15 years old, "from kazoku down to commoners." The age range was revised in 1874 to 14–17. It closed on 19 Feb 1877 for lack of funds after the Satsuma Rebellion. At its opening the 太政官正院 ordered that girls' dress include **samurai men's hakama** (士分男子の袴). This is described as "the starting point of women's dress in modern schooling" (citing 岩崎雅美, 日本家政学会誌 44(1), 1993). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東京女学校
- **The 1872 ban on women's short hair.** Some women understood the 1871 散髪脱刀令 (hair-and-sword decree) to mean they too should crop their hair. Tokyo-fu therefore issued a 女子断髪禁止令 on **Meiji 5/4/5 (11 May 1872)**. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/断髪令
- **1880s girls' print culture used "jogaku," "joshi" and "jogakusei," not "shōjo."**
  - 女学雑誌 (*Jogaku zasshi*) ran from July 1885 to Feb 1904 (526 issues). It was a Christian women's-enlightenment magazine, edited from no. 30 by 巌本善治 (Iwamoto Yoshiharu), principal of 明治女学校. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A5%B3%E5%AD%A6%E9%9B%91%E8%AA%8C-80055
  - 明治女学校 was founded in 1885 by 木村熊二. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%98%8E%E6%B2%BB%E5%A5%B3%E5%AD%A6%E6%A0%A1-141081
  - Kume Yoriko's dissertation summary speaks of 明治20年代 (around 1890) as the time 「少女がまだ女子と呼ばれていた」, "when girls were still called *joshi*." https://jwu.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/1711/files/2013_%E4%B9%9961_%E4%B9%85%E7%B1%B3%E4%BE%9D%E5%AD%90.pdf
- **藪の鶯 (*Yabu no uguisu*) by 田辺花圃 (Tanabe Kaho, later 三宅花圃), Kinkōdō, June 1888.** It is regarded as the first modern novel by a Japanese woman. She wrote it while a pupil at 東京高等女学校, after reading Tsubouchi's 当世書生気質 and thinking "I could write this." It satirises Rokumeikan-era Westernised girls. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/藪の鶯 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E8%97%AA%E3%81%AE%E9%B6%AF-1820289

### When shōnen and shōjo split
- **Children's magazines of the 1880s addressed both sexes under "shōnen."** The founding of 少年園 (*Shōnen-en*) in 1888 was followed by a string of titles carrying 少年. Their sense of a gendered readership was weak, and they addressed 「少年男女」 ("young people, male and female"). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女雑誌
  - Kume Yoriko shows that 少年園 (1888–95) did *not* exclude girls. It catered to girls in secondary schooling, but after the Imperial Rescript on Education (1890) and with the approach of the Sino-Japanese War, articles for girls shrank and old 女徳 (female virtue) teaching returned. https://jwu.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/1711/files/2013_%E4%B9%9961_%E4%B9%85%E7%B1%B3%E4%BE%9D%E5%AD%90.pdf
  - Other early youth magazines: 穎才新誌 (*Eisai shinshi*, 1877) was the first. Then 少年園 (1888), 日本之少年 (1889) and 小国民 (1889). 大橋眞由美, OMU repository: https://omu.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/4901/files/2012000112.pdf
- **The split is usually dated to September 1895 (Meiji 28/9).** In that month Hakubunkan's 少年世界 (*Shōnen sekai*, founded 1895) opened a girls-only column titled 「少女」. Kume: 「それ以後、年少者全体を指す言葉であった「少年」から、「少女」の分離が明示される」. Early contributors included Wakamatsu Shizuko, whose 「着物の生る木」 appeared in Meiji 28/9. https://jwu.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/1711/files/2013_%E4%B9%9961_%E4%B9%85%E7%B1%B3%E4%BE%9D%E5%AD%90.pdf ; 渡辺貴規子 (Osaka Univ. 言語文化の比較と交流 9, 2022): 「『少年世界』の紙面に女児専用の記事を集めた「少女欄」が開設され（1895年9月）、やがてそれが『少女世界』の創刊（1906年）へと発展する」. https://ir.library.osaka-u.ac.jp/repo/ouka/all/88375/gbkp_2021_g9.pdf
- **The 高等女学校令 (Girls' High School Order) was Imperial Ordinance No. 31 of Meiji 32.** It was promulgated 7 Feb 1899 (published in the Kanpō 8 Feb) and took effect 1 Apr 1899. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/高等女学校令 . Watanabe treats it as the background to the sharp rise in jogakusei numbers that fed the girls' magazines. https://ir.library.osaka-u.ac.jp/repo/ouka/all/88375/gbkp_2021_g9.pdf
- **少女界 (*Shōjo kai*), Kinkōdō, first issue 11 Apr 1902 (Meiji 35/4).** It was the first magazine with 少女 in its title. Kinkōdō had launched 少年界 in Feb 1902 and the girls' magazine two months later. https://omu.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/4901/files/2012000112.pdf ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女雑誌 . Its editorial staff in 1902–03 included **Makiguchi Tsunesaburō** (牧口常三郎, later founder of Sōka Gakkai), writing as 「澎湃」. 岩木勇作, 創価教育 17: https://soka.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2000276/files/sokakyoiku0_17_07.pdf
- **Later titles:**
  - 少女世界 (*Shōjo sekai*), Hakubunkan, first issue Sept 1906 (Meiji 39/9), the sister magazine of 少年世界; ran 1906–31.
  - 少女の友 (*Shōjo no tomo*), 1908.
  - 少女 (女子文壇社), 1909.
  - 少女画報 (*Shōjo gahō*), 1912.
  - 少女倶楽部 (*Shōjo kurabu*), 1923.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女雑誌 ; https://ir.library.osaka-u.ac.jp/repo/ouka/all/88375/gbkp_2021_g9.pdf
- **Imada Erika (今田絵里香), 『「少女」の社会史』 (Keisō shobō 2007; new ed. 2022).** Her argument: 「少女」 was a category of the **urban new middle class**. School created "girlhood" as a span of time, and girls' magazines gave it images. Girls' high schools held the shōjo "cut off from the future," placed between 幼女 (little girl) and 人妻 (wife). Her periodisation of magazine imagery:
  - 1895–1910: girls protected by mothers; boys studying and playing sports.
  - 1910–20: "non-child" girls.
  - 1920–30: girls playing sports.
  - 1930–45: militarised girls and boys.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女 (summarising Imada)
- **Honda Masuko (本田和子, b. 1931), main works:** 『異文化としての子ども』 (1982), 『少女浮遊』 (1986), 『少女論』 (1988), 『女学生の系譜――彩色される明治』 (1990), 『少女へのまなざし』 (1993). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/本田和子
- **Kawamura Kunimitsu (川村邦光), main works:** the "Otome" trilogy, 『オトメの祈り――近代女性イメージの誕生』 (1993), 『オトメの身体』 (1994) and 『オトメの行方』 (2003). The katakana オトメ marks his topic as the modern ideal of the pure maiden. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/川村邦光
- **Upshot for an 1880s reader.** 少女 was a literary or classical written form, readable as せうぢょ or をとめ. It was not a social category. Schoolgirls were 女学生 or 女子, and girls read magazines addressed to 少年. The "shōjo" as a named readership and type is a product of 1895–1906.

## 2. "Kakumei" 革命

### Classical meaning
- **Classical sense.** From the *Yijing* 革卦: 「湯武命を革め、天に順ひて人に応ず」. The heavenly mandate changes, so the dynasty changes (易姓革命). NKD's earliest Japanese example is 本朝文粋 (c.1060), 三善清行's letter to Sugawara no Michizane: 「天道革命之運、君臣尅賊之期」. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%9D%A9%E5%91%BD-43816
- **Calendrical sense.** In 暦道 (calendar science) 革命 is one of the 三革: the **辛酉 year**, a year of upheaval. Era names were changed to avert it (北山抄 1012–21; 史記抄 1477: 「前代を改を革命と云ぞ。嘉吉元年辛酉は革命之年ぞ」). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%9D%A9%E5%91%BD-43816
  - This usage survived to the eve of Meiji. NDL holds a manuscript dated **Man'en 1 (1860)**, 『勘申明辛酉當革命否事』, which deliberates whether the coming 辛酉 year (1861) counts as a "kakumei" year. https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/books/R100000136-I1971430859767783590 . Similar 1801 and 1804 deliberations are also catalogued.
- **NKD 語誌 (usage note).** Until Bakumatsu the word kept its original dynastic-change sense. Edo Confucian debates over Mencius's 湯武放伐論 (whether armed overthrow was justified) prepared the modern sense, and "the Meiji 革命 should be seen as *reborn* as the translation of revolution." NKD names **Fukuzawa Yukichi** as the probable chooser of 革命 for "revolution." https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%9D%A9%E5%91%BD-43816

### Earliest attestation as "revolution"
- **NKD sense ②, the modern political sense.** Its earliest example is Fukuzawa, 西洋事情 (*Seiyō jijō*, 1866–70), 外編: 「兵乱に由て俄に政府の革まるを革命と云ひ」 ("when a government is suddenly changed through armed upheaval, this is called *kakumei*"). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%9D%A9%E5%91%BD-43816
- **Who spread it.** The JapanKnowledge column (from the NKD editors) says that Fukuzawa chose the word, but those who **spread it** were 「中江兆民ら、自由党系列の自由民権運動家たち」 ("Nakae Chōmin and other Freedom and People's Rights activists of the Liberal Party line"). Hence 「本来の「革命」と明治以降の「革命」とは全く別の語」 ("the original kakumei and the post-Meiji kakumei are entirely different words"). https://japanknowledge.com/articles/blognihongo/entry.html?entryid=368
- **Later extensions.**
  - "Inner/spiritual revolution": Tōson, 破戒 (1906), 「精神(こころ)の内部(なか)の革命が丑松には猛烈に起って来て」.
  - "Industrial revolution": 横山源之助, 日本の下層社会 (1899), 「工業革命」.
  - https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%9D%A9%E5%91%BD-43816

### Connotations in the 1880s (synthesis from the above)
- **Foreign subject matter.** In the 1880s 革命 overwhelmingly meant **foreign** revolutions: France above all, then England and "the West" in general. It sat in two milieus. One was Liberal-Party popular print (illustrated newspapers, Dumas adaptations). The other was government-side refutation (Roesler via the 独逸学協会).
- **Romantic titles.** By 1888–90 the word was already in romantic or comic titles (恋の革命, 色の革命), so a love story under a "revolution" banner had real precedents.
- **Religious use.** "Religious revolution" (宗教革命, 1889) existed as a phrase.
- **Danger at home.** In Japan the dynastic sense collides with the dogma of an unbroken imperial line. ja.wikipedia 革命 notes the traditional claim that Japan "has never had a revolution." https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/革命 . Radical fiction about revolution and terror could be prosecuted (see Muryū above).

### Later association with socialism
- **Founding of the Japan Socialist Party.** On 28 Jan 1906 the first legal socialist party, 日本社会党, was founded under the tolerant first Saionji cabinet. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/赤旗事件
- **赤旗事件 (Red Flag Incident), 22 June 1908.** At the 錦輝館 in Kanda, 荒畑寒村, 大杉栄 and others unfurled red flags lettered in white 「無政府共産」, 「社会革命」 and "SOCIALISM," and sang revolutionary songs. Mass arrests followed. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/赤旗事件 . By 1908, "shakai kakumei" was a slogan of anarcho-communists.
- **Marxist usage.** The modern dictionaries' framing of 革命 is Marxist: プロレタリア革命, ブルジョア革命 (Nipponica, 加藤哲郎). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%9D%A9%E5%91%BD-43816

## 3. "Ōji" 王子 and "prince"; Western fairy tales in Meiji

### Japanese words for "prince"
- **王子 (ōji), NKD.**
  - ① The son of a monarch. The note says: 「天皇・皇帝の子には「皇子」を、王の子には「王子」をあてるのが普通」 ("for children of a tennō or an emperor, 皇子 is normally used; for children of a *king*, 王子").
  - ② Historically, a male member of the imperial family who has *not* received the 親王宣下 (proclamation as imperial prince).
  - Place names: [一] the **九十九王子** (ninety-nine Ōji subsidiary shrines of Kumano) on the Kyoto–Kumano pilgrimage road; [二] **王子** village in Tokyo.
  - https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%8E%8B%E5%AD%90-38677
- **王子 in Tokyo.** For a Tokyo reader in the 1880s, 王子 was also a place. It had the 王子権現 shrine and the 王子稲荷, famous from the *rakugo* 「王子の狐」. The 抄紙会社 (founded 1873 by Shibusawa Eiichi, later 王子製紙) began making Japan's first Western paper there in **1875**. The 上野–熊谷 railway (1883) put a station there. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%8E%8B%E5%AD%90-38677
- **太子 (taishi), NKD.** ① A Chinese ruler's heir. ② A Japanese crown prince (皇太子, 東宮). ③ A son of the emperor. As a proper noun it also means [一] **Shōtoku Taishi** and [二] **悉達太子 (Prince Siddhārtha)**. The citation for [二] is 梁塵秘抄 (c.1179): 「たいしの御幸には、犍陟駒(こんでいこま)に乗りたまひ…檀特山にぞ入りたまふ」 ("the prince rode out on the steed Kanthaka… into Mount Dantaloka"). So the oldest "prince on a horse" image in Japanese is the Buddha's Great Departure. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A4%AA%E5%AD%90-503445
- **皇太子 (kōtaishi), crown prince.** First attested in 続日本紀, Wadō 7 (714). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%9A%87%E5%A4%AA%E5%AD%90-62674
- **公子 (kōshi).** "Son of a noble; *kindachi*" (家伝 c.760; 太平記). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%85%AC%E5%AD%90-495327
- **若殿 (wakatono).** "Heir of a lord; young lord" (狂言). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E8%8B%A5%E6%AE%BF-664621

### How Meiji Japan named real Western princes
- **Prince Heinrich of Prussia, 1879–80.** Aged 16, he arrived at Yokohama on **23 May 1879** as a naval cadet. On **7 Feb 1880**, at 釈迦ヶ池 in 島下郡小路村 near Suita (Osaka), he went duck-hunting incognito in a posted no-hunting zone. A local man, 井田元吉, who did not know he was the 「プロイセン皇孫」 (Prussian imperial grandson), struck him. Prussia protested a 「皇孫に対して不敬」 ("disrespect to the imperial grandson"), and on 14 Feb a formal apology ceremony was held at the Osaka prefectural office and 吉志部神社. Scholarship calls this the 吹田プロイセン皇孫遊猟事件. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ハインリヒ・フォン・プロイセン_(1862-1929)
- **British princes Albert Victor and George (later George V), October 1881.** Both had themselves tattooed in Japan. Sources disagree on the designs: a dragon according to Dalton, a crane according to 長崎省吾, the Imperial Household official who escorted the 「両王子」. They also disagree on the tattooist; 彫千代 is often named but doubted. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ジョージ5世_(イギリス王)
- **The Ōtsu Incident, 11 May 1891.** The Russian heir, later Nicholas II, is the 「ロシア帝国皇太子」 or 露国皇太子. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/大津事件
- **Upshot.** Meiji usage followed genealogical rank: **皇太子** (crown prince), **皇孫** (imperial grandson), **王子** (son of a king). A generic fairy-tale "prince" had no settled word. As late as 1900, Tsubouchi rendered Cinderella's prince as **若殿** (see below).

### Western fairy tales in Meiji translation
- **Aesop.**
  - The Jesuit Amakusa romanised edition dates from 1593 and the kanazōshi 伊曽保物語 from the 17th century. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/伊曾保物語
  - Meiji: 渡部温 tr., 『通俗伊蘇普物語』, from Thomas James's English version, illustrated by **河鍋暁斎**, six volumes of about 40 fables each. It reportedly sold 10,000 copies soon after publication and was later used in elementary-school morals textbooks. The NDL exhibition dates it **1875**. https://www.ndl.go.jp/d_exhibitions/honyaku/421
- **Arabian Nights.**
  - **永峰秀樹 (Nagamine Hideki), 『暴夜物語 : 開巻驚奇』, 奎章閣, 1875 (Meiji 8), 2 vols.** NDL calls it 「我が国での最初の翻訳」, a partial translation from English with a "reward good, punish evil" preface.
  - Followed by **井上勤 『全世界一大奇書』 (1883)**, which transcribes names phonetically (西加亜利亜 = Shahriyar; 世辺羅亜土 = Scheherazade). It was a long-seller that influenced later writers.
  - https://www.ndl.go.jp/d_exhibitions/honyaku/421
- **Grimm.**
  - **1873:** the earliest Japanese rendering, inside a translated English school reader. 「釘」 (KHM 184) was translated by 松山棟庵.
  - **1886:** 『RŌMAJI ZASSI』 printed KHM 152 and KHM 18 in romanised Japanese.
  - **First book: 1887 (Meiji 20), 桐南居士 (= 菅了法, Suga Ryōhō), 『西洋古事 神仙叢話』.** The table of contents lists 10 tales but the book contains 11, including 「金の鳥」, 「忠臣ヨハネス」, 「踊ってすり切れた靴」, 「十二人の兄弟」, 「蜜蜂の女王」, **「灰かぶり」 (Aschenputtel)** and 「金の毛が三本ある悪魔」. It was translated from an English version, probably Paull's, and names neither the source nor the Grimms. (The title is *Shinsen sōwa* 神仙叢話, not "Shinzoku kusa.")
  - **Sept 1887:** 呉文聰 published 「八ツ山羊」 as 「西洋昔噺第一号」, a colour pop-up picture book of *The Wolf and the Seven Kids*, with the kids changed to **eight**.
  - **Oct 1889:** 上田万年 published the adaptation 『おほかみ』.
  - **1895:** 巌谷小波 recast the same tale with cats as 「子猫の仇」.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/グリム童話
  - **15 more Grimm tales** appeared in 『教育雑誌』's 「湖海談林」 column from **15 Jan to 25 Jun 1887**, in 17 instalments, 13 of them with copies of E. H. Wehnert's illustrations. Nishiguchi Hiroko, Senshu Univ.: https://senshu-u.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/7540/files/3062_0047_09.pdf
  - *Hansel and Gretel* was first translated in **1901** as 「一太郎とおすみ」 by 「東海生」, identified as Ueda Bin. https://baika.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/292/files/HK13_Koizumi_a.pdf
- **Cinderella in Japan.**
  - **1886:** 郵便報知新聞 printed 「新貞羅」.
  - **1887:** Suga's 「シンデレラの奇縁」 in 神仙叢話.
  - **1900:** Tsubouchi Shōyō (as 坪内雄蔵), writing for a higher-elementary textbook, made it **「おしん物語」**. The heroine is おしん (お辛), the fairy godmother becomes **弁天**, the glass slipper becomes **a fan (扇)**, **the prince becomes 若殿**, and the spell ends at 6 p.m.
  - Later titles included 「灰かぶり娘」 (Kikuchi Kan) and 「消炭さん」 (Nakajima Kotō).
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/シンデレラ
- **Andersen.**
  - An exhibition text (Azumino Chihiro Art Museum) says 「明治19年に「オウサマノアタラシキイショウ」が紹介されてから」 ("since *The Emperor's New Clothes* was introduced in Meiji 19, 1886"). https://bijutsutecho.com/exhibitions/4588
  - Search results (not opened) point to an 1888 adaptation by 巌本善治 in 女学雑誌, 「不思議の新衣裳」. See Uncertain.
- **Perrault's *Sleeping Beauty*.** I found no Meiji first-translation date (see Uncertain).
- ***Little Lord Fauntleroy* → 『小公子』 (*Shōkōshi*), Wakamatsu Shizuko.** The title 小公子 is her own coinage (1890). She serialised it in 女学雑誌 from **Aug 1890 to Jan 1892** in a colloquial です/ます style praised by Morita Shiken and Tsubouchi. Vol. 1 came out from 女学雑誌社 in **Oct 1891**. Her revised manuscript of vol. 2 burned; the complete text was published posthumously by Hakubunkan in **1897**. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/小公子 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%B0%8F%E5%85%AC%E5%AD%90-79178
  - Wakamatsu (real name 松川甲子) was born **元治元年3月1日 (6 Apr 1864)** in Aizu and died 10 Feb 1896. She graduated from Ferris's higher course in 1882. Her pen-name 賤子 meant 「神のしもべ」 ("servant of God"). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/若松賤子 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E8%8B%A5%E6%9D%BE%E8%B3%A4%E5%AD%90-153912
- **"白馬の王子" (hakuba no ōji).** I could not establish an origin date. Kotobank has only the 1982 Tanith Lee novel title. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%99%BD%E9%A6%AC%E3%81%AE%E7%8E%8B%E5%AD%90-729607 . NKD has no entry. Given that the generic fairy-tale prince was still 若殿 in 1900, the set phrase is almost certainly 20th-century, and probably post-1937 (Disney); this is not verified.

## 4. "Eien" 永遠 and "eikyū" 永久

- **永遠 (eien), NKD.**
  - ① "Continuing endlessly from past to future." The earliest example is Nakamura Masanao's 西国立志編 (*Saigoku risshi hen*, translation of Smiles's *Self-Help*, 1870–71): 「永遠無疆の福を望むことなり」.
  - ② "Existence transcending time." Earliest example: **哲学字彙 (*Tetsugaku jii*, 1881): 「Eternity 永遠」**.
  - So 永遠 enters common Japanese as a Meiji translation-word for *eternity/eternal*, through Smiles and the 1881 philosophical lexicon.
  - https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%B0%B8%E9%81%A0-35801
- **永久 (eikyū), the older and commoner word.**
  - "A long time" is attested in 曾我物語 (Nanbokuchō), 膝栗毛 (1802–09) and Fukuzawa's 西洋事情 初編 (「此政治は天地と共に永久すべし」).
  - Sense ② "lasting infinitely in time" is listed in the 物理学術語和英仏独対訳字書 (1888) and exemplified in Kunikida Doppo's 悪魔 (1903).
  - It is also the name of an era (永久, 1113–1118).
  - https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%B0%B8%E4%B9%85-443017
- **Native words** (listed as synonyms): とこしえ, とわ, ときわ (常磐 < トコ+イハ, "ever-rock"). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%B0%B8%E9%81%A0-35801
- **Christian "eternal life."** NKD: 永生 (eisei) ② "limitless life; eternal life" is recorded in Hepburn's 『改正増補 和英語林集成』 (1886). The older Buddhist reading 永生 (ようしょう) meant nirvāṇa (蓮如 御文章, 1461–98: 「後生は永生の楽果なり」). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%B0%B8%E7%94%9F-443204
  - From memory and **not verified here**: the 1880 Meiji New Testament renders John 3:16 with 「永生」, and 「永遠の生命」 becomes the standard formula with the 1917 Taishō revision.
- **Upshot.** For an 1880s reader, 永遠 was a fresh, learned word with a translated, philosophical or Christian tone. 永久 or とこしへ would sound ordinary.

## 5. "Kettō" 決闘 (duel)

- **NKD sense ①, "a decisive battle."** Earliest example 日本外史 (1827): 「寧各以単騎決闘、決雌雄」.
- **NKD sense ②, the formal pre-arranged duel.** Earliest example is a **1881 debate, 「決闘論」**: 「夫れ決闘即ち果合なるものは古来我邦にも之れありて」 ("this kettō, that is *hatashiai*, has existed in our country since antiquity"). The modern word was glossed by the native 果合 (hatashiai).
- https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%B1%BA%E9%97%98-59704
- **矢野龍渓 (Yano Ryūkei), 経国美談 (1883–84):** 「今ぞ正邪両党の巨魁が死生の決闘なり」. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%B1%BA%E9%97%98-59704
- **The 1888 challenge to Inukai Tsuyoshi.**
  - In Sept 1888, 松岡好一 of the magazine 『日本人』 published exposés of the **高島炭鉱** (Takashima coal mine).
  - **犬養毅** denied them in the 朝野新聞.
  - Matsuoka challenged Inukai to a duel, with **三宅雄二郎 (Setsurei)** and **志賀重昂** as seconds.
  - Inukai refused, calling duelling 「野蛮な遺風」 ("a barbaric remnant").
  - A wave of duel challenges followed.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/決闘罪ニ関スル件
- **光妙寺三郎 (Kōmyōji Saburō, 1847–93), "duelling is the flower of civilisation."** He was a France-educated official and prosecutor, a friend of Saionji from Paris, nicknamed 「東洋のクレマンソー」. On **30 Sept 1888**, at the 3rd 五大法律学校連合大討論会 held in a lecture hall of the Imperial University, he argued 「決闘は文明の華」. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/光妙寺三郎
- **決闘罪ニ関スル件 (Act on Duels), Law No. 34 of Meiji 22, promulgated 30 Dec 1889.** Penalties: challenging or accepting a duel, 6 months to 2 years; fighting one, 2 to 5 years; seconds and those who provide a venue, up to 1 year. Insulting someone for refusing a duel is punished as defamation. A Great Court of Cassation ruling of 14 Oct 1907 defined a duel as any agreed fight to injure body or life, regardless of numbers or equality of weapons. The law is still in force. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/決闘罪ニ関スル件 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%B1%BA%E9%97%98-59704
- **European duel lore** in the reference works: the challenge by throwing a glove; seconds; 8,000 dead in France 1589–1608; the 17th-century opera singer and swordswoman Maupin. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%B1%BA%E9%97%98-59704
- **Upshot.** In the 1880s "kettō" was a *new*, Western-coded concept being argued over. Debates in 1881, a cause célèbre and a "flower of civilisation" speech in 1888, then criminalisation at the end of 1889. A late-1880s duel plot would read as topical.

## 6. School words: seitokai, rijichō, gakuen, dormitories

- **生徒会 (seitokai).** All dictionaries describe it as a **postwar** institution, introduced in the Occupation reforms from the US "student council" model. The prewar 校友会 and the wartime 学校報国団 were reorganised into 生徒自治会. NKD gives no prewar example. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%94%9F%E5%BE%92%E4%BC%9A-86301
- **自治会 (jichikai) in the school sense.** NKD's earliest example is Ishizaka Yōjirō's 若い人 (1933–37): 「舎生に自治会を開かせて委せきりにした方がいい」. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E8%87%AA%E6%B2%BB%E4%BC%9A-73805
- **校友会 (kōyūkai), "alumni or student association."** Exemplified in 破戒 (1906). The word 同窓 was already in use in **1882** when the 茗渓会 of Tokyo Normal School was founded. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%A0%A1%E5%8F%8B%E4%BC%9A-497718
- **Student self-government did exist before 1945, in the dormitories.**
  - From **1886**, normal schools introduced **military-style dormitory regimes**, with ex-army 舎監 (dorm supervisors), curfews and night roll-calls, to block People's Rights ideas.
  - In **1890** the First Higher Middle School (第一高等中学校, later 一高) approved 「籠城自治」 ("self-government under siege"). This was the model for higher-school dormitory autonomy and its dorm festivals, "storms" and 寮歌 (dorm songs).
  - In Taishō, dormitory self-government spread to normal schools, middle schools and **girls' high schools**.
  - https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%AF%84%E5%AE%BF%E8%88%8E-50439
  - 一高 chronology: 「1890年（明治23年）- 木下廣次校長が学生自治を認可（自治寮開設）」; the dorm song 「嗚呼玉杯に花うけて」 dates from 1902. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/第一高等学校_(旧制)
- **寄宿舎 (kishukusha) and 寮 (ryō).**
  - NKD's earliest example of 寄宿舎 is about a *factory* dorm: 風俗画報 no. 124 (1896), 「鐘淵紡績会社女工寄宿舎」.
  - Sekai Daihyakka: early-Meiji school dorms were 「〈安下宿〉風で、放恣な〈書生〉気質に支配されていた」 ("cheap-lodging style, dominated by unruly *shosei* habits"). Full boarding schools in the Western sense barely existed outside military schools, the First Higher Middle School and some private schools.
  - In the 1880s textile industry, the dormitory system was the means of **confining girl workers**.
  - https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%AF%84%E5%AE%BF%E8%88%8E-50439
- **理事長 (rijichō).** NKD's earliest example is 報知新聞, **8 Jan 1897**. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%90%86%E4%BA%8B%E9%95%B7-1435836 . 理事 as the organ of a juridical person belongs to the civil-code era: Sekai Daihyakka refers to articles 53–54 of the Civil Code, enacted in the late 1890s; the exact date is not verified here. For an 1880s school, "board chairman" is anachronistic. Contemporary heads were 校長 (principal) or, at Dōshisha, **社長**: Niijima Jō is listed as 「同志社長：1883年 - 1889年」. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/新島襄
- **学園 (gakuen).** NKD's earliest example is Ishikawa Takuboku's 葬列 (**1906**): 「…幾多うら若き学園の逍遙者の…」. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%AD%A6%E5%9C%92-460102 . In the 1880s the usual words were 学校, 女学校, 塾, 学舎 and, for mission schools, **学院**. Meiji Gakuin (明治学院) took that name in 1886. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/明治学院

## 7. "Majo" 魔女 (witch) and native analogues

- **魔女 (majo), NKD sense ①, the European witch.** The earliest example is **Mori Ōgai, 青年 (1910–11)**: 「お伽話にある、魔女(マヂョ)に姿を変へられた人のやうな気がしてならない」. NKD also lists the Buddhist **魔女 (まにょ, manyo)**, "a woman of the Māra realm; a demon's daughter" (米沢本 沙石集, 1283: 「下品は广女(マニョ)となりて」; Lotus Sutra 普賢菩薩勧発品). In the 1880s, then, the characters 魔女 would most naturally be read *manyo*, a Buddhist demoness or temptress. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%AD%94%E5%A5%B3-136187
- **魔法使い (mahō-tsukai)** is old: 浄瑠璃 用明天皇職人鑑 (1705), 「まほうつかひの益良殿」. Sekai Daihyakka notes there is no Japanese word 魔男; male witches are 魔術師 or 魔法使い. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E9%AD%94%E6%B3%95%E4%BD%BF%E3%81%84-635639
- ***Macbeth* in Japanese.** The first complete translation is Ōgai's of 1913; about 19 more followed. Translators' words for the witches:
  - **魔女** (the most common in the 20th century);
  - **妖婆** (yōba, "uncanny hag");
  - **妖女** (yōjo);
  - **Tsubouchi's 妖巫** glossed ウィッチ, "a shrine-maiden with dark powers."
  - 梅宮悠, 日本英語表現学会 abstract: https://www.jaseus.org/kiyou/kikangou/031,%20032/031,%20032_08.html
- **Native analogues: 山姥 (yamauba).** She has a double image: the man-eating hag of 「三枚の護符」 and the nurturing mother who raised Kintarō. The Noh *Yamanba* (listed in 申楽談儀, 1430) treats her as a figure seeking salvation. Chikamatsu's 嫗山姥 premiered in 1712. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%B1%B1%E5%A7%A5-144009
- **憑き物筋 (tsukimono-suji), hereditary "possessing" lineages.** The types are:
  - オサキ狐 (Kantō);
  - イヅナ / 飯綱使い (Tōhoku, Chūbu);
  - クダ狐 (Chūbu, Tōkai);
  - 人狐 (San'in);
  - トウビョウ, a snake (Chūgoku, northern Shikoku);
  - **犬神** (Chūgoku, Shikoku, Kyūshū);
  - ヤコ (southern Kyūshū).
  - Such houses were believed to send the spirit against people they resented. Marriage with them was avoided, because the taint spread by marriage, and they were often said to be wealthy (十方庵遊歴雑記 on オサキ-holders). Medieval Awa and Tosa authorities sometimes executed alleged 犬神 users. Those possessed were "mostly women."
  - https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%8A%AC%E7%A5%9E-31878 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%86%91%E3%81%8D%E7%89%A9-571255 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%8B%90%E6%86%91%E3%81%8D-241776
- **Upshot.** An 1880s Japanese reader had no ready-made word for the European witch. The closest frames were the yamauba, the female 狐憑き victim or 犬神-holding household, and the Buddhist *manyo*. "魔女" in the Western sense is a 1900s–1910s development.

## 8. "Bara" 薔薇 and roses as symbols

- **Readings and early citations.**
  - しょうび (shōbi): 文華秀麗集 (818); 田氏家集 (c.892), 「薔薇嫌有刺」.
  - そうび (sōbi): Ki no Tsurayuki's 物名 poem on さうび in the 古今集 (905–914).
  - It is also a *kasane* colour name (red over purple).
  - The everyday native word is いばら/ばら.
  - https://kotobank.jp/word/%E8%96%94%E8%96%87-533010
- **History of the rose in Japan (ja.wikipedia).**
  - Anciently the plant was うまら/うばら (Man'yōshū 「茨（うまら）の末に延ほ豆の」); the place name 茨城 comes from it.
  - In the Edo period Chinese roses (コウシンバラ, モッコウバラ) were grown, Kaempfer recorded roses, and Buson wrote 「愁ひつつ岡にのぼれば花いばら」.
  - 「バラが日本でも愛好されるようになるのは明治以降である」 ("roses came to be loved in Japan only after Meiji").
  - The government obtained 'La France' (named 天地開) as an agricultural test plant at the 青山官制農園. Crowds came to smell it and a **wire fence** had to be put up; no year is given.
  - Mass popularity came in the Taishō–Shōwa era.
  - The Marian title **Rosa Mystica = 「奇しきばらの花」**.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/バラ
- **The Myōjō circle and *Midaregami*.** 明星 ran Apr 1900 – Nov 1908 (100 issues), edited by Yosano Tekkan. Issue 8 was banned for nude illustrations by 一條成美. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/明星_(文芸誌)
  - 『みだれ髪』 was published 15 Aug 1901 (399 poems), with a cover by **藤島武二**: a heart pierced by an arrow, and 「矢の根より吹き出でたる花は詩を意味せる」 ("the flowers springing from the arrowhead signify poetry").
  - Its six sections are 臙脂紫, **蓮の花船**, **白百合**, はたち妻 and two others. The flower emblems in evidence are the **lotus and the white lily**, not the rose.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/みだれ髪
- **Hakushū.** ja.wikipedia バラ credits Kitahara Hakushū's poetry with rose imagery and says it belongs to the Taishō–Shōwa diffusion. The specific poem 「薔薇二曲」 (「薔薇ノ木ニ 薔薇ノ花サク…」) is commonly placed in 『白金之独楽』 (1914), but that is not verified here. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/バラ
- **Later connotation, flagged as an anachronism trap.** 「薔薇族」 (*Barazoku*, founded July 1971) was Japan's first commercial gay magazine. The association of "bara" with male homosexuality dates from the 1970s, not before. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/薔薇族
- **Upshot.** In the 1880s a rose was an exotic Western garden novelty with a Sino-classical name, sometimes a Christian or Marian emblem. It was not yet a stock symbol of romantic love in Japanese poetry. That role in modern verse grows from the 1900s–1910s.

## 9. Christian vocabulary

- **The Kirishitan era (1549–).** Xavier, coached by Yajirō, first rendered Deus as **大日 (Dainichi)**. He then realised the danger and switched to the transliteration **デウス/でうす**. Monks were startled when the missionaries began saying 「大日を拝んではなりません。デウスを拝みなさい」. Other renderings used alongside it were 天帝, 天主 and 天道. Missionaries rejected **神** as a polytheistic word. Opponents punned **ダイウス → ダイウソ (大嘘, "big lie")**. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/デウス
  - NKD's first example of デウス is さるばとるむんぢ (1598). The variant 提宇子 (だいうす) appears in 信長公記 (1598), 「高山右近だいうす門徒に候」, and in 兼見卿記 in the sense of "a Catholic." Fabian Fucan's anti-Christian tract was titled 『破提宇子』. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E3%81%A7%E3%81%86%E3%81%99-3160288 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%8F%90%E5%AE%87%E5%AD%90-1372088
  - 天主 in the Christian sense is attested in サントスの御作業の内抜書 (1591) as *tenxu*. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A4%A9%E4%B8%BB-102426
- **The Catholic return.**
  - **1865 (Keiō 1):** at Yokohama, Fr. Pierre Mounicou (MEP) secretly printed 『聖教要理問答』, the first Japanese printed catechism in 265 years. It kept the Chinese **天主 (てんしゅ)**.
  - **1868:** Petitjean's 『聖教初学要理』, which adopted some Nagasaki hidden-Christian terms, used **天帝 glossed 【でうす】** with the note "same as 天主."
  - **1869 and 1872 editions:** **天主【でうす】**.
  - After the 1887 Protestant Bible, 天主 and 神 were mixed from the early 1890s. The Catholic Church officially replaced 天主 with 神 only in **1959**.
  - 高橋五郎訳『聖福音書』論文 (Kyoto Univ. repository): https://repository.kulib.kyoto-u.ac.jp/server/api/core/bitstreams/927bd1b8-536d-4510-a750-ea9414cb6059/content ; also https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A4%A9%E4%B8%BB-102426
  - "Catholicism" was 天主教, and in the Meiji–Taishō period also 天主公教会. Bakumatsu churches called themselves 天主堂 (e.g. 大浦天主堂). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A4%A9%E4%B8%BB%E6%95%99-102432
- **The Protestant "Kami."** Japanese Protestant Bibles have used **神** from the Hepburn translations on, with "no recorded dispute" among missionaries. Suzuki Norihisa's explanation is that the American translators relied on the Chinese Bibles that favoured 神 over 上帝. Japanese readers resisted: when 井深梶之助 preached that the 「創造主たる真神は唯一無二」, a *kokugaku* scholar retorted 「神は決して唯一に非ず多数あるべき道理なり」 ("gods are by nature many, never one"). Early Protestant writers therefore also used 真神 (まことのかみ), 上帝, 天父, 造物主 and 天主, with varied glosses. 植村正久 and 内村鑑三 used 上帝. 金香花 (Kim Hyang-hwa), 京都大学 キリスト教学研究室紀要 3 (2015): https://repository.kulib.kyoto-u.ac.jp/server/api/core/bitstreams/1158fcb0-ebbc-4609-a853-bb54a0cf6b39/content
  - NKD's earliest Christian example of 上帝 is 津田真道 tr., 泰西国法論 (1868). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E4%B8%8A%E5%B8%9D-79549
- **The Meiji Bible (明治元訳).**
  - New Testament: translated by Hepburn, S. R. Brown and D. C. Greene, work finished 3 Nov 1879, final parts published in 1880 with a completion celebration in April 1880.
  - Old Testament: completed 1887, celebrated 3 Feb 1888.
  - The joint translation was resolved at the 1872 Yokohama missionary conference; the committee first met in March 1874.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/明治元訳聖書
- **Would "Dios" be recognised?** I found no attested Japanese use of the Spanish form *Dios*. What Meiji Catholics, the hidden-Christian communities and anyone who knew Edo anti-Christian lore recognised was **デウス/でうす (or ダイウス, 提宇子)**, still printed as a gloss in Catholic catechisms of 1868–72. "Dios" would at most be heard as a variant of Deusu by the few who knew it.
- **Legal status.**
  - The 五榜の掲示 (posted Keiō 4/3/15 = 7 Apr 1868) renewed the ban on 切支丹 and 邪宗門. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/五榜の掲示
  - In the 浦上四番崩れ (fourth Urakami persecution), **3,394** Urakami Christians were exiled to various domains and **662 died**. The survivors called their exile 「旅」 ("the journey").
  - On **24 Feb 1873** the government took down the anti-Christian notice boards. It announced toleration to foreign governments but **never declared legalisation to its own people**. Prisoners were ordered released on 14 Mar 1873.
  - https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/浦上四番崩れ
- **The 信徒発見 (Discovery of the Hidden Christians), 元治2年2月20日 = 17 Mar 1865.** At 大浦天主堂, Urakami villagers led by 杉本ゆり told Fr. Petitjean 「われらのむね、あなたのむねと同じ」 ("our heart is the same as yours"). Before the Virgin's statue they said 「本当にサンタ・マリア様だよ。…御子ゼスス様を抱いておいでです」 ("it really is Santa Maria… holding her son Zesus"). They told him they were in 「悲の節」 ("the season of sorrow," i.e. Lent). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/信徒発見
- **Sunday schools and Christian literacy.**
  - 日曜学校 appears first in Fukuzawa's 西洋事情 (1866–70): 「日曜学校と唱ふるものあり」. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%97%A5%E6%9B%9C%E5%AD%A6%E6%A0%A1-109765
  - Britannica Japan: 「日本では1887年頃には日曜学校の用語が一般化していた」 ("in Japan the term Sunday school had become common by about 1887").
  - The Japan Sunday School Association was founded in 1907. The **8th World Sunday School Convention** met in **1920 at the Imperial Theatre, Tokyo**, presided over by Ōkuma Shigenobu, with a gift from the Imperial Household Ministry.
  - Catholic Sunday schools date from around 1919.
  - https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%97%A5%E6%9B%9C%E5%AD%A6%E6%A0%A1-109765
  - The first Christian periodical was 『七一雑報』 (*Shichiichi zappō*), published in Kobe from **1875** by the American Board's 新報社 and closed in June 1883. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/七一雑報 . 女学雑誌 and 明治女学校 are covered in §1.
- **Marian imagery.**
  - The **「親指のマリア」 (Thumb Madonna)**, Important Cultural Property, 17th century, oil on copper, 24.5 × 19.4 cm, Tokyo National Museum. It shows the Virgin with downcast eyes and **tears on her cheek**: a sorrowing Madonna in the manner of Carlo Dolci (1616–86), though without swords. It was carried by the Italian missionary **Sidotti**, captured on Yakushima in 1708, and later confiscated by the Nagasaki magistracy. https://online.bunka.go.jp/heritages/detail/585609
  - **"マリア観音" (Maria Kannon).** The name first appears around **1920**: Akutagawa's 『黒衣聖母』 has 「麻利耶観音像」. Hidden Christians themselves called such images **「ハンタマルヤ」** (from *Santa Maria*). Many are 17th-century Chinese **Dehua white-porcelain** Guanyin. Images confiscated in the 1856 浦上三番崩れ passed via the 教部省 and Home Ministry to the Imperial Museum. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/マリア観音
  - I found **no** evidence of a seven-swords Mater Dolorosa in Japanese Christian art available to 1880s readers (see Uncertain).

## 10. "Hime/Himemiya," "miya" titles; "S" and "dōseiai"; "onee-sama"

### Miya and Himemiya
- **姫宮 (himemiya) = 姫御子 (himemiko), an imperial princess.** Examples: 落窪物語 (10th c.), 「我御娘、姫宮にも劣らず」 ("my daughter, no less than an imperial princess"); 源氏物語 桐壺, 「后の宮の姫宮こそ、いとようおぼえて」. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A7%AB%E5%AE%AE-612553
- **宮 (miya).** It originally meant the residence of the emperor or imperial kin, then the people in it. 宮号 is the name of a princely house (宮家); 御称号 is a personal "○○宮" title. ja.wikipedia notes that some Shinto priestly families carry surnames with 宮, which have nothing to do with 宮号. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宮家
  - Princely houses in existence in the 1880s: 伏見宮, 有栖川宮, 閑院宮, 久邇宮 (from 中川宮, 1863), 山階宮 (1864), 梨本宮, 北白川宮, 華頂宮, 小松宮 (formerly 東伏見宮), and **桂宮**, which lasted until its last head, 淑子内親王, died in **1881**. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宮家
- **Personal 御称号 titles of the Meiji Emperor's children,** among them daughters: 梅宮, 滋宮, 増宮, 久宮, **常宮昌子内親王**, **周宮房子内親王**, 富美宮, 泰宮, 貞宮. 「明治期以降、皇族の出生に際して「○○宮」という御称号を定める慣行が明確に確認できる」 ("from Meiji on, the custom of fixing a ○○宮 title at an imperial birth is clearly attested"). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/御称号
  - Several of these princesses were born, and some died in infancy, in the 1880s. Birth years are not given on the page.
- **Upshot.** A character surnamed 姫宮 would read as "imperial princess" to anyone who knew *Genji*. In an era when "○○宮" announced imperial births, it would at the least sound high-born, and possibly presumptuous for a commoner.

### "S" (エス), "o-me," and dōseiai
- **"お目" (o-me), the earlier slang.** In 1909 the Yomiuri reported 「女学生間に『お目』なる異様の風習あり 元祖学習院女子部 大売捌虎門女学館 今や都下全校に全播す ▲お目とは女性同志の間に起る変則の愛情也」 ("among schoolgirls there is a strange custom called *o-me*; originated at the Gakushūin girls' department, chief distributor the Toranomon Jogakkan, now spread to every school in the capital; *o-me* is an irregular affection arising between women"). The word was also written with the characters **男女** read オメ. Source: 赤枝香奈子 (2004), via 鄒韻, *Juncture* 9 (Nagoya Univ.). https://nagoya.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/29090/files/juncture_9_74.pdf
- **"エス" (S).** Per 佐藤(佐久間)りか (1996), "S" was very fashionable in the **1910** reader columns of 『少女世界』.
  - 『少女画報』, April 1926, 「女学生隠し言葉辞典」: 「sister の頭文字、お姉さん、妹さん、等の意。主として妹に使用」 ("initial of *sister*; means elder sister, younger sister; used mainly of the younger").
  - 『婦人公論』, Feb 1928: 「お姉さま、妹の一対をさしたもの」 ("a pair of *onee-sama* and younger sister").
  - School-specific variants (稲垣恭子 2007): 跡見 「ご親友」, 学習院 「おハイカラ」, お茶の水 「オネツ」, plus 「オカチン」, 「バウ」, 「ハンドイン」.
  - https://nagoya.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/29090/files/juncture_9_74.pdf
  - Other names, 「おめ」「オデヤ」「同性の恋」「エス」, are said to have spread in girls' schools 「明治半ば頃から」 ("from about mid-Meiji"). Sugiura, review of Akaeda 2011: https://wako.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/1825/files/queer05_sugiura.pdf
- **The 1911 親不知 double suicide.** In late **July 1911** two graduates of a girls' school drowned themselves together on the 親不知 coast in Niigata. Yomiuri headline: 「恐るべき同性の愛」 ("fearful same-sex love"). Tokyo Asahi headline: 「極端なる同性の愛」 ("extreme same-sex love"). Both appeared **31 July 1911**. Hiruma Yukiko (2003) treats the case as the "discovery" of female homosexuality in modern Japan. In the same year Tamura Toshiko's 「あきらめ」 was serialised and 『青鞜』 was founded. 加藤明日菜, 立教大学ジェンダーフォーラム年報 18: https://rikkyo.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/15089/files/AA11427614_18_03.pdf ; Nagoya dissertation summary: https://nagoya.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/30605/files/k13334_summary.pdf
  - Note that the 1911 headlines used the phrase **「同性の愛」**, not the compound 同性愛.
- **How the compound developed.**
  - In late Meiji, the translation of Krafft-Ebing (『色情狂編』) had 「同性に対する情慾」, alongside the old 鶏姦 (sodomy).
  - In the Taishō popular-sexology boom the vocabulary went from 同性色情 and 同性性欲 to **同性愛**.
  - Havelock Ellis's 「女性間の同性恋愛」 appeared in 『青鞜』 4:4 (1914).
  - https://nagoya.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/30605/files/k13334_summary.pdf ; https://rikkyo.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/15089/files/AA11427614_18_03.pdf
  - NKD's earliest entry for 同性愛 is only a 1925 popular dictionary (『増補改版 や、此は便利だ』). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%90%8C%E6%80%A7%E6%84%9B-103769
- **The S relationship as a literary type.** Defining traits: one-to-one, exclusive, mutually improving, meant to be lasting, pure, mirroring. It is tied to Yoshiya Nobuko's 『花物語』 (from 1916 in 少女画報) and peaked in the 1930s. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/エス_(文化)
- **Upshot for the 1880s.** None of these words existed yet: no エス, no お目, no 同性愛. An 1880s reader would map love between girls onto either (a) Edo 男色/衆道 frames transposed (the Edo sense of 少年 as *wakashu*, above), or (b) sisterly or devoted friendship with no name at all. The vocabulary arrives in 1909–1925.

## 11. "Utena" 台・萼 and the Buddhist frame

- **うてな【台・萼】, NKD:**
  - ① An earthen terrace for viewing (和名抄 c.934).
  - ② A tall building or pavilion: 竹取物語, 「玉のうてな」 ("a jewelled tower").
  - ③ **The lotus seat** of those reborn in paradise: 源氏物語 鈴虫, **「はちす葉をおなじうてなと契おきて露のわかるるけふぞかなしき」** ("we vowed the same lotus seat; how sad today, as dew parts from the leaf").
  - ④ **Lineage or bloodline**: kabuki 御摂勧進帳 (1773), 「義経いやしくも清和の台(ウテナ)を出で」 ("Yoshitsune, sprung from the Seiwa *utena*").
  - ⑤ **The calyx of a flower (萼)**, extended from 花のうてな: 浄瑠璃 嫗山姥 (c.1712), 「あさがほの花のうてな」. 萼 = うてな also appears in the botanical manual 植学啓原 (1833).
  - https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%8F%B0-90713 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E8%90%BC-43523
- **蓮の台 (hasu no utena / hachisu no utena).** "The lotus pedestal of buddhas and bodhisattvas; the lotus seat of those reborn in the Pure Land" (有房集 c.1182; 大輔集 late 12th c.). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E8%93%AE%E3%81%AE%E5%8F%B0-601451
- **一蓮托生 (ichiren takushō).**
  - ① Being reborn after death **on the same lotus** in paradise (岷江入楚, 1598).
  - ② Hence "sharing one fate to the end": Chikamatsu, 心中宵庚申 (1722), 「一れんたくしゃうの閨のお同行」.
  - NKD 語誌: the four-character form does not occur in the Chinese sutras. The idea of **two people on one lotus** was present in Heian times (the 鈴虫 poem). In the Edo period the 浄瑠璃 (puppet-theatre) usage fixed the modern sense.
  - Nipponica: in Edo literature it meant **hoping to fulfil a love in the next life**, the language of lovers' double suicide (*shinjū*).
  - https://kotobank.jp/word/%E4%B8%80%E8%93%AE%E6%89%98%E7%94%9F-433874
- **The Dragon King's daughter and 変成男子 (henjō nanshi).**
  - In the Lotus Sutra, chapter 12 (提婆達多品), the **eight-year-old daughter of the dragon king Sāgara** becomes a buddha in an instant, despite being a dragon, a child and female, by first being **transformed into a man**. The story became the scriptural basis for women's salvation and a motif in literature and art. It was given special weight at the 法華八講 lectures. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%AB%9C%E5%A5%B3%E6%88%90%E4%BB%8F-877944
  - **変成男子, NKD:**
    - ① A woman changed into a man, because women's five obstructions make buddhahood hard. 梁塵秘抄 (c.1179): 「龍女が仏に成ることは…変成男子として終には成仏道」.
    - ② A joke on the replacement of women's kabuki by young men's kabuki: 東海道名所記 (1659–61), 「諸寺の僧達は、変成男子の利やくはここなり、とよろこび」 ("the priests rejoiced: here is the benefit of *henjō nanshi*!").
    - **③ "A woman dressed as a man."** 雑俳 三尺の鞭 (1753): 「よい月夜変成男子落し佩し」 ("a fine moonlit night: a *henjō nanshi* with sword slung low").
    - https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A4%89%E6%88%90%E7%94%B7%E5%AD%90-131204
  - The 35th vow of the 大無量寿経 (Larger Sukhāvatī-vyūha Sūtra) implies there are no women in the Pure Land (Sekai Daihyakka, s.v. 極楽). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A4%89%E6%88%90%E7%94%B7%E5%AD%90-131204
- **五障 (goshō), the five obstructions.** Per the Lotus Sutra (提婆達多品), a woman cannot become **Brahmā, Śakra, Māra, a wheel-turning king or a buddha**. The earliest Japanese example is in 本朝文粋 (c.1060). The phrase was paired with 三従 (obedience to father, husband, son) as **五障三従**, and figured as 「五障の雲/霞/氷」 ("clouds, mist, ice of the five obstructions"). https://kotobank.jp/word/%E4%BA%94%E9%9A%9C-64791
- **Meiji context.** ja.wikipedia 変成男子 claims that after the Restoration, as Confucian patriarchy spread to commoners, "women cannot become buddhas" was used across Japanese Buddhism to justify female inferiority, and that Nichiren Shōshū abolished nuns. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/変成男子 . This is weakly sourced; see Uncertain.
- **Upshot.** An 1880s reader with ordinary Buddhist and theatrical literacy would hear "utena" as the lotus seat. The phrase 一蓮托生 tied it to lovers who die together, and 変成男子 was an existing, even jokey, term for a woman in male dress.

## Telling details

- **A real 1888 "Revolution of Love."** The Liberal Party's illustrated daily 絵入自由新聞 ran a serial titled 「恋の革命」. Its illustration of 14 March 1888 shows police halting a People's Rights speech. In March 1890 the 戯作 (comic-fiction) writer 花笠文京 II published 『色の革命』 about *sōshi* (political toughs) and geisha. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/絵入自由新聞 ; https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/books/R100000002-I000000510326
- **The French Revolution as an illustrated newspaper serial,** within weeks of the paper's founding. Miyazaki Muryū's 『仏蘭西革命記 自由乃凱歌』 (Oct–Dec 1882), illustrated by Utagawa Yoshimune II. Sakurada Momoe died at 25 (*kazoedoshi*) with his Dumas serial unfinished. https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/books/R100000136-I1970304959840087302 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/桜田百衛
- **"Kakumei" was still a calendar emergency in 1860.** A manuscript of Man'en 1 deliberates whether the next 辛酉 year is a "revolution year" requiring precautions. Fukuzawa's new sense of the word appeared within a decade. https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/books/R100000136-I1971430859767783590
- **"Duelling is the flower of civilisation."** France-educated prosecutor 光妙寺三郎 argued this at an inter-law-school debate on 30 Sept 1888, months after Inukai Tsuyoshi refused a duel over the Takashima mine reports, with Miyake Setsurei as one challenger's second. Duelling became a crime on 30 Dec 1889. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/光妙寺三郎 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/決闘罪ニ関スル件
- **"Henjō nanshi" meant a cross-dressed woman by 1753.** The Buddhist term for the dragon girl turning male was used for a woman in male dress (「よい月夜変成男子落し佩し」). A century earlier, priests joked that the switch from women's to boys' kabuki was "the benefit of henjō nanshi." https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A4%89%E6%88%90%E7%94%B7%E5%AD%90-131204
- **The first government girls' school put its pupils in samurai men's hakama** (1872), and Tokyo banned women's short hair the same spring (Meiji 5/4/5 = 11 May 1872). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/東京女学校 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/断髪令
- **Utena and Himemiya both come out of *Genji*.** 姫宮 is Heian for an imperial princess. The *Genji* "Suzumushi" poem is the classic citation for うてな as a lotus seat two people vow to share. https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A7%AB%E5%AE%AE-612553 ; https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%8F%B0-90713
- **Utena also meant "bloodline."** 「清和の台を出で」 means "sprung from the Seiwa (Minamoto) stock." https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%8F%B0-90713

## Additional facts found while verifying

- **The 1873 cross-dressing article explicitly allowed women to wear hakama.** The exemption reads 「女ノ着袴スル類此限ニ非ス」. Women who had a reason for short hair, or nuns letting their hair grow back, could apply for a police certificate (Tokyo-fu notice of 19 Feb 1873, signed by 大久保一翁). They had to carry it until their hair could be dressed again. The 詿違 penalty was at first a fine of 6.25–12.5 sen, or 1–2 days' detention. Sakago 2016, above.
- **Press hostility to schoolgirls in men's dress in 1872.** 『新聞雑誌』 (Meiji 5/3) called women's cropped hair 「醜体陋風」. The same article complained of **female students wearing men's hakama and geta**, calling both 「文化開化ノ弊」. 『日要新聞』 (Meiji 5/2) blamed short-haired women on teahouse waitresses and 「おてんば」. Sakago 2016.
- **Hakama at the women's normal school.** Students of the Tokyo Women's Normal School (1875) inherited the 東京女学校 hakama: striped cotton, government-issued. Hakama and the school hairpin were dropped around 1879. By 1885 (per 女学雑誌) many of its students and teachers wore Western dress. Iwasaki 1993, above.
- **Meiji renderings of Cinderella's prince (Noguchi 2023).**
  - The first Japanese Cinderella was 「新貞羅」 in the 郵便報知新聞, 29–30 Dec 1886, probably by 矢野龍渓. In it the prince is a **公子**, and the heroine becomes his **貴妃** (a secondary consort), not his queen.
  - Other Meiji versions render the prince as 城主 (Suga 1887), 皇太子/皇子/太子 (two 1891 versions), 公子 (1896), 公達 (1899) and 若殿 (1900). 王子 is established only in 1909 (「真珠姫」).
  - Noguchi counts twelve Meiji Cinderella translations: nine from Perrault, three from Grimm.
  - This supports the report's "no settled word" conclusion. In the mid-1880s the most likely word was **公子**. https://kpu.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2000019/files/j_75_008.pdf
- **The 1886 Andersen used 王 (Ō), "king"; 女学雑誌's 1888 version used 新衣裳; the first book was by 高橋五郎.** 高橋五郎 is the same man as the Catholic gospel translator in §9. ja.wikipedia 裸の王様.
- **少年世界's girls' column ran only from 1895 to 1897.** The label 「少女小説」 first appears there in 1897. Girls' material returned only with vol. 9 (1903), after the 高等女学校令 (Kume 2013 summary).
- **The 1888 duel debate.** The debate question was a hypothetical duel case, set (it is suggested) by someone close to 政教社, the 『日本人』 group. That links it directly to the Matsuoka–Inukai affair. The 1882 penal code had no explicit duel provision, which is why the 1889 law was needed. Kōmyōji, a Paris law graduate (Feb 1878, the first Japanese to take the degree there), died poor. His geisha companion 米八 became the shinpa actress 千歳米坡. NDL Monthly 670.
