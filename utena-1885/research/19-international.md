## 1. Translations of Japanese fiction and drama into Western languages before 1910

### Background: the first translation (1847)
- August Pfizmaier (b. Karlsbad 17 Mar 1808, d. Vienna 15 May 1887) published in Vienna in 1847 *Sechs Wandschirme in Gestalten der vergänglichen Welt*, his German translation of Ryūtei Tanehiko's (柳亭種彦, 1783–1842) gōkan *Ukiyogata rokumai byōbu* (浮世形六枚屏風, 1821). The German Wikipedia calls it the first Japanese novel translated into a Western language. From 1850 until his death he averaged about 200 pages a year of translation from Chinese and 125 from Japanese. A digitised copy is at MDZ (bsb10250414). https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Pfizmaier
- Search-only, unverified: a snippet said Pfizmaier's edition printed the Japanese text alongside the German, and that his German was in turn translated into English, Italian and French.

### A. B. Mitford, *Tales of Old Japan* (1871)
- Published in London in 1871 (Meiji 4), by Algernon Bertram Freeman-Mitford, later Lord Redesdale. It is an anthology of retold tales: "The Forty-Seven Rônins", "The Loves of Gompachi and Komurasaki", "Kazuma's Revenge", "A Story of the Otokodaté of Yedo", "The Eta Maiden and the Hatamoto", nine fairy tales (Momotarō, Kachi-kachi yama, Shita-kiri suzume and others), "The Ghost of Sakura", tales of supernatural cats, foxes and badgers, the sermons of Kiu-ô, and appendices on hara-kiri (with Mitford's eyewitness account), the marriage ceremony, the birth and rearing of children, and funeral rites. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tales_of_Old_Japan
- The Wikipedia page cites the 1903 Macmillan edition and does not name the 1871 publisher. I did not verify the first-edition imprint.

### F. V. Dickins, *Chiushingura, or the Loyal League* (1875)
- Frederick Victor Dickins (24 May 1838 – 16 Aug 1915) was a naval surgeon at Yokohama from 1863, then a barrister in Yokohama from 1871. In the *María Luz* case he represented the Peruvian captain. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Dickins
- Kawamura Hatsue (川村ハツエ, 1995) found the 1875 Yokohama first edition at the Tōyō Bunko. Dickins's own fourth edition (1912) lists: "First Edition, Yokohama, 1875. Second Edition, Revised, with 29 illustrations (Allen & Co.) 1880. Third Edition … (Gowans and Gray) July 1910. Fourth Edition, unillustrated, June 1912." Donald Keene had wrongly called the 1880 edition the oldest. Dickins had earlier translated the *Hyakunin isshu* (1866) and later the *Taketori monogatari* (1888) and *Hōjōki* (1905). https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1050282677919245312
- An American edition was published in New York by G. P. Putnam's Sons in 1876, with an introduction by Hoffman Atkinson: 213 pp. plus 29 leaves of plates on Japanese paper. A woodblock leaf before the title page reads 「享保三年 竹田出雲著 假名手本忠臣藏 / 明治八年 横 天琴壽譯」, so it dates the translation to Meiji 8 (1875). The appendix includes a "metrical version of the Eighth Book" and of the *Takasago* ballad. https://shinku.nichibun.ac.jp/gpub/book/g0409.html
- Search-only: the Royal Academy catalogue gives the 1875 imprint as "Yokohama, printed at the 'Japan Gazette' office". The page itself was blocked by Cloudflare.

### Suematsu Kenchō's partial *Genji* (1882)
- Suematsu Kenchō (末松謙澄, 1855–1920) arrived in London in 1878 with the Japanese legation and entered Cambridge in 1881 (St John's College; law degree 1884; returned to Japan 1886). His *Genji Monogatari: The Most Celebrated of the Classical Japanese Romances* (London: Trübner, 1882), written while at Cambridge, was the first English translation and covers the first 17 chapters. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suematsu_Kench%C5%8D

### Bakin in the West
- Edward Greey (1835–1888), *A Captive of Love: founded upon Bakin's Japanese romance = Kumono tayema ama yo no tsuki = The moon shining through a cloud-rift on a rainy night* (Boston: Lee and Shepard; New York: Charles T. Dillingham, 1886, ©1885). It has 280 pp. and [24] leaves of plates. The original is Bakin's 『雲妙間雨夜月』. https://opac.nijl.ac.jp/opac/opac_link/bibid/1000216091
- Greey also published *The Golden Lotus and Other Legends of Japan* (1883) and *The Loyal Ronins* (from Tamenaga Shunsui; a French version, *Les fidèles Ronins*, was made from his English). Gowans & Gray reissued *A Captive of Love* in 1911 and 1912. https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/who/Greey%2c%20Edward%2c%201835%2d1888
- *Okoma: roman japonais illustré*, by "Bakin Takizawa", with a preface by Émile Guimet and illustrations by Félix Régamey after drawings by "Chiguenoi". It was published in Paris by E. Plon et Cie in 1883 as vol. 2 of the *Annales du Musée Guimet, Bibliothèque d'art*. https://sammlung.mak.at/en/collect/annales-du-musee-guimet-bibliotheque-dart-2_283170

### Meiji novels translated before 1910
- **Tokutomi Roka 徳冨蘆花, *Hototogisu* 不如帰.** It was serialised in the *Kokumin Shinbun* from 29 Nov 1898 to 24 May 1899, and the book came out from Min'yūsha on 15 Jan 1900. The first printing of 1,000 copies sold out in a month, and 10,000 copies sold in two years. By Roka's death in 1927 it had reached 190 printings and about 500,000 copies. Heroine Namiko's line 「ああ辛い！辛い！最早婦人なんぞに生まれはしませんよ」 ("Oh, it's hard, it's hard! I'll never be born a woman again!") became one of the most famous lines in modern Japanese fiction. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/不如帰_(小説)
- English: *Nami-ko, a realistic novel*, by Kenjiro Tokutomi, translated by Sakae Shioya and E. F. (Edwin Francis) Edgett (Boston: H. B. Turner, 1904; [xv], 314 pp.; LCCN 04007710). Archive.org's record gives the original as "first published in 1900 under the title Hototogisu". https://archive.org/details/namikorealisticn00tokuiala
- Chinese: Lin Shu 林紓 and Wei Yi 魏易 translated it as 《不如歸》 in 1908. It was reprinted many times, and Chinese sequels were written. In Korea the novel was published in 1912 and counted as one of the three bestsellers of the 1910s. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/不如帰_(小説) ; https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/林紓
- **Ozaki Kōyō 尾崎紅葉, *Konjiki yasha* 金色夜叉.** It ran in the *Yomiuri Shinbun* from 1 Jan 1897 to 11 May 1902 and was left unfinished. In 2000 Hori Keiko showed that its source was Bertha M. Clay's (Charlotte M. Brame's) *Weaker than a Woman* (serialised in the *Family Herald*, 17 Aug–23 Nov 1878). In Korea it was adapted as 長恨夢 (*Changhanmong*). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/金色夜叉
- English: *The Gold Demon* (1905), translated by Arthur Lloyd (1852–1911), a lecturer at the Imperial University of Tokyo. Lloyd also translated a Tokutomi tale, *Glowing Embers: A Tale of the Satsuma Rebellion* (c. 1900), and *Nature and Man* by Tokutomi Roka (c. 1900). From Karl Florenz's German he translated *White Aster* (1897; Inoue Tetsujirō's 孝女白菊の歌) and *Poetical Greetings from the Far East* (c. 1897). https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Author:Arthur_Lloyd
- Search-only, conflicting: one library record lists a 1906 Yūrakusha edition of *The Gold Demon* in two volumes, credited to A. and M. (Mary von Fallot) Lloyd. I could not open it.

### Lafcadio Hearn and B. H. Chamberlain
- Hearn (27 Jun 1850 – 26 Sep 1904) came to Japan in 1890. He taught at Matsue from summer 1890, where he married Koizumi Setsu, then at Kumamoto from late 1891. He worked for the *Kobe Chronicle* from Oct 1894, became a Japanese subject as Koizumi Yakumo 小泉八雲 in Jan 1896, and taught at Tokyo Imperial University from 1896 to 1903. His books include *Glimpses of Unfamiliar Japan* (1894), *Kokoro* (1896), *In Ghostly Japan* (1899) and *Kwaidan* (1904). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lafcadio_Hearn
- Chamberlain (18 Oct 1850 – 15 Feb 1935) landed on 29 May 1873, taught at the Naval Academy from 1874 to 1882, and was professor of Japanese at Tokyo Imperial University from 1886. He published *The Classical Poetry of the Japanese* (1880), the first English *Kojiki* (1882), *A Handbook of Colloquial Japanese* (1888), *Things Japanese* (1890; six editions to 1936), and Murray's *Handbook for Travellers in Japan* (1891, with W. B. Mason). He and Hearn fell out. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_Hall_Chamberlain

### Japanese political novels and their spread
- Tōkai Sanshi (Shiba Shirō), *Kajin no kigū* 佳人之奇遇 (1885–97, 16 vols, unfinished), opens at Independence Hall in Philadelphia. There an exiled Aizu samurai meets two beautiful women patriots: the Irish Kōren 紅蓮 and the Spanish noblewoman Yūran 幽蘭. Liang Qichao serialised a Chinese version, 『佳人奇遇』, from the first issue of *Qingyi bao* (清議報, Yokohama, 1898). A Vietnamese verse version appeared in 1926. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/佳人之奇遇
- Yano Ryūkei's *Keikoku bidan* 経国美談 (1883–84) retells the rise of Thebes. Liang attempted Chinese versions of it and of *Kajin no kigū*, and wrote his own *Xin Zhongguo weilai ji* 新中國未來記 (1902) on their model. Around 1890 Japan also had a short-lived sub-genre of "women's-rights novels" (女権小説). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/政治小説 ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/経国美談

## 2. Hasegawa Takejirō's crêpe-paper books (chirimen-bon)

- Hasegawa Takejirō (長谷川武次郎) was born in 1853. His death year is given as 1938 by en.wikipedia and as 1936 by Rikkyō University Library (see Uncertain). He spoke good English and traded goods. From about Meiji 16 (1883) he made woodblock picture books with English text, which sold poorly. He then "suddenly thought: what if I used crêpe paper" (「不図縮紙でしたらばと思いついて」). His firm was 長谷川弘文社 (Kōbunsha), later "T. Hasegawa" and "Hasegawa & Co." https://gssc.dld.nihon-u.ac.jp/wp-content/uploads/journal/pdf14/14-013-019-Otsuka.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasegawa_Takejir%C5%8D
- **Japanese Fairy Tale Series (日本昔噺).** The first six volumes appeared in 1885 (Meiji 18), all translated by the Presbyterian missionary David Thompson ("Thomson" on en.wikipedia; ja.wikipedia's ディビッド・タムソン): *Momotaro*, *The Tongue-Cut Sparrow*, *The Battle of the Monkey and the Crab*, *The Old Man who Made the Dead Trees Blossom*, *Kachi-Kachi Mountain* and *The Mouse's Wedding*. No. 7, *The Old Man and the Devils* (1886), was by James Curtis Hepburn. Basil Hall Chamberlain did *Urashima* and *The Serpent with Eight Heads* (1886), *The Silly Jelly-Fish* and *My Lord Bag-o'-Rice* (1887). Mrs T. H. (Kate) James did many titles, including *The Matsuyama Mirror* (1886), *The Wooden Bowl* (hachi-kazuki, 1887), *Schippeitaro* (1888) and *The Ogre's Arm* (1889). Lafcadio Hearn did *The Boy who Drew Cats* (1898), *The Old Woman who Lost her Dumpling* (1902), *Chin Chin Kobakama* (1903) and *The Goblin Spider*. By 1903 the series had 28 volumes in two series. Illustrations were by Kobayashi Eitaku (小林永濯) until his death in 1890. Outside the series, Hasegawa published E. R. Miller's *Princess Splendor* (Taketori monogatari, 1889) and Chamberlain's three *Aino Fairy Tales* (1887). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasegawa_Takejir%C5%8D
- **Languages.** English came first, then French, German and Dutch. From the Taishō era there were also Spanish, Portuguese, Russian and Danish editions (Otsuka 2013). en.wikipedia lists French, German, Spanish, Portuguese and Swedish. A modern facsimile set, 『ちりめん本日本昔噺輯』, collects 92 volumes in English, French, Italian, Russian, German, Portuguese, Swedish and Spanish (search-only; see Uncertain). Karl Florenz translated the German editions. https://gssc.dld.nihon-u.ac.jp/wp-content/uploads/journal/pdf14/14-013-019-Otsuka.pdf
- **How sold abroad.**
  - In Japan they were sold at Hasegawa's own shop, at Maruzen, and at the Yokohama outlet of the *Japan Gazette*.
  - For export they went through Kelly & Walsh (branches in Yokohama, Hong Kong, Shanghai and Singapore).
  - A co-publishing agreement with Griffith, Farran & Co. of London put the English series on sale in London and Sydney. Hanyū Noriko calls this the first case of a Japanese publisher partnering with a foreign one (1885).
  - Through Florenz, Hasegawa also contracted with C. F. Amelang of Leipzig, which co-published Florenz's German translations of Japanese literature.

  https://gssc.dld.nihon-u.ac.jp/wp-content/uploads/journal/pdf14/14-013-019-Otsuka.pdf
- Tourist trade: the books sold at the Fujiya Hotel (Hakone) and the Kanaya Hotel (Nikkō). Hasegawa also contracted with dealers in London, Sydney and Chicago, and made illustrated calendars (for example a 1906 calendar printed on Japanese towels). https://library.rikkyo.ac.jp/_asset/pdf/archives/exhibition/exhibition1/2023_chirimen.pdf
- **Paris 1900.** Hasegawa prepared French-language books printed on costly hōsho paper (21 × 28 cm). One was *Scènes du théâtre japonais: L'école de village: Terakoya*, Florenz's version of the Terakoya scene of *Sugawara denju tenarai kagami*, illustrated by Arai (Utagawa) Yoshimune (新井芳宗, 1863–1941). It won a **gold medal** at the 1900 Exposition. A German edition followed in Sept 1900, *Japanische Dramen: Terakoya und Asagao*. https://gssc.dld.nihon-u.ac.jp/wp-content/uploads/journal/pdf14/14-013-019-Otsuka.pdf
- Other Hasegawa titles: *Japanese Pictures of Japanese Life* (1895), Mrs E. S. Patton's *Japanese Topsyturvydom* (1896), *Sword and Blossom: Poems from Japan* (Charlotte Peake and Kimura Shōtarō, 1907–10), and *The Favorite Flowers of Japan* (1901). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasegawa_Takejir%C5%8D
- **Print runs and prices:** not found. No page I opened gives edition sizes or retail prices. Ishizawa Saeko's 『ちりめん本のすべて』 (2004) is the likely source.

## 3. Expositions, Japonisme, and the making of the "Japanese woman" image

### London 1862
- The Takenouchi embassy (竹内下野守保徳, 38 men, including Fukuzawa Yukichi and Fukuchi Gen'ichirō) reached London on 30 April 1862 (文久2年4月2日). The next day, 1 May 1862 (文久2年4月3日, derived from NDL's pairing), it attended the opening of the International Exhibition as guests. Japan sent no official exhibit; Rutherford Alcock showed his own collection of lacquer, swords, straw rain-capes, lanterns and sandals. Fuchibe Tokuzō (淵辺徳蔵) complained in his 『欧行日記』 that it was "all junk like old curios" (「全く骨董品の如く雑具」). https://www.ndl.go.jp/exposition/s1/1862-1.html
- Judith Gautier is said to have first met Japanese art at this exhibition with her father; French Wikipedia marks this "citation needed". https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judith_Gautier

### Paris 1867
- The Exposition ran from 1 April to 3 November 1867; ja.wikipedia's link text gives 31 Oct, see Uncertain. It had 42 participating countries and 15 million visitors.
- The shogunate, Satsuma (as 「薩摩琉球国太守政府」) and Saga each exhibited separately. Satsuma sent about 400 crates of more than 100 kinds of goods, and struck Japan's first decoration, the 薩摩琉球国勲章, which it gave to Napoleon III's officials.
- Tokugawa Akitake (徳川昭武, 15 sai) led the shogunal mission of 25. It left Yokohama on the French mail-steamer *Alphée* on 15 Feb 1867 (= 慶応3年1月11日 by my conversion; the ja page gives only the Gregorian date) and reached Marseille on 3 April 1867.

  https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/パリ万国博覧会_(1867年)
- **The three women at the teahouse.** Shimizu Usaburō (清水卯三郎), an Asakusa merchant and the only Japanese merchant exhibitor, built a sukiya-style teahouse of hinoki. Three geisha from Yanagibashi served in it: **Osumi, Okane and Osato** (おすみ、おかね、おさと). They simply spun tops and smoked kiseru pipes, and drew bigger crowds than the official displays. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/パリ万国博覧会_(1867年)
- Shimizu received a silver medal from Napoleon III. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/清水卯三郎
- NDL's summary: "日本女性3人がキセルで一服する様子などが見られる茶店が人気であった." https://www.ndl.go.jp/exposition/s1/1873-2.html

### Vienna 1873
- The Exposition ran from 1 May to 31 October 1873, with 35 countries and about 7.26 million visitors, and was hit by the stock-market crash and cholera. It was the **Meiji government's first official participation**.
  - On a site of about 1,300 tsubo Japan built a shrine and garden with a plain-wood torii, a kagura hall and an arched bridge.
  - Exhibits included the golden *shachi* of Nagoya Castle, a model of the Kamakura Daibutsu, a model of the Yanaka Tennōji five-storey pagoda about 4 m high, a 2 m drum, and a 4 m lantern painted with a dragon in waves.
  - Heinrich von Siebold advised big, eye-catching objects; Gottfried Wagener advised crafts rather than imitation machinery.
  - Several thousand fans sold within a week. Franz Joseph and Elisabeth walked across the unfinished bridge, and the ladies-in-waiting kept plane shavings as souvenirs.
  - Afterwards the buildings and garden were sold to an English company at Alexandra Palace, and the Kiritsu Kōshō Kaisha (起立工商会社) was founded in 1874.

  https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ウィーン万国博覧会 ; https://www.ndl.go.jp/exposition/s1/1873-2.html

### Philadelphia 1876
- The Japan exhibit had a pavilion, a house and a garden, with one of the first "dwarfed trees" (bonsai) shown outside Japan. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centennial_Exposition

### Paris 1878 and 1889
- 1878: Japan had a pavilion on the rue des Nations and a farm in the Trocadéro gardens; Émile Guimet arranged two of its rooms. Philippe Burty praised the gate as "quelque chose de primitif et de raffiné tout ensemble". https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exposition_universelle_de_1878
- Hayashi Tadamasa (林忠正, b. 嘉永6年11月7日 = 7 Dec 1853, d. 10 Apr 1906) first came to Paris in 1878 as interpreter for the Kiritsu Kōshō Kaisha. He stood in the Trocadéro history pavilion explaining Japanese objects in fluent French to Impressionist painters and critics. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/林忠正
- 1889: Japan was among the states participating officially. https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exposition_universelle_de_1889

### Chicago 1893: the Hōōden and the Japanese women's exhibit in the Woman's Building
- The **Hōōden** (鳳凰殿) was modelled on the Byōdō-in. Its three wings represented the Fujiwara, Ashikaga and Tokugawa periods. It was designed by Kuru Masamichi (久留正道, a pupil of Josiah Conder), with interiors by the Tokyo Fine Arts School; Kuki Ryūichi and Okakura Kakuzō were involved. It stood on the Wooded Island, influenced Frank Lloyd Wright, and later burned. Japan's stated aims included showing "how Japan differs from China". https://www.ndl.go.jp/exposition/s1/1893-1.html
- **Japanese Woman's Commission.** Its report, quoted by Maud Howe Elliott: the Empress "generously bestowed a large gift"; "Princess Mori assumed the duty of chairman"; the first meeting was held "on the 13th of May, 1892 … at Shiba-Hama-Rikyu", and after that it met twice a month. Elliott, *Art and Handicraft in the Woman's Building* (Paris & New York: Goupil, 1893), p. 37. https://tekniskkulturarv.dk/book/41c1198e-f0cb-4007-a095-6d6877d6dfa1?page=55
- Elliott's description, p. 36:
  - An outer guest-chamber with "a raised cushion of state".
  - An inner room "sacred to the toilet of the lady of the house", with lacquered coffers and "a polished steel mirror".
  - In the second-storey Japanese parlour, a painting on silk of "Sei Shonagun, a learned Japanese woman who served the Empress Sada Ko".
  - Her verdict: **"The real secret of the peculiar charm which the Japanese women have always possessed for men of their own and the European nations lies in the fact that they are taught to be agreeable."**

  https://tekniskkulturarv.dk/book/41c1198e-f0cb-4007-a095-6d6877d6dfa1?page=54
- At the 30 April 1893 gift ceremony, Elliott's own rhetoric was: "From the far Orient comes back an answer to our cry. The slave woman of the harem murmurs, 'I hear!'" (p. 37). https://tekniskkulturarv.dk/book/41c1198e-f0cb-4007-a095-6d6877d6dfa1?page=55
- **The book *Japanese Women*.** It was "privately printed by A. C. McClurg & Company, Chicago, for the Japanese Woman's Commission for the World's Columbian Exposition, 1893". Each chapter was "undertaken by a different lady". The chapters cover Politics, Literature, Religion, Domestic Life, Industrial Occupations, Accomplishments, and the Meiji period (charities and education). The introduction says Japanese women "are misunderstood to a great extent" because foreign writers never saw their homes. The book reports that the Peeresses' School (華族女学校) opened in new buildings on 5 Oct 1885, was formally opened by the Empress on 13 Nov 1885, admitted noble daughters aged 6–18, and had 47 teachers and 362 students. https://archive.org/details/japanesewomen00worl
- Scholarship: Ajioka Kyōko, 「1893年シカゴ万国博覧会『女性館』への日本の出品」, 『人間文化論叢』 9 (2006): 1–11. The abstract says Japan spent heavily, aimed to show itself as "the only modernized country in Asia" on the eve of the Sino-Japanese War, and redefined "women's art". https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1050282677928115968
- Search-only (Lisa K. Langlois, Michigan dissertation, 2004): the exhibit consisted of three rooms (a boudoir, a library, and a gallery of women's art) known together as "A Japanese Lady's Boudoir". I could not open the page.
- The World's Congress of Representative Women ran from 15 to 21 May 1893 in the Woman's Building: 76 sessions, over 600 participants, almost 500 women speakers from 27 countries, and more than 150,000 attendances. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World%27s_Congress_of_Representative_Women

### Paris 1900: Hayashi, Sadayakko, Loie Fuller
- Hayashi Tadamasa was commissioner-general (事務官長) for Japan. Itō Hirobumi, Saionji and Prince Arisugawa recommended him for a post normally held by a vice-minister. He refused any salary and mounted a historical exhibition of Japanese art borrowed partly from the imperial collection. He reportedly told the worried Emperor that if anything was lost he would die. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/林忠正
- **Kawakami Sadayakko** (川上貞奴; real name Koyama Sada 小山貞; b. 明治4年7月18日 = 2 Sep 1871, d. 7 Dec 1946) was a Yoshichō geisha, adopted at 7 by the okiya Hamadaya. Itō Hirobumi and Saionji were her patrons. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/川上貞奴
- 1899 American tour: the troupe of 18 left Kobe on 30 April and reached San Francisco three weeks later. The promoter Kushibiki Yumindo had billed "Sadayakko", without telling her, as the troupe's star, a Japanese Sarah Bernhardt. She debuted on 25 May 1899 with the death scene of *Musume Dōjōji*. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sada_Yacco
- In Paris in 1900 the troupe played at **Loie Fuller's theatre** at the Exposition. Rodin was invited to the 4 July opening and asked to sculpt her; she declined. In August she danced *Dōjōji* at President Loubet's garden party. A kimono-like "Yacco dress" became fashionable, Gide and Picasso admired her, and the French government made her an Officier d'Académie. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/川上貞奴
- In London (Criterion Theatre, 18 June–13 July 1901, then the Shaftesbury) the company played *The Geisha and the Knight* 42 times, *Kesa* 17 times, *Zingoro* 36 times and *The Shogun* 12 times, and Loie Fuller danced between the plays. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sada_Yacco
- Judith Gautier and Louis Benedictus transcribed the music as *La Musique japonaise à l'exposition de 1900, danses de Sada-Yacco* (1900). https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judith_Gautier

### Japonisme: Burty, Goncourt, Bing, Hayashi
- Philippe Burty coined "Japonisme" in 1872. Around 1856 Félix Bracquemond found a *Hokusai Manga* in the printer Delâtre's workshop. Shops such as *La Porte Chinoise* drew Whistler, Manet and Degas. Alfred Stevens painted *La Parisienne japonaise* (1872), and Monet painted *Madame Monet en costume japonais* (1875). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japonisme
- Edmond de Goncourt (1822–1896): *La Maison d'un artiste* (1881), and *Outamaro, le peintre des maisons vertes* (1891, "painter of the green houses", i.e. brothels). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmond_de_Goncourt
- Hayashi helped with both of Goncourt's late books (*Utamaro*, *Hokusai*). Goncourt's diary (19 Mar 1885) records Hayashi saying the Japanese treat philosophical ideas like a collector with a glass case, keeping only what attracts them without asking why. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/林忠正
- Hayashi's own career:
  - He wrote the special "Le Japon" number of *Paris Illustré* (May 1886), described as the first introduction to Japan written for the West by a Japanese.

### Loti, *Madame Chrysanthème* (1887)
- Pierre Loti arrived in Nagasaki on 9 July 1885 and at once took, by a renewable one-month contract arranged by an agent and registered with the police, an 18-year-old wife, Okane-san, whom he renamed Kiku-san. He left on 12 August 1885, aged 35. The novel was published by Calmann-Lévy in 1887 (331 pp.). https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madame_Chrysanthème
- The book ran to 25 editions in its first five years and was translated into several languages. It is "considered a key text in shaping western attitudes toward Japan". The neighbourhood was Jūzenji (十善寺, now 十人町). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madame_Chrysanth%C3%A8me_(novel)
- André Messager's opera *Madame Chrysanthème* premiered at the Théâtre de la Renaissance on 21 June 1893. https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madame_Chrysanthème
- In *Japoneries d'automne* (1889) Loti compared the Rokumeikan to a mediocre spa-town casino, and its European-style ball to a "monkey show". The Rokumeikan had opened on 28 November 1883 with 1,200 guests. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rokumeikan

### *The Mikado* (1885)
- Opened 14 March 1885 at the Savoy Theatre, ran for 672 performances to 19 Jan 1887, and by the end of 1885 had an estimated 150 or more companies performing it in Europe and America. The Knightsbridge Japanese Village opened on 10 Jan 1885, after Gilbert had already outlined the plot, so the story that it inspired the opera is doubtful. Gilbert said the shortness of Leonora Braham, Jessie Bond and Sybil Grey suggested making them "three Japanese school-girls", and that "a young Japanese lady, a tea server at the Japanese Village", came to rehearsals to teach them Japanese dance (*New York Daily Tribune*, 9 Aug 1885). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mikado

### Long, Belasco, Puccini: *Madame Butterfly*
- John Luther Long's story "Madame Butterfly" (1898) drew on stories from his sister Jennie Correll and on Loti. Belasco's one-act *Madame Butterfly: A Tragedy of Japan* opened on 5 March 1900 at the Herald Square Theatre, New York, with Blanche Bates. Puccini saw it in London in summer 1900. *Madama Butterfly* premiered at La Scala on 17 Feb 1904 (Rosina Storchio) and was a fiasco. The revised version succeeded at Brescia on 28 May 1904, and the first performance outside Italy was in Buenos Aires on 7 July 1904 under Toscanini. Butterfly kills herself with her father's tantō. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madama_Butterfly ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madame_Butterfly_(play)
- Tamaki Miura (三浦環, 1884–1946) first sang Cio-Cio-San in London in Vladimir Rosing's Allied Opera Season (May–June 1915), then in Chicago and St Louis. The *St. Louis Post-Dispatch* headline of 13 Oct 1915: "Japanese Prima Donna Would Wear American Evening Gowns If She Were Not So Little." In 1918–19 she also sang Messager's *Madame Chrysanthème* in the US, where it was seen as a warmed-over *Butterfly*. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamaki_Miura

### Isabella Bird (1878 journey; book 1880)
- Bird travelled with the interpreter Itō Tsurukichi (伊藤鶴吉) from Tokyo to Hokkaido between June and September 1878, aged 46. *Unbeaten Tracks in Japan* came out in 2 vols from John Murray in 1880, with an abridged one-volume edition in 1885. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbeaten_Tracks_in_Japan
- From the text:
  - "the women so very small and tottering in their walk".
  - Watching a girl's three-hour toilet, Bird says that when finished she "looked as if a very unmeaning-looking wooden doll had been dressed up with the exquisite good taste, harmony, and quietness which characterise the dress of Japanese women".
  - "Japanese women have their own gatherings, where gossip and chit-chat, marked by a truly Oriental indecorum of speech, are the staple of talk."
  - She notes "a most rigid social etiquette" separating the dress of "the virtuous woman" from "her frail sister", which Japanese women would think it "a shame" to copy.

## 4. The Western "New Woman" context

- ***A Doll's House*.**
  - Published in Copenhagen on 4 Dec 1879 in an edition of 8,000 copies that sold out within a month; second printing of 3,000 on 4 Jan 1880, third of 2,500 on 8 Mar 1880.
  - Premiered at the Royal Danish Theatre (Betty Hennings as Nora). The body text says 21 Dec 1879; the lead says 20 Dec (see Uncertain).
  - Ibsen was forced to write a German alternative ending in which Nora stays; he called it a "barbaric outrage".
  - In England it was first read privately at Eleanor Marx's lodgings in 1886, with Shaw as Krogstad. The first public British production opened on 7 June 1889 at the Novelty Theatre with Janet Achurch.
  - US: Louisville 1883 (Helena Modjeska); Broadway 21 Dec 1889. France: 1894.
  - James Huneker (1905): "That slammed door reverberated across the roof of the world."
  - Lu Xun's 1923 speech "What happens after Nora leaves home".

  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Doll%27s_House
- Na Hye-sok, in Tokyo in the 1910s, idolised Nora and Sudermann's Magda (see §7). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/羅蕙錫
- **"New Woman" (1894).** Sarah Grand used the term in an 1894 article, and Ouida titled her reply "The New Woman". Henry James's heroines Daisy Miller (1878) and Isabel Archer (1880–81) were earlier types. New Woman fiction includes Ella Hepworth Dixon's *The Story of a Modern Woman* (1894) and *Ann Veronica* (1909). In China, New Women drew on American and European models, on missionaries, and on "Japanese feminists and New Women". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Woman
- **Cross-dressing women.**
  - Since an ordinance of November 1800, Parisian women needed a police **permission de travestissement**, renewable every six months, to wear trousers. Rosa Bonheur's permit of 1857 survives, as does one for her companion Nathalie Micas. In official photographs and in town both wore dresses. The Empress Eugénie decorated her personally on 10 June 1865, saying "le génie n'a pas de sexe". In April 1894 Bonheur became the first woman made an officer of the Légion d'honneur. https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosa_Bonheur
  - Dr Mary Edwards Walker (26 Nov 1832 – 21 Feb 1919) was captured on 10 Apr 1864 and held in Castle Thunder, Richmond, until 12 Aug 1864, refusing the clothes "more becoming of her sex". She received the Medal of Honor on 11 Nov 1865. Her name was struck from the roll in 1917 and restored in 1977. She was arrested repeatedly for men's dress and said: **"I don't wear men's clothes, I wear my own clothes."** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Edwards_Walker
- **Bicycle and rational dress.** The safety bicycle was developed between about 1885 and 1895, and drop frames, divided skirts and bloomers followed. Annie Londonderry set out in June 1894 to ride around the world. Susan B. Anthony said in 1896 that bicycling "has done more to emancipate women than anything else in the world". In 1897 the *National Review* warned of "the hidden dangers of cycling" for women. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycling_and_feminism
- **Suffrage chronology.**
  - **New Zealand:** royal assent 19 Sep 1893; women first voted 28 Nov 1893. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_in_New_Zealand
  - **Finland:** 1906, the first equal suffrage including the right to stand for parliament; 19 women MPs elected in 1907. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_by_country
  - **UK:** Representation of the People Act, 6 Feb 1918 (women over 30 with property qualifications; about 8.4 million); equal franchise in 1928. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_in_the_United_Kingdom
  - **US:** the 19th Amendment was ratified on 18 Aug 1920, when Tennessee became the 36th state, and certified on 26 Aug 1920. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_in_the_United_States ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/キャリー・チャップマン・キャット
  - **France:** ordinance of 21 April 1944; women first voted in municipal elections on 29 April 1945 and in legislative elections on 21 Oct 1945. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_by_country
  - **Japan, for comparison:** the cabinet decided on 10 Oct 1945 to give the vote to all citizens 20 and over. On 10 April 1946, 39 women were elected. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川房枝

## 5. Western women's movements and Japan

- **Mary Clement Leavitt** (22 Sep 1830 – 5 Feb 1912) was the WCTU's first round-the-world missionary. She arrived at Yokohama on **1 June 1886** with no one to meet her, and on that day met James Curtis and Clara Hepburn. Between 1 June and 12 Oct 1886 she lectured at Yokohama, Tokyo, Nikkō, Hieizan, Kyoto, Osaka, Wakayama, Sakai, Kobe, Okayama and Nagasaki. The Tokyo union was organised on **6 December 1886**, after she had left. It took the English name "Tokyo Woman's Association for Reforming Customs" (ja.wikipedia calls it 東京キリスト教婦人矯風会) and concentrated on prostitution and concubinage rather than drink. A Japanese official told her that her mission "is doing for Japanese women what Commodore Perry did for the country." She went on to Chemulpo (16 Oct 1886) and China (21 Oct 1886 – 1 Feb 1887). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Greenleaf_Clement_Leavitt
- **Yajima Kajiko** (矢嶋楫子; b. 天保4年4月24日 = 11 June 1833, d. 16 June 1925):
  - She was the aunt of Tokutomi Sohō and of Tokutomi Roka, the author of *Hototogisu*.
  - Leaving a drunken, violent husband, she cut off her hair at the root and sent it to him wrapped in paper as a wordless divorce (1868). On the steamer to Tokyo she renamed herself 楫子 ("rudder").
  - She bore a child to a married student and kept it secret all her life.
  - She was baptised in 1879 by David Thompson, the first translator of the Fairy Tale Series. She became first head of Joshi Gakuin in 1890 and first president of the Tokyo WCTU in 1886.
  - In 1906, aged 74, she attended the World WCTU convention in Boston and met Theodore Roosevelt.
  - In 1921, aged 89, she went to the Washington Conference, saying 「天国は日本からでも、米国からでも距離は同じでしょう」 ("Heaven is the same distance from Japan as from America").

  https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/矢嶋楫子
- English Wikipedia adds: Tokyo WCTU founded "in late 1886" with 28 other women. In 1920, with Tsuneko Gauntlett and Michiko Kawai, she went to temperance and suffrage conferences in London and Geneva. In 1921 she delivered to President Harding "a 300-foot-long peace petition signed by Japanese women" (*Popular Mechanics*, Jan 1922). Quote: "the women of Japan want education, not battleships or armies." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajima_Kajiko
- **Carrie Chapman Catt's world tour.** She left New York on 1 April 1911 and arrived in San Francisco on 4 Nov 1912. Her route: South Africa, East Africa, Egypt, Palestine, Lebanon, Ceylon, India, Burma, Java, Sumatra, Sulawesi, the Philippines, then China (Hong Kong, Shanghai, Peking, Nanking, Hankow), Korea and Japan, and Hawaii on the way home. Her diaries are in the Library of Congress. The IWSA congress in Geneva in 1920 had delegates from Japan and China. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrie_Chapman_Catt
- Search-only: the Wisconsin Historical Society holds a diary volume "Korea and Japan, October 4th–30th, 1912". The FromThePage transcription is login-only and I could not read it.
- **Tang Qunying** (唐群英, 1871–1937) was a schoolmate of Qiu Jin at Shimoda's school and the first woman member of the Tongmenghui (1905). In 1911 she organised a Women's Northern Expedition troop (女子北伐隊). She founded the 中華民國女子參政同盟會 (founding meeting at Nanjing on 8 Apr 1912). At the founding congress of the Guomindang on 25 Aug 1912 she climbed onto the platform and slapped Song Jiaoren over the deletion of "equal rights for men and women". Sun Yat-sen wrote to her on 2 Sep 1912: "切勿依賴男子代為出力，方不為男子所利用也" ("never rely on men to act for you, so that men cannot use you"). https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/唐群英
- Catt's diaries probably record meetings with Chinese suffragists in 1912, but I found no published account of Catt meeting Tang Qunying (see Uncertain).
- **Ichikawa Fusae** (市川房枝) went to the US in July 1921 as a *Yomiuri* correspondent. She worked in Chicago and New York, studied the women's and labour movements, and met Alice Paul and Carrie Chapman Catt. She returned in January 1924, worked for the ILO's Tokyo office (1924–27), and on 13 Dec 1924 founded the 婦人参政権獲得期成同盟会, renamed the 婦選獲得同盟 in 1925. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/市川房枝
- **Shimoda Utako** (下田歌子) inspected European girls' education from Sept 1893 to Aug 1895. She visited Cheltenham Ladies' College (headmistress Dorothea Beale), Newnham, and the Cambridge Training College. On 8 May 1895 she was received by Queen Victoria at Buckingham Palace, insisting on Japanese court dress (袿袴). Cheltenham shaped her Jissen Jogakkō (founded April 1899). https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/下田歌子
- **Tsuda Umeko** (津田梅子, 31 Dec 1864 – 16 Aug 1929) went to the United States at age 7 in 1871 on the ship of the Iwakura Mission, as Japan's first female exchange student. She later studied at Bryn Mawr and St Hilda's, Oxford, and taught at the Peeresses' School. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsuda_Umeko

## 6. China: *geming*, students in Japan, Qiu Jin, translations, tanci

### How *kakumei* became *geming*
- **Feng Ziyou's account (primary text).** In 『革命逸史』 (first collection, 1939), chapter 「"革命"二字之由來」, Feng writes:
  - Before the Xingzhonghui's failure in 1895 Chinese rebels never used the word 革命, but 造反, 起義 or 光復.
  - After the Guangzhou failure in the ninth month of yiwei (乙未九月), Sun Yat-sen, Chen Shaobai and Zheng Bichen sailed from Hong Kong to Japan. At Kobe they went ashore and bought a Japanese paper with an item headed **「支那革命黨首領孫逸仙抵日」** ("Leader of the Chinese revolutionary party Sun Yat-sen arrives in Japan").
  - Sun told Chen: 「革命二字出於《易經》湯武革命順乎天而應乎人一語，日人稱吾黨爲革命黨，意義甚佳，吾黨以後卽稱革命黨可也」 ("The word *geming* comes from the *Yijing* phrase about Tang and Wu changing the mandate in accord with Heaven and men; the Japanese call our party the revolutionary party, a fine meaning; let us henceforth call ourselves the revolutionary party").
  - Feng adds that the Japanese had first used 革命 to translate "revolution".

  https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E2%80%9C%E9%9D%A9%E5%91%BD%E2%80%9D%E4%BA%8C%E5%AD%97%E4%B9%8B%E7%94%B1%E4%BE%86
- Same book: Feng was 14 in 1895 and living in Yokohama. Days after Sun and Chen called on his father (文經活版所, 53 Yamashita-chō), they **cut off their queues** in the shop and put on Western suits made by the tailor Tan Fa (譚發). https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%AD%AB%E9%99%B3%E5%89%AA%E8%BE%AE%E6%98%93%E6%9C%8D
- **Scholarly doubt (Yasui Sankichi 安井三吉, Kobe University emeritus).** Sun arrived at Kobe on the NYK *Hiroshima Maru* around 10 Nov 1895 (光緒21年9月24日, computed). The *Kobe Yūshin Nippō* (神戸又新日報) of 10 Nov 1895 printed an article headed **「広東暴徒巨魁の履歴及計画」** ("Career and plans of the ringleader of the Canton rioters"), which named the leader as **「范某」** ("a certain Fan"). Yasui's conclusion: the Kobe press "knew nothing at all about him" (「神戸の新聞は彼のことを全く知らなかった」). Perhaps only three Japanese knew Sun then: Umeya Shōkichi, the Hong Kong consul Nakagawa Tsunejirō, and Sugawara Den. Yasui's paper does not quote a "革命黨" headline. Yasui Sankichi, 「孫文と神戸」 (Aichi University repository), p. 67–68. https://aichiu.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/8321/files/065%E2%88%92075%EF%BC%9A%E5%AE%89%E4%BA%95%E4%B8%89%E5%90%89%EF%BC%9A%E5%AD%AB%E6%96%87%E3%81%A8%E7%A5%9E%E6%88%B8.pdf
- Chen Shaobai's own version (『興中會革命史要』, 1935) is the usually cited original. I could not open it; Wikisource has no copy. Search-only: a Chinese article paraphrases Chen's memoir as saying Sun decided to use "革命党" after reading the Kobe paper.
- **Liang Qichao.**
  - He founded *Qingyi bao* in Yokohama in 1898; its first issue is dated 23 Dec 1898 = 光緒24年11月11日 (computed).
  - In 1902 he founded *Xinmin congbao* and *Xin xiaoshuo* (新小説), and published 『十五小豪傑』, a retranslation of Morita Shiken's Japanese version of Verne.

  https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/梁啓超
- His essay 「論小說與群治之關係」 appeared in *Xin xiaoshuo* vol. 1 no. 1 on 14 Nov 1902 (光緒28年10月15日, computed). It ends: **「故今日欲改良群治，必自小說界革命始！欲新民，必自新小說始！」** ("to reform the governance of the people we must begin with a *revolution in the world of fiction*!"). https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%AB%96%E5%B0%8F%E8%AA%AA%E8%88%87%E7%BE%A4%E6%B2%BB%E4%B9%8B%E9%97%9C%E4%BF%82
- In 「釋革」 (14 Dec 1902) Liang says Revolution "若轉輪然，從根柢處掀翻之，而別造一新世界，如法國一千七百八十九年之Revolution是也，日本人譯之曰革命。'革命'二字，非确譯也" ("is like turning a wheel, overturning from the roots and making a new world, like the French Revolution of 1789; the Japanese translate it as *kakumei*; the word is not an exact translation"). ### Qiu Jin 秋瑾
- Birth: 8 Nov 1875 (光緒元年10月11日) per zh/en Wikipedia; other years are given elsewhere (see Uncertain). She was born in Fujian, and her ancestral home was Shanyin, Shaoxing. Her names included 閨瑾 and 璿卿. Her studio names 競雄 ("vying with heroes") and 鑑湖女俠 ("knight-errant woman of Jian Lake") were adopted in Japan. https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/秋瑾
- As a child she went with her family to Taiwan in 1886 (via Shanghai; a typhoon at sea), returning after three months. https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/秋瑾
- **Men's clothing:** at Mid-Autumn 1903 in Beijing she went to the theatre in men's dress, causing a sensation (「秋瑾身着男装到戏院看戏，轰动一时」). https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/秋瑾
- **Japan, 1904–06.**
  - She sold her jewellery and went to Japan in May 1904. She studied first at a Japanese-language institute, then at Shimoda Utako's 実践女学校 (Kōjimachi).
  - With Chen Xiefen she founded the 共愛会 (Encompassing Love Society).
  - She joined a Triad lodge in Yokohama as 白紙扇 (strategist).
  - She joined the Guangfuhui in 1905, and the Tongmenghui in August 1905 at Huang Xing's lodgings, about two weeks after its founding.
  - She returned in February 1906 in protest against the 2 Nov 1905 regulations.

  https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/秋瑾
- **The sword.** At a meeting of Zhejiang students she drew a Japanese sword (倭刀) from her boot, stuck it into the lectern and said 「如有人回到祖國，投降滿虜，賣友求榮，欺壓漢人，吃我一刀」 ("if anyone returns home and surrenders to the Manchus … let him taste my blade"). This is Xu Shuangyun's eyewitness version. Nagata Keisuke's claim that she threatened Lu Xun is disputed. https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/秋瑾
- She was "known by her acquaintances for wearing Western male dress". She returned in 1906 along with about 2,000 students (Ono Kazuko). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qiu_Jin
- Shimoda took her first Chinese woman student in 1901 and later opened a Qing women's short course (清国女子速成科) with a branch school and dormitory. Nearly 100 Chinese women had graduated by 1911. In 1902 she co-founded the publishing house Sakushinsha (作新社) with Ji Yihui (戢翼翬), which issued Chinese translations of Japanese books. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/下田歌子
- ***Zhongguo nü bao* (中國女報).** Issue 1 is dated 光緒32年12月1日 (= 14 Jan 1907, computed) and issue 2 光緒33年正月20日 (= 4 Mar 1907, computed). Only these two issues appeared. https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/秋瑾
- **Death.** The Datong school (大通學堂) was raided on 14 July 1907, and she was arrested at 4 p.m. Her "confession" was the single line **「秋風秋雨愁煞人」** ("autumn wind, autumn rain: they grieve one to death"). She was beheaded at Xuanting kou (古軒亭口), Shaoxing, at 3–4 a.m. on **15 July 1907** (光緒33年6月6日, computed), aged 31 sui. Contemporary opinion, even among conservatives, thought beheading a woman too harsh, and the officials then cracked down on girls' schools. Sun Yat-sen visited her grave on 10 Dec 1912 with the couplet 「江戶矢丹忱，重君首贊同盟會；軒亭灑碧血，愧我今招俠女魂」 ("In Edo you swore loyalty, and I honour you for being first to endorse the Tongmenghui; at Xuanting your blood was spilled, and I am ashamed now to call back the heroine's soul"). The grave was blown up in 1965 and rebuilt in 1981. https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/秋瑾
- **Her tanci.** Between 1905 and 1907 Qiu wrote *Stones of the Jingwei Bird* (精衛石) "in traditional ballad form", a genre composed by and for women. In it five wealthy girls flee arranged marriages to study and make revolution in Tokyo. The unfinished chapter titles point to "education, manufacturing, military activities, speechmaking, and direct political action, eventually overthrowing the Qing dynasty and establishing a republic" (Hershatter 2019). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qiu_Jin

### Chinese translations and stagings of Japanese and Japanese-mediated fiction, c. 1898–1915
- Lin Shu translated over 180 works; Tarumoto Teruo counts 213. A Ying's count includes "日本一部，一冊" (one Japanese work, in one volume), which is 不如歸 (1908, with Wei Yi). Lin knew no foreign language and worked from oral interpreters. https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/林紓
- Hototogisu's afterlife in China: a Chinese film, 『不如帰』 (国光公司, 1926). In 1939 Feng Zhifen adapted it as the Cantonese opera 『胡不歸』, which led to seven Hong Kong films between 1940 and 1966. A Chinese verse said "國恥と痴情" (national shame and lovesick passion) made it too painful to read. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/不如帰_(小説)
- **Spring Willow Society (春柳社).**
  - It was founded in Tokyo in late 1906 by Chinese students, guided by the shinpa dramatist Fujisawa Asajirō.
  - Its first show, early in 1907, was Act 3 of *La Dame aux camélias*, with **Li Shutong in the title role** (men played all female parts). This was probably from a Japanese translation, with reference to Lin Shu's Chinese.
  - It staged *Black Slave's Cry to Heaven* (Uncle Tom's Cabin) on 1–2 June 1907 at the Hongō-za, and *Hot Blood* (from a Japanese adaptation of *Tosca*) in 1909.
  - The Chinese legation threatened in 1909 to cut the stipends of student actors.
  - Members continued as the New Drama Comrades in Shanghai (1912–15), with an *Othello* adapted from Kawakami Otojirō's Japanese version.

  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_Willow_Society
- Chen Xiefen (陳撷芬) founded China's first women's paper, 《女學報》, in Shanghai on 24 July 1898. It was closed after the 1898 coup, having published 12 issues. https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/女學報

### The tanci tradition of cross-dressed heroines
- *Zaisheng yuan* (再生緣), by Chen Duansheng (陳端生), is a 17-juan unfinished tanci of more than 600,000 characters in seven-character verse, rhymed in Hangzhou dialect. Liang Desheng (梁德繩) added 3 juan, and Hou Zhi (侯芝) reissued it in 80 chapters. Its heroine **Meng Lijun** (孟麗君) flees a forced marriage in men's clothes under the name Li Junyu (酈君玉). She passes the examinations, rises to chief minister, and resists unmasking because she does not want to return to the single destiny allowed to women. Her adopted sister Su Yingxue (蘇映雪) follows her. https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/再生緣 ; https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/彈詞
- The three most famous women's tanci are *Zaishengyuan*, *Tianyuhua* (Tao Zhenhuai) and *Bishenghua* (Qiu Xinru). Cheng Huiying's *Feng shuangfei* (鳳雙飛, printed 1897–99) treats gender and sexual politics. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanci
- **Yue opera (越劇)**, from Shengzhou, Shaoxing (1906), became famous for actresses in male roles. Wang Jinshui opened the first all-girl training class in February 1923, and Yue opera later dominated Shanghai with mostly female performers and audiences (Jiang Jin, *Women Playing Men*, 2009). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yue_opera

## 7. Korea and Taiwan

### Korea
- Girls' schools:
  - **Ewha** (梨花學堂) was founded on 31 May 1886 by Mary Scranton; the name was conferred by King Gojong in 1887. Students in the 1900s came to school wearing the *sseugae-chima* veil, and wore uniforms from 1904. https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/이화여자대학교
  - **Sookmyung** began as Myeongsin Girls' School on 22 May 1906 under Imperial Consort Sunheon. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sookmyung_Women%27s_University
  - **Keijō Daiichi Kōjo** (京城第一公立高等女學校), a school for Japanese girls, opened on 26 Apr 1908 with 84 pupils; Korean girls attended only occasionally. In 1922 it moved to the former Seonwonjeon site of Deoksugung. Each year had four classes (松竹梅菊), about 200 pupils. https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/경성제일공립고등여학교
- **Na Hye-sok** (羅蕙錫, 28 Apr 1896 – 10 Dec 1948):
  - She studied Western painting at the Joshi Bijutsu Gakkō in Tokyo from April 1913 to 1918 (teachers included Okada Saburōsuke). She refused a forced marriage and taught for a year to pay her own fees.
  - She admired Hiratsuka Raichō, Yosano Akiko, Magda and Nora, and wrote 「理想的婦人」 (*Hakjigwang*, Dec 1914) and the story 「瓊姫」 (*Yeojagye*, Mar 1918).
  - She was jailed for five months after the March First movement (1919).
  - In 1920 she married on conditions: that her husband let her paint freely and that he put up a monument to her dead lover.
  - In April 1921 she held Korea's first solo oil-painting show while nine months pregnant.
  - She toured Europe 1927–29 and had an affair in Paris with Choe Rin; divorced 1930. Her 『離婚告白状』 appeared in *Samcheolli* in 1934.
  - She died in a Seoul old people's home.

  https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/羅蕙錫
- **Kim Myeong-sun** (金明淳, 1896–1951) studied in Tokyo and was a Sookmyung graduate (1917). She published the poetry collection 『生命の果実』 (1925). Attacked by Confucian critics, she ended her life in poverty and mental illness in Tokyo. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/金明淳
- The magazine *Sinyeoseong* (新女性) was published in the 1920s. The term "new woman" probably came via Japan (Tsubouchi Shōyō used "new woman" in the 1910s). https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/신여성
- Japanese popular fiction in Korea: *Hototogisu* was published in Korea in 1912 and was one of the three bestsellers of the 1910s. *Konjiki yasha* was adapted as 長恨夢. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/不如帰_(小説) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/金色夜叉
- **Hong Ok-im (홍옥임) and Kim Yong-ju (김용주).**
  - They met at high school; Korean sources say Dongduk Girls' High School (search-only). On **8 April 1931**, at about 4:15 or 4:45 p.m., they threw themselves in front of train no. 428 about 2 km from Yeongdeungpo station.
  - Hong, 21, was the daughter of the Severance Hospital ophthalmologist Hong Seok-hu and had entered Ewha's music department a week before. Kim, 19, was the daughter of a large bookseller-publisher; she had been made to leave school to marry, was abandoned by a husband who went to Tokyo to train as a pilot, and was refused readmission as a married woman.
  - The press called it Korea's first same-sex love suicide. A month earlier they had tried to drown themselves together. Their note said "the world is meaningless".
  - A week later plans began for a film, *Woman of Tomorrow*, which was never made. The scholar Lee Young-ah doubts homosexuality was the cause.

  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Ok-im_and_Kim_Yong-ju

- **Taipei Third Girls' High School** (today's Zhongshan Girls'):
  - 1897: founded as the girls' branch of the Government-General Japanese-Language School's First Attached School (臺灣總督府國語學校第一附屬學校女子分教場), for Taiwanese girls.
  - 1919: 臺灣公立臺北女子高等普通學校.
  - 1922: renamed 臺北州立臺北第三高等女學校 under nominal Japanese–Taiwanese co-education.

  https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/臺北市立中山女子高級中學
- **Taipei First Girls' High School** (臺北州立臺北第一高等女學校, mainly for Japanese girls) was founded in 1904. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taipei_First_Girls%27_High_School
- **Yang Qianhe** (楊千鶴, 1 Sep 1921 – 16 Oct 2011) was a graduate of the Taihoku Women's Higher Academy and a schoolgirl table-tennis champion. In 1941 she joined the *Taiwan Nichinichi Shinpō* as Taiwan's first woman reporter, insisting on the same pay as Japanese staff. She resigned in protest in 1942 over the paper's kōminka line. Her Japanese-language story 「花開時節」 (1942) is about girls'-school graduates caught between marriage and selfhood, and about friendship among them. https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/楊千鶴

## 8. The West's reception of Takarazuka (1938–39) and of shōjo culture after the war

- **1938–39 Europe tour.**
  - The "訪独伊芸術使節団" was 30 performers led by Amatsu Otome, 56 people in all, with Kobayashi Yonezō as company head. They sailed from Kobe on the *Yasukuni Maru* on 2 Oct 1938 and played Germany (Berlin, Dresden, Düsseldorf, Munich, Leipzig, Cologne and others), Italy (Rome, Florence, Venice, Milan, Naples and others), Poland (Warsaw, and Breslau, then German) and Fiume. They came home on the *Fushimi Maru* in German-made uniforms.
  - A documentary, 『日・独・伊親善宝塚振袖使節道中記』, was made in May 1939.

  https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- Details from Kinugasa Yayoi, 「演劇をめぐるファシズム期イタリアの文化政策」:
  - The troupe landed at Naples on 2 Nov 1938, filmed by LUCE newsreel cameras, in furisode and hakama dyed with cherry blossoms. It was billed as a goodwill mission for the first anniversary of the Anti-Comintern Pact.
  - It gave **34 performances in 25 cities**, including a hastily added show in Warsaw.
  - Berlin: a trial show on 14 Nov and the main run on 20–23 Nov 1938. The German provincial tour ran from 28 Dec 1938 to 26 Jan 1939. They left Naples on 29 Jan and reached Kobe on 4 Mar 1939.
  - They were in Berlin during **Kristallnacht** (9–10 Nov 1938), the subject of Iwabuchi Tatsuji's book 『水晶の夜、タカラヅカ』.
  - The general director was Hata Toyokichi (秦豊吉). Audiences at Fiume and Venice were lukewarm; Bologna, Genoa, Turin and Milan were full houses.
  - Mussolini and the royal family attended the second Rome night (15 Dec 1938). The girls sang the Fascist hymn with the audience while gazing at his box.
  - The cheapest seats were 5 lire.

  https://repository.kulib.kyoto-u.ac.jp/dspace/handle/2433/243185
- **1939 US tour.**
  - The "訪米芸術使節団" was 40 performers led by Sayo Fukuko, 60 people in all. It ran from 5 Apr to 4 July 1939: out on the *Kamakura Maru*, then Honolulu, San Francisco, Los Angeles, New York and Portland. Colour film survives from Vancouver, 21 June 1939, and photographs from a visit to the RKO studios. They returned from Seattle on the *Hikawa Maru*.

  https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団
- *Seattle Times*, 14 June 1939, "Takarazuka Maidens Fancy American — Also American Men":
  - "Forty squealing Japanese show girls bounced off a train at the Union Station."
  - Tom C. Girton of San Francisco managed "their appearance at both world fairs". The girls were 16–23, "All are members of wealthy families", and "None of the girls can speak English".
  - The star "Fukanko Sayo" was billed as the **"Japanese Jeanette MacDonald"** and said American men are "very nice". They played the Music Hall Theatre and sailed on the *Hikawa Maru*.

  https://ddr.densho.org/media/ddr-densho-56/ddr-densho-56-493-mezzanine-f2b3c438dd.html
- The "star" was **Sayo Fukuko** (小夜福子, 1909–1989), a Moon Troupe **otokoyaku** (male-role player) and troupe head from 1939. In 1989 she recalled the tour on *Tetsuko no heya*. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/小夜福子
- William Peterson's abstract (in *Asian Self-Representation at World's Fairs*, Amsterdam UP, 2020) speaks of "the genderbending performing arts company, Takarazuka in May, 1939". He argues its reception suggests Americans would not give up their image of Japan as a land of cherry blossoms and willowy maidens. https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/asian-selfrepresentation-at-worlds-fairs/performing-japan-in-the-world-of-tomorrow-japan-at-the-19391940-new-york-worlds-fair/52AFEB8C5500A789478669F84C627089
- **Postwar.** The Japanese-Italian film 『蝶々夫人』 (*Madama Butterfly*) was shot at Cinecittà in 1954. Yachigusa Kaoru starred, and 15 Takarazuka members flew to Rome on 2 Oct 1954. Production cost was about ¥200 million, and the trip set off a rush of overseas tours. Later tours: Hawaii 1955, 1956, 1957 and 1966; Canada and the US 1959. *The Rose of Versailles* (1974) revived Takarazuka's popularity. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/宝塚歌劇団 ; https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/다카라즈카_가극단
- **Shōjo manga in English.** Translations began in North America in the late 1990s and found an untapped female readership, with *Sailor Moon*, *Boys Over Flowers* and *Fruits Basket* as bestsellers. Viz later ran the Shojo Beat imprint and magazine. The early-20th-century French illustrated magazines and Art Nouveau that shaped Japanese shōjo illustration (Yumeji, Nakahara, Takabatake) were the subject of an exhibition at the Kyoto International Manga Museum. *The Rose of Versailles* (1972–73) was the first major critical and commercial success of shōjo manga. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sh%C5%8Djo_manga

## Telling details

- In Paris in 1867 the most popular Japanese "exhibit" was three Yanagibashi geisha (Osumi, Okane and Osato) spinning tops and smoking kiseru in a hinoki teahouse. Their merchant-sponsor got a silver medal from Napoleon III; the shogun's samurai got none.
- In 1862 a Japanese envoy looked at Japan's first appearance at a world's fair and wrote that it was "all junk like old curios": straw raincoats, lanterns and sandals from the British minister's own collection.
- In Vienna in 1873 the Empress Elisabeth's ladies-in-waiting folded up wood shavings from the Japanese carpenters' planes and carried them home as souvenirs.
- Gilbert's "three little maids from school" were three short actresses, coached in Japanese movement by a tea-server lent from the Knightsbridge Japanese Village.
- In 1893 an American art writer put the "secret" of Japanese women's charm in one sentence: "they are taught to be agreeable". In the same building, the Japanese women's own commission, chaired by Princess Mōri, printed a book insisting foreigners had "misunderstood" them and opening with a chapter on women in politics.
- Loti's "Madame Chrysanthème" was an 18-year-old called Okane, rented by monthly contract registered with the Nagasaki police on the day he arrived, 9 July 1885.
- The first "Madama Butterfly" in London (1915) was a Japanese soprano. St Louis headlined that she "Would Wear American Evening Gowns If She Were Not So Little".
- Hototogisu's line "I'll never be born a woman again" came, Roka said, from a widow he met at Zushi. The real heroine was Ōyama Nobuko. The novel turned her stepmother, Ōyama Sutematsu (Japan's first woman to study abroad, and who had had short nursing training in America and isolated the tubercular girl for medical reasons), into a cold, English-spouting villain. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/不如帰_(小説)
- *Konjiki yasha*, the great Meiji melodrama, was secretly built on an English penny novel (*Weaker than a Woman*, 1878), then exported back to English as *The Gold Demon* (1905) and on to Korea as *Changhanmong*.
- Hasegawa thought of crêpe paper "suddenly". The first translator of his fairy-tale books, David Thompson, also baptised Yajima Kajiko, and the second, Hepburn, greeted Mary Leavitt on the Yokohama dock.
- Yajima Kajiko divorced her husband by sending him her severed hair in paper, renamed herself "Rudder" on the boat to Tokyo, and at 89 told those trying to keep her home that heaven was the same distance from Washington as from Tokyo. At Atlantic City a hotel flew the Hinomaru in her honour; she thought some important Japanese must be staying there. (ja.wikipedia citing Miura Tamaki's 『お蝶夫人』, 1947.)
- Rosa Bonheur needed a police permit renewed every six months to wear trousers to the horse fairs, but always wore a dress for photographs. Mary Walker: "I don't wear men's clothes, I wear my own clothes."
- Sun Yat-sen cut off his queue in a Yokohama print-shop in November 1895, and a 14-year-old boy watched. The Kobe paper of 10 Nov 1895 did not call him a "revolutionary"; it called him "the ringleader of the Canton rioters, a certain Fan".
- Liang Qichao complained in 1902 that students back from Japan went about shouting "*geming, geming*" because the Japanese had used that word.
- Qiu Jin pulled a Japanese sword from her boot, stabbed it into a lectern in Tokyo, and wrote a tanci about five girls who run away to Tokyo to make a republic. Her only "confession" before beheading was a single line of verse about autumn wind and rain.
- Tang Qunying, Qiu Jin's schoolmate at Shimoda's school, slapped Song Jiaoren on the platform at the Guomindang's founding congress (25 Aug 1912) when "equality of men and women" was dropped from the platform.
- Li Shutong, later the monk Hongyi, made China's first spoken-drama heroine in Tokyo in 1907, playing Marguerite in *La Dame aux camélias*.
- Two Seoul schoolgirls from prominent families jumped in front of train no. 428 near Yeongdeungpo on 8 April 1931. A week later someone began planning a film called *Woman of Tomorrow*.
- The reader columns of *Shōjo no tomo* used a boyish "kimi/boku" register among girls. A 1910 magazine described the senior–junior "o-me" courtship: a go-between handed over a purple or crimson ribbon, and the younger girl accepted by tying it in her hair. https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/エス_(文化) ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/少女の友
- Takarazuka's girls were rehearsing in Berlin during Kristallnacht, sang the Fascist hymn to Mussolini's box on 15 Dec 1938, and six months later were marketed in Seattle with an otokoyaku billed as the "Japanese Jeanette MacDonald".
- The 1954 Takarazuka film venture was a *Madama Butterfly* shot at Mussolini's Cinecittà, so the company that had performed for Mussolini returned to Italy to play Puccini's suicidal geisha.
- In wartime Taiwan, Taiwanese-opera actresses were made to perform in kimono with samurai swords. A decade later the same genre staged Qiu Jin, the "Knight-errant Woman of Jian Lake".
