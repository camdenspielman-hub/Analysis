I

The premise and the rules of the game

What appears, where, and what is held fixed

On Thursday, 2 April 1885, Jiyū no Tomoshibi (自由燈, "The Lamp of Liberty"), a Tokyo "small newspaper" of the kind that printed its kanji with readings beside them so that shop clerks and their wives could follow, carried the first installment of a new serial novel. Its heading read 少女をとめ革命かくめいウテナ, Otome Kakumei Utena, and under it, where an author's name would go, stood three characters nobody recognised: 鳳雛子.

Over the next two years, in four parts and thirty-nine chapters, interrupted twice by the police and finished in book form, that serial told the whole story of the 1997 television series Revolutionary Girl Utena: Utena Tenjō in her boy's jacket, Anthy Himemiya and the duels for the Rose Bride, the Student Council and its creed of the chick and the egg, the Black Rose, Akio Ōtori and the night rides, the Rose Gate, and Anthy walking out of the academy at the end. This document works out what follows. Where the record allows, it uses documented history. Where the record runs out, it reasons from the nearest documented case and says so.

The idea comes from a forum thread that asked what the classical world would have made of Tolkien's legendarium if it had been found in antiquity, and from an earlier companion piece that sent the first Magic: The Gathering cards back to 1593. That piece transported the cards unchanged. A television series cannot travel that way. Thirty-nine half-hours of animation, with their sports car and planetarium and electric lift, would be magic in any century before the twentieth, and magic is the wrong genre for this story. So the first rule here is different.

The rules of the thought experiment

A novel, not a relic. What appears in 1885 is a work of fiction written in 1885, by someone living then, in the language, print technology and visual conventions of a Tokyo newspaper of that year. Nothing comes from the future, and nobody in this history ever learns that the story existed anywhere else. The premise is simply that this book was written. The question of where its author got the story does not arise, any more than it arises for Genji.

The story intact. Every episode has a chapter, in order. The events, the people, their names and their relations are the series'; so is everything the series refuses to explain (what Akio shows his passengers, who lit the fire at Nemuro Memorial Hall, what happens to Utena). What changes is the furniture. Where the anime uses a machine or a custom 1885 did not have, the serial uses the nearest thing an 1885 reader could picture, and one that carries the same charge where possible: Akio's sports car becomes a light carriage he drives himself, the planetarium becomes an orrery and a magic lantern, blood types become the family register. Part IV gives all thirty-nine chapters side by side with the episodes.

An author nobody can name. The byline is a pen name and stays one. I do not decide who wrote the serial. Part XIV sets out what the scholars of this timeline argue, and leaves it there.

Everything else is real. Laws, prices, people, newspapers, wars and institutions behave as the record says they behaved until the serial gives them a reason to behave otherwise. When I say that something happened differently, I say why, and what in the record makes it plausible.

Real people. Real people appear in this history, because a story that mattered in Meiji Japan would have mattered to them. What they do differently is labelled, and kept to things their own records make likely. I never put invented words in a real person's mouth. The quoted letters, notices and reviews in Part XVI are by invented people.

Impact is allowed. A cultural object cannot rewrite a constitution by itself. It can tip decisions that were already close, give a movement a vocabulary, a set of symbols and an audience, and change what a generation takes for granted. When it does, I follow the consequences forward, through law and money as well as literature, and do not stop at listing what might have changed.

How to read the labels

Three labels run through the whole document. documented marks real history, sourced in Part XVIII. inference marks what follows from documented conditions once the serial exists: a prediction, argued, that a reader could dispute. choice marks a place where several outcomes were plausible and I picked one; those are the places to push back. A sentence with no label is either the serial's own content (which is the anime's) or connective tissue. The buttons in the contents rail highlight any one kind.

What this document is not

It is not a novel, and it is not a summary of the anime. It is a systematic working-out of one premise: the serial itself (Parts III and IV); how its first readers read it (V); a chronicle from 1885 to 2026 in five parts (VI to XI), with a detour abroad (VIII); its economy (XII); what it did not change (XIII); the authorship question (XIV); its texts and editions (XV); a set of period documents (XVI); and the two timelines side by side (XVII).

A note on names. Japanese names are given family name first when they are real people of the period (Kishida Toshiko, Tsubouchi Shōyō) and in the anime's familiar Western order when they are the serial's characters (Utena Tenjō, Akio Ōtori), as English writing about both usually does. Long vowels are marked (Ōtori, Tōga) in running prose; the anime's English spellings ("Ohtori", "Touga") appear only where they are the series' own.
