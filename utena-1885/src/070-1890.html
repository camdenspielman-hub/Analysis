VII

Chronicle, 1890–1912

The backlash, the underground, and the girls who grew up on it

From 1890 the state built the family it wanted: a household under its head, a wife who was a minor in law, daughters schooled to be good wives and wise mothers. Utena could not stop any of it. What it did, through the twenty years in which the new order took hold, was to supply that order's daughters with a counter-story and a private language, in dormitories the state had built and could not see into.

1891–1893The reaction

January 1891. At the First Higher School the Christian teacher Uchimura Kanzō hesitates to bow low enough before the emperor's signature on the Rescript on Education, and is hounded from his post; a campaign against Christianity as disloyal follows, led by the philosopher Inoue Tetsujirō. documented In this timeline the campaign's pamphlets cite Utena as an example of what Christian schools teach girls: to love each other and to despise the household. The mission schools on the Bluff, where the serial was born, lose pupils for the rest of the decade, as many real mission schools did. documented for the decline; choice for the pamphlets.

January 1891. Shimizu Toyoko, writing as Shikin, publishes The Broken Ring in the Jogaku zasshi: a woman who leaves a husband who keeps a mistress, and keeps the ring whose stone has fallen out as a reminder of what she will spend her life working to end. It is counted as the first feminist novel in Japanese. documented In this timeline every reader of 1891 sees the ring as the answer to Utena's: a ring not from a prince but from a husband, not a key to a castle but a broken thing kept on purpose. inference It is the first important work of a genre that will last for a century, fiction in conversation with Utena.

1891–1892. The Civil Code controversy. Hozumi Yatsuka's essay, "If the Civil Code comes out, loyalty and filial piety will perish", rallies the opponents of the French-inspired draft; in 1892 the Diet votes to postpone it. documented In this timeline the code's opponents use the serial's last chapter as an image of what the liberal draft would produce: daughters walking out of their brothers' houses. choice The postponement passes, as it did. documented

1893. The Ministry of Education instructs the girls' schools under its supervision to forbid "novels harmful to morals" in dormitories. choice (Part XVI, document 8, imitates the rules that follow.) In our history the ministry sent the girls' high schools a confidential instruction to tighten discipline over their pupils' daily lives in September 1902, and in June 1906 the minister, Makino Nobuaki, told every school to forbid, "inside or outside school", reading that preached "pessimism" or depicted "base passions", and complained of students who "agonise over fantasies". documented In this timeline the same steps come nine years earlier, with one book in view. The ban does what bans do. A book that girls had read openly becomes one that they read under the blankets, lend in wrapped covers and quote in code. inference

1890–1898Women on the stage

August 1890. The Metropolitan Police announce that men and women may henceforth act together on the same stage, ending a ban that went back to the Edo period. The papers print it in small type, and no major theatre hires an actress. The same month's theatre regulations create a class of "small theatres". documented

1891–1893. The first of the small theatres, the Misaki-za, opens in Kanda on 27 June 1891, on a former army drill ground. In 1893 it becomes the home of a permanent company of women players, the heirs of the women who had performed kabuki for the shogun's and the lords' inner quarters, under Ichikawa Kumehachi, "the female Danjūrō", Danjūrō IX's only woman pupil, whom he expelled that same year for playing Kanjinchō without his leave. For more than ten years the Misaki-za plays every month, to houses with many young women in them, and students, and literary men. documented In this timeline the company's first new piece, in the autumn of 1893, is Utena, with every part played by a woman for the first time: Kumehachi, forty-six and famous for her men, plays Akio, and the pit fills with schoolgirls in maroon hakama. choice The critic Okamoto Kidō, who saw Kumehachi play the mountain witch in 1890, wrote that she had been born forty years too early. documented In this timeline the Misaki-za Utena, revived almost every year while the company lasted, is the bridge between the Edo women's troupes and the Takarazuka of 1914. inference

1888–1898. The craze for the girl chanters of gidayū. Takemoto Ayanosuke, who had grown up with a boy's crop, was called "the man-woman" and performed in a boy's topknot, and her rival Kotosa, both in their early teens, drew student fan clubs, the dōsuru-ren, who chased their rickshaws and fought over their fallen hairpins; in 1900 the Yorozu chōhō printed the names and addresses of leading fans as "the corruption of youth". documented In this timeline a gidayū text of the duel in the forest garden, newly written in 1889, is Ayanosuke's most requested piece. choice

1890sThe rose and the bride

The girls' school friendship culture that our history calls "S", after "sister", took shape in the 1900s and 1910s: intense attachments between older and younger girls, exchanged letters and photographs, and a moral panic about them. documented (Part XVIII) In this timeline it takes shape a decade earlier, in the 1890s, in the mission schools and the new public girls' schools, and it takes its forms from Utena. inference An older girl and a younger one are a "prince" and a "bride"; the relationship itself is "a rose" (薔薇), and to have one is to be "engaged"; a white rose left in a desk is a proposal, where in our history, as a girls' magazine reported in 1910, the proposal was a purple or crimson ribbon delivered by the older girl's friends, which the younger accepted by tying it in her hair documented; a cheap brass ring with a rose stamped on it is worn on a ribbon under the collar. choice Rose became the code word for love between girls, and stayed one. One consequence lies far ahead: in our timeline "rose" (bara) became Japanese slang for gay men after a men's magazine took the name in 1971, and "lily" (yuri) became the word for love between women in answer to it. In this one the flowers are the other way round. documented for our history's terms; choice for the inversion.

Speech went the same way. The new schoolgirl slang of the 1890s, with its clipped -teyo and -dawa endings, appalled older writers in both timelines. documented In this one it also kept Utena's boku, which girls used among themselves and dropped at home. The magazine Shōjo no tomo would later be famous for its readers' "kimi and boku" slang; in this timeline that is simply how its readers' mothers talked. documented for the magazine's slang; choice for the rest.

1894–1899War, and the code

1894–95. The war with China. The stage turns to war plays, Kawakami Otojirō's troupe among them, and the serial's audience turns to the news. documented for the war plays. The only wartime Utena is a shinpa piece in which the prince's sword is a Japanese officer's; it closes in a week. choice

1896. Wakamatsu Shizuko dies of tuberculosis in February, five days after the Meiji Girls' School burns; Higuchi Ichiyō dies of the same disease in November, at twenty-four, having published Child's Play (Takekurabe), whose heroine Midori, a spirited girl at the centre of the neighbourhood children's quarrels, is destined to follow her sister into the licensed quarter. documented In this timeline critics have argued for a century over whether Midori is Ichiyō's answer to Utena: the girl who holds her own among the children, and whom the adult world is waiting to take. choice Nothing in Ichiyō's diaries settles it.

16 July 1898. The family and succession books of the Civil Code take effect: the household head controls the family's property and members' marriages and residence; the wife is legally incapable of most acts without her husband's consent; the household passes to the eldest son. documented (Part XVIII) In this timeline the articles on wives' legal incapacity are known, within a year, as "the Rose Bride articles": the wife, like Anthy, can do nothing on her own account and belongs to whoever holds the household. choice The name does not change the law. It changes how the law is talked about for the next forty-nine years, and it is the reason those articles are the first thing reformers try to remove (Part IX). inference

1899. The Girls' High School Order makes a secondary school for girls compulsory in every prefecture and defines its aim as "good wives and wise mothers"; Japan's first modern Copyright Act is passed and Japan joins the Berne Convention. documented Under the new act, an anonymous work is protected for a fixed term from publication, after which it falls to the public. documented (to confirm term) For Utena, whose author never came forward, that sets a clock. Shun'yōdō and the newspaper's successor dispute the rights in 1900–02 and settle (Part XII).

1900–1905The second generation

10 March 1900. The Public Peace Police Law keeps women out of political associations and political meetings, in its Article 5. documented The first organised campaign against Article 5, by socialist women, would begin in 1905. documented In this timeline it begins in 1900, with a petition circulated through the alumnae associations of girls' schools, whose members had, in many cases, first met in a "rose". choice It fails. inference

Summer 1900. In Paris, the actress Kawakami Sadayakko plays at Loïe Fuller's theatre at the Exposition Universelle, and plays a fragment of Utena among her pieces (Part VIII). choice

1901. Japan Women's University opens; Kishida Toshiko dies in May. documented Her obituaries in this timeline mention the authorship theory, which she never addressed in public. choice

1902. Shōjo kai, the first magazine for girls, appears, followed by Shōjo sekai (1906), Shōjo no tomo (1908) and Shōjo gahō (1912). documented In this timeline the editors who founded them knew exactly whom they were selling to: the daughters and younger sisters of the girls who had read Utena in 1885, now organised into a reading public by fifteen years of a banned book. inference What the girls' magazines printed most, after poetry and readers' letters, was a genre with no equivalent in our timeline: the "Anthy story" (アンシー物), in which a girl leaves home with a trunk and a small animal and goes looking for someone she has lost. Hundreds were printed between 1902 and 1930, almost all by amateurs. choice They are where the Japanese girls' novel learned its first plot.

1903. Kosugi Tengai's Makaze koikaze in the Yomiuri: a brilliant schoolgirl on a bicycle, her "sworn sister" a viscount's daughter, a love story that sells out the paper. documented In this timeline it is the first best-seller about schoolgirls that is not about Utena, and its success is read as proof that the genre had outgrown its source. inference

1904–1905. The war with Russia. Thousands of Chinese students come to Tokyo, among them a young woman from Shaoxing named Qiu Jin, who wears Western men's clothes, carries a Japanese sword, and goes home to plan a revolution (Part VIII). documented

1906–1912Toward the Bluestockings

1905–1909. The women of the socialist Heiminsha, among them Sakai Tameko and Nishikawa Fumiko, petition the Diet year after year to amend Article 5; Fukuda Hideko's Sekai fujin, founded in January 1907, campaigns for it until the authorities close the paper in 1909. Nothing changes until 1922. documented In this timeline the petitions carry many more signatures, because the girls' school networks of the 1890s sign alongside the socialists, and a member of the Peers quotes Utena on the floor, against them. choice

1908. Kawakami Sadayakko and her husband open the Imperial Actresses' Training Institute in Tokyo, for girls of sixteen to twenty-five with two guarantors; Ichikawa Kumehachi, who had founded a smaller actresses' school with a dancer two years before, teaches there. In May 1911 the new Imperial Theatre stages its first production with actresses. documented The institute's first public performance in this timeline is the ball scene from Utena, with women in both parts. choice

25 January 1911. Kanno Suga, journalist, socialist and the only woman among the twelve executed for the alleged plot against the emperor, is hanged. documented The government's case against the "High Treason" defendants reaches into what they read; in this timeline the prosecution's list of seized books includes a copy of Utena with Kanno's notes in the margins of chapter 38. choice No one has seen it since 1945.

September 1911. Hiratsuka Raichō and four other young women found Seitō, the "Bluestocking", whose first issue opens with her manifesto: "In the beginning, woman was the sun." documented The same month, on 22–24 September, the Literary Society gives Ibsen's A Doll's House on the private stage in Tsubouchi Shōyō's garden, and in November at the Imperial Theatre, with Matsui Sumako as Nora, the wife who leaves her husband's house and slams the door. documented In our history Nora became the emblem of the "new woman" in Japan. In this one she arrives as a second Anthy, and a reviewer calls her "the Norwegian Anthy": a woman who leaves with no destination, twenty-four years after the first one did. choice The comparison is not flattering to Nora, who has a husband to slam the door on. Anthy had only a brother and a world.

1912. Otake Kazue, a painter's daughter of nineteen who joins Seitō that year under the boy's name Kōkichi and wears men's kimono, falls in love with Raichō; their affair, her glass of "five-coloured liquor" at a bar and a visit to a Yoshiwara brothel to see the women there make her the press's favourite "new woman", and she leaves the society in October. documented The papers in this timeline do not need to invent a nickname for Kōkichi. Every reader already has one. On 30 July the Meiji emperor dies. documented
