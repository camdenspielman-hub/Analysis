VII

Chronicle, 1890–1912

The backlash, the underground, and the girls who grew up on it

From 1890 the state built the family it wanted: a household under its head, a wife who was a minor in law, daughters schooled to be good wives and wise mothers. Utena could not stop any of it. What it did, through the twenty years in which the new order took hold, was to supply that order's daughters with a counter-story and a private language, in dormitories the state had built and could not see into.

1888–1898Women on the stage

1888–1898. The craze for the girl chanters of gidayū. Takemoto Ayanosuke, who had grown up with a boy's crop, was called "the man-woman" and performed in a boy's topknot, and her rival Kotosa, both in their early teens, drew student fan clubs, the dōsuru-ren, who chased their rickshaws and fought over their fallen hairpins; in 1900 the Yorozu chōhō denounced the young men "crazed by such wenches as the girl chanters" as "the great corruption of youth". documented In this timeline a gidayū text of the duel in the forest garden, newly written in 1889, is Ayanosuke's most requested piece. choice

August 1890. The Metropolitan Police announce that men and women may henceforth act together on the same stage, ending a ban that went back to the Edo period; the critic Okamoto Kidō, who recorded it, remembered that the papers printed it in small type and that no major theatre hired an actress. The same month's theatre regulations create a class of "small theatres". documented

1891–1893. The first of the small theatres, the Misaki-za, opens in Kanda on 27 June 1891, on a former army drill ground. In 1893 it becomes the home of a permanent company of women players, the heirs of the women who had performed kabuki for the shogun's and the lords' inner quarters, under Ichikawa Kumehachi, "the female Danjūrō", Danjūrō IX's only woman pupil, whom he expelled that same year for playing Kanjinchō without his leave. For more than ten years the Misaki-za plays every month, to houses with many young women in them, and students, and literary men. documented In this timeline the company's first new piece, in the autumn of 1893, is Utena, the first all-woman staging by a standing company in a Tokyo theatre: Kumehachi, forty-six and famous for her men, plays Akio, and the pit fills with schoolgirls in black jackets over their kimono. choice Kidō, who saw Kumehachi play the mountain witch in 1890, wrote that she had been born forty years too early. documented In this timeline the Misaki-za Utena is revived almost every year while the company lasts. choice It is the bridge between the Edo women's troupes and the Takarazuka of 1914. inference

1891–1893The reaction

January 1891. At the First Higher Middle School the Christian teacher Uchimura Kanzō hesitates to bow low enough before the emperor's signature on the Rescript on Education, and is hounded from his post; a campaign against Christianity as disloyal follows, led by the philosopher Inoue Tetsujirō. documented In this timeline lesser pamphleteers of the campaign, never Inoue himself, cite Utena as an example of what Christian schools teach girls: to love each other and to despise the household. The mission schools on the Bluff, where the serial first found a following, lose pupils for the rest of the decade, as many real mission schools did. documented for the decline; choice for the pamphlets.

January 1891. Shimizu Toyoko, writing as Shikin, publishes The Broken Ring in the Jogaku zasshi: a woman who leaves a husband who keeps a mistress, pulls the stone out of the ring he gave her, and goes on wearing it broken as a reminder of what she will spend her life working to end. It is counted as the first feminist novel in Japanese. documented In this timeline every reader of 1891 sees the ring as the answer to Utena's: a ring not from a prince but from a husband, not a key to a castle but a broken thing kept on purpose. inference Critics here count it the first important work of a genre that will last for a century, fiction read in conversation with Utena, though Shikin never names the serial. choice

1891–1892. The Civil Code controversy. Hozumi Yatsuka's essay, "If the Civil Code comes out, loyalty and filial piety will perish", rallies the opponents of the French-inspired draft; in 1892 the Diet votes to postpone it. documented In this timeline the code's opponents use the serial's last chapter as an image of what the liberal draft would produce: daughters walking out of their brothers' houses. choice The postponement passes, as it did. documented

1893. The Ministry of Education instructs the girls' schools under its supervision to forbid "novels harmful to morals" in dormitories. choice (Part XIX, document 9, imitates the rules that follow.) In our history the ministry sent the girls' high schools a confidential instruction to tighten discipline over their pupils' daily lives in September 1902, and in June 1906 the minister, Makino Nobuaki, told every school to forbid, "inside or outside school", reading that preached "pessimism" or depicted "base passions", and complained of students who "agonise over fantasies". documented In this timeline the same steps come nine years earlier, with one book in view. The ban does what bans do. A book that girls had read openly by day, whatever they did after lights out, becomes one that they read only under the blankets, lend in wrapped covers and quote in code. inference

1890sThe rose and the bride

The girls' school friendship culture that our history calls "S", after "sister", took shape in the 1900s and 1910s: intense attachments between older and younger girls, exchanged letters and photographs, and a moral panic about them. documented (Part XXI) In this timeline it takes shape a decade earlier, in the 1890s, in the mission schools and the new public girls' schools, and it takes its forms from Utena. inference An older girl and a younger one are a "prince" and a "bride"; the relationship itself is "a rose" (薔薇), and to have one is to be "engaged"; a white rose left in a desk is a proposal; a cheap brass ring with a rose stamped on it is worn on a ribbon under the collar. choice In our history, as a girls' magazine reported in 1910, the proposal was a purple or crimson ribbon delivered by the older girl's friends, which the younger accepted by tying it in her hair. documented In this timeline rose became the code word for love between girls, and stayed one. One consequence lies far ahead: in our timeline "rose" (bara) became Japanese slang for gay men after a men's magazine took the name in 1971, and "lily" (yuri) became the word for love between women in answer to it. In this one the flowers are the other way round. documented for our history's terms; choice for the inversion.

Speech went the same way. The new schoolgirl slang of the 1890s, with its clipped -teyo and -dawa endings, appalled older writers in both timelines. documented In this one it also kept Utena's boku, which girls used among themselves and dropped at home. The magazine Shōjo no tomo would later be famous for its readers' "kimi and boku" slang; in this timeline that is simply how its readers' mothers talked. documented for the magazine's slang; choice for the rest.

1894–1899War, and the code

1894–95. The war with China. The stage turns to war plays, Kawakami Otojirō's troupe among them, and the serial's audience turns to the news. documented for the war plays. The only wartime Utena is a shinpa piece in which the prince's sword is a Japanese officer's; it closes in a week. choice

1896. Wakamatsu Shizuko dies of tuberculosis in February, five days after the Meiji Girls' School burns; Higuchi Ichiyō dies of the same disease in November, at twenty-four, having published Child's Play (Takekurabe), whose heroine Midori, a spirited girl at the centre of the neighbourhood children's quarrels, is destined to follow her sister into the licensed quarter. documented In this timeline critics have argued for a century over whether Midori is Ichiyō's answer to Utena: the girl who holds her own among the children, and whom the adult world is waiting to take. choice Nothing in Ichiyō's diaries settles it.

16 July 1898. The family and succession books of the Civil Code take effect: the household head controls the family's property and members' marriages and residence; the wife is legally incapable of most acts without her husband's consent; the household passes to the eldest son. documented (Part XXI) In this timeline the articles on wives' legal incapacity are known, within a year, as "the Rose Bride articles": the wife, like Anthy, can do nothing on her own account and belongs to whoever holds the household. choice The name does not change the law. It changes how the law is talked about for the next thirty-three years, until the articles go in 1931, and it is the reason they are the first thing reformers try to remove (Part IX). inference

1899. The Girls' High School Order requires every prefecture to have a secondary school for girls, and the education minister, Kabayama Sukenori, defines its aim as making "wise mothers and good wives"; Japan's first modern Copyright Act is passed and Japan joins the Berne Convention. documented Under the new act an anonymous or pseudonymous work is protected for thirty years, counted from the year after publication, or, for a work issued in parts, from the year after its last part. Works published before 1899 without registration under the old law, its drafter explained, had no rights to carry over. documented For Utena that means two texts with two fates. The newspaper text had never been registered and was anyone's to reprint once the old law's two years of the editor's control had passed, and pirated editions of chapters 1–33 had been on sale since the late 1880s. Shun'yōdō's book of Part Four (1887) and its collected edition (1888), registered by the publisher with the paper's serial editor named in the colophon as compiler, since the book laws wanted a responsible name there and the author would give none, now run on until the end of 1917 and 1918. choice Shun'yōdō and the Tokyo Asahi, as heir to the paper that had issued the first three parts, dispute the rights in 1900–02 and settle by sharing them until the term runs out (Part XII). choice

1900–1905The second generation

10 March 1900. The Public Peace Police Law keeps women out of political associations and political meetings, in its Article 5. documented The first organised campaign against Article 5, by socialist women, would begin in 1905. documented In this timeline it begins in 1900, with a petition circulated through the alumnae associations of girls' schools, whose members had, in many cases, first met in a "rose". choice It fails. inference

Summer 1900. In Paris, the actress Kawakami Sadayakko plays at Loïe Fuller's theatre at the Exposition Universelle, and plays a fragment of Utena among her pieces (Part VIII). choice

1901. Japan Women's University opens; Kishida Toshiko dies in May. documented Her obituaries in this timeline mention the authorship theory, which she never addressed in public. choice

1902. Shōjo kai, the first magazine for girls, appears, followed by Shōjo sekai (1906), Shōjo no tomo (1908) and Shōjo gahō (1912). documented In this timeline the editors who founded them knew exactly whom they were selling to: the daughters and younger sisters of the girls who had read Utena in 1885, now organised into a reading public by seventeen years of a book their schools had kept out of the dormitories since 1893. inference What the girls' magazines printed most, after poetry and readers' letters, was a genre with no equivalent in our timeline: the "Anthy story" (アンシー物), in which a girl leaves home with a trunk and a small animal and goes looking for someone she has lost. Hundreds were printed between 1902 and 1930, almost all by amateurs. choice They are where the Japanese girls' novel learned its first plot.

1903. Kosugi Tengai's Makaze koikaze in the Yomiuri: it opens with a schoolgirl's bicycle crash, and its hero, a viscount's adopted son engaged to her best friend, a girl who calls the heroine "elder sister", falls in love with her. documented In this timeline it is the first best-seller about schoolgirls that is not about Utena, and its success is read as proof that the genre had outgrown its source. inference

1904–1905. The war with Russia. Thousands of Chinese students come to Tokyo, among them a young woman from Shaoxing named Qiu Jin, who wears Western men's clothes, carries a Japanese sword, and goes home to plan a revolution (Part VIII). documented

1905–1912Toward the Bluestockings

1905–1909. The women of the socialist Heiminsha, among them Sakai Tameko and Nishikawa Fumiko, petition the Diet year after year to amend Article 5; Sekai fujin, founded in January 1907 by Fukuda Hideko, who as Kageyama Hideko had been arrested in the Osaka Incident, campaigns for it until July 1909, when the authorities crack down on its thirty-eighth number and the paper, already short of money, closes. In March 1908, by the usual account, the lower house passes an amendment based on Fukuda's petition and the House of Peers rejects it the next day. Nothing changes until 1922. documented In this timeline the petitions carry many more signatures, because the girls' school networks of the 1890s sign alongside the socialists, and a member of the Peers quotes Utena on the floor, against them. choice

1908. Kawakami Sadayakko and her husband open the Imperial Actresses' Training Institute in Tokyo, for girls of sixteen to twenty-five with two guarantors; Ichikawa Kumehachi, who had founded a smaller actresses' school with a dancer two years before, teaches there. When the new Imperial Theatre opens in March 1911, actresses from the institute, now its training school, appear in its first production, Mori Ritsuko among them. documented The institute's first public performance in this timeline is the ball scene from Utena, with women in both parts. choice

25 January 1911. Kanno Suga, journalist, socialist and the only woman among the twelve executed for the alleged plot against the emperor, is hanged. documented The government's case against the "High Treason" defendants reaches into what they read; in this timeline the prosecution's list of seized books includes a copy of Utena seized at her lodgings, its chapter 38 marked in pencil by a hand nobody has identified. choice No one has seen it since 1945.

September 1911. Hiratsuka Raichō and four other young women found Seitō, the "Bluestocking", whose first issue opens with Yosano Akiko's poem on "the day the mountains move" and then prints Raichō's manifesto: "In the beginning, woman was the sun." documented The same month, on 22–24 September, the Literary Society gives Ibsen's A Doll's House on the private stage in Tsubouchi Shōyō's garden, and in November at the Imperial Theatre, with Matsui Sumako as Nora, the wife who leaves her husband's house and slams the door. documented In our history Nora became the emblem of the "new woman" in Japan. In this one she arrives as a second Anthy, and a reviewer calls her "the Norwegian Anthy": a woman who leaves with no destination, twenty-four years after the first one did. choice The comparison is not flattering to Nora, who has a husband to slam the door on. Anthy had only a brother and a world.

1912. Otake Kazue, a painter's daughter of nineteen who joins Seitō that year under the boy's name Kōkichi and wears men's kimono, falls in love with Raichō, who calls her "my boy"; their affair, her glass of "five-coloured liquor" at a bar and a visit to a Yoshiwara brothel to see the women there make her the press's favourite "new woman", and she leaves the society in October. documented The papers in this timeline do not need to invent a nickname for Kōkichi. Every reader already has one. choice On 30 July the Meiji emperor dies. documented

Shadow play · Part VII

B-ko(A matron, with a lamp.) Inspection! Desk lids up! …And what is this?

A-koA white rose, Miss.

B-koFrom whom?

A-koMy prince, Miss.

B-koA prince! Is he rich?

A-koVery, Miss. Two hair ribbons and a bicycle.

B-koAnd you're engaged?

A-koRing on a ribbon, Miss. Under the collar.

B-koHow modest! A model wife in the making!

A-koDo you know? Do you know…

B-ko…how to read a rose?

(The matron sweeps on down the row. In every desk, a white rose.)
