XVIII

Sources and method

How the real history was checked, what could not be, and where to read further

Every statement tagged documented was checked against a source, nearly always one read in full during the research for this document. The sources below are the ones the argument leans on most; the working notes behind them cite nearly fifteen hundred distinct web pages and scans.

How the research was done

  • Fifteen research reports were compiled before and during the writing: on the Meiji press and serial fiction; press and book censorship and the laws on dress, swords and duels; the Rokumeikan and the schools; Meiji technology; women and the law; theatre, the women's stage and fandom; Meiji politics, ideology and the economy; the twentieth century from Seitō to 2026; the international reception routes; the vocabulary of 1885; and five independent checks, on court names and Meiji vocabulary, technology, education and dress, law and copyright, and culture and the arts. Each fact in them names the page it was read from. The finished text was then read by independent reviewers for errors about the show, the Meiji period, the modern period, its own consistency and the rules it sets itself.
  • Kinds of source. Japanese reference works as reproduced on Kotobank (Nihon Kokugo Daijiten, Nipponica, Sekai Daihyakka, the Yamakawa and Ōbunsha historical dictionaries); Japanese Wikipedia read as source text, used for dates and cross-checked where it matters; the National Diet Library's catalogue, reference database and exhibitions; statute texts from the Nagoya University historical statute database, Japanese Wikisource and e-Gov; the Ministry of Education's centennial history of the school system; the statistical yearbooks digitised by Hitotsubashi University; university repository papers; and memoirs on Aozora Bunko, above all the critic Okamoto Kidō's recollections of the Meiji stage.
  • The show was checked against a first-hand reference archive of Revolutionary Girl Utena (all 39 episodes watched shot by shot), which keeps what is on screen apart from what its makers said and what critics have argued. The makers' statements quoted in Part XI are attributed as such.
  • Limits. The research's search budget ran out before every question was settled, and some sites could not be reached. The points that stay open are listed below rather than smoothed over.

Still open

  • Whether any prefecture kept a ban on cross-dressing in its police rules after the national Penal Code dropped it in 1882.
  • Which rule barred women from the galleries of the lower house in 1890. The ban, and its lifting on 3 December after women wrote to the parties, are documented; the instrument itself was not found.
  • Whether a book whose author would not give a name could be registered at all, as Part VII assumes. The book ordinance of 1875 punished anonymous and pen-named books; its replacement of December 1887 let the publisher file for an author who could not be known (Art. 5). Whether naming a compiler in the colophon, as Part VII has Shun'yōdō do, would have satisfied the 1875 ordinance, under which Part Four appeared in April 1887, was not established.
  • The circulation of Jiyū no Tomoshibi in numbers: the dictionaries say only that it was for a time the largest in Tokyo.
  • The vote by which the House of Peers rejected the women's civic-rights bill in 1931; a figure of 184 to 62 circulates but could not be traced to a primary source.
  • A single traced instance of 革命 printed as ××. The reference works name it among the words publishers blanked from the 1920s to the mid-1930s, but no particular page was found.
  • Whether Judith Gautier's 1885 Poèmes de la libellule were made from literal versions by Saionji Kinmochi, as is often said.
