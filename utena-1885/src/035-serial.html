III

The serial

What appeared, where, when, in what form, and under what name

Otome Kakumei Utena ran in a Liberal party paper that changed its name while the serial was in it, was suspended by the police two chapters before the end of its third part and given up when that part ended, and was finished as a book. The newspaper it began in became, in 1888, the Tokyo Asahi.

The paper

Jiyū no Tomoshibi, "The Lamp of Liberty", was founded on 11 May 1884 by Hoshi Tōru as the popular organ of the Liberal Party, the Jiyūtō: a small newspaper, in the small papers' style, meant to carry the party's ideas to readers who would never open its serious daily. documented Five months later the party dissolved itself, on 29 October 1884, under police pressure and the shame of the risings its radicals had led. documented The paper survived it. On 14 January 1886 it renamed itself Tomoshibi Shinbun, on 1 April 1887 Mezamashi Shinbun ("Wake Up!"), and on 10 July 1888, bought by the Osaka publisher of the Asahi, it reappeared as the Tokyo Asahi Shinbun, the paper that would later employ Natsume Sōseki and, in 1940, merge with its Osaka parent into the national Asahi. documented

It was not a struggling paper. For a time in 1884–86 it had the largest sale in Tokyo, and part of the reason was fiction. documented From December 1884 it had been serialising Miyazaki Muryū's Kishūshū (鬼啾啾, "Ghosts Wailing"), a history of the Russian nihilists whose heroines were Vera Zasulich, who shot the governor of St Petersburg, and Sophia Perovskaya, hanged for the assassination of Alexander II, who in Muryū's telling gives up the man she loves for "ten million compatriots". The serial ended on 3 April 1885; its book edition was banned in October and Muryū was convicted. documented Political fiction had been allegorising Japan's politics as a woman courted by rival suitors since 1880, when the first political novel cast the people's rights as a geisha named O-Ken ("Rights") wooed by "People" and "Government". documented The paper's readers were therefore primed for exactly what arrived in its mailbox in February 1885: a story with "revolution" in its title, a girl who fights in a man's coat, and a bride whom rival suitors win by force. inference It also needed a successor to Kishūshū. inference

The first installment

It appeared on Thursday 2 April 1885, the day before the last installment of Kishūshū, under a two-line heading beside the day's woodblock cut. choice The opening is the anime's fairy-tale prologue, told in the voice of an Edo storyteller, with the narrator's doubt at the end turned into the kind of aside a gesaku author made to the reader. The shape of the serial

The newspaper printed about 210 installments in a year and a half, and the unserialised Part Four was the length of about forty more. A chapter took between three installments (the "story so far" chapters) and nine (chapter 39), usually five to seven, so a chapter took about a week of reading, much like a weekly episode. Each installment had one cut. Each chapter ended with a notice naming the next chapter's heading and the same unexplained tag, 絶対運命黙示録. choice for the counts.

The pictures

The cuts were signed 芳年: Tsukioka Yoshitoshi, the greatest print artist of the day, who had begun a series called One Hundred Aspects of the Moon that very year and who had got into trouble in 1884 with another Liberal paper for supplying pictures to Jiyū no Tomoshibi. documented for Yoshitoshi's 1884 work for the paper and his newspaper illustration in general; choice that he drew this serial. The plates of the 1887 book were by his pupil Mizuno Toshikata. choice Each newspaper cut was a small woodblock, cut by a professional carver from the artist's drawing and printed with the text: line only, no tone, no colour. documented for the method. Within those limits the illustrator invented most of what later generations thought of as the look of Utena, because the text describes very little: Utena's long hair loose under a boy's cap, which she never wears in the anime and always carries in the cuts; Anthy's spectacles as two blank white circles; the duelling garden as a flat ellipse seen from an angle, with the upside-down castle cut off by the top of the frame; the silhouetted girls of the shadow plays against a wall of rose lattice. The single-sheet colour prints that publishers made from the most popular scenes added the colours readers argued about for decades: a pink that 1880s aniline dyes made almost violet for Utena's hair, a deep red-brown for Anthy's skin, black for Akio's carriage. choice

The language

The narrative is in the mixed literary style of 1885, Edo storyteller's rhythms over a frame of Sino-Japanese, with a narrator who steps forward now and then to address the reader. The dialogue is in the spoken Tokyo Japanese of its speakers, which is how the small papers already wrote dialogue and how the serial gets most of its effects. documented for the convention. Each character's speech places them in 1885 society as exactly as their clothes:

  • Utena says 僕 (boku) for "I" and 君 (kimi) for "you", the pronouns of male students. In the anime she uses the same boku; in 1885, from a girl, it is a social act, and the first thing readers imitated. inference
  • Anthy speaks the soft, deferential language of a maid in a great house, calls Utena ウテナ様, and changes the form of address with each new owner, exactly as in the anime. To readers who had been in service, that was the most frightening thing in the book.
  • Nanami and the Kiryū household speak the new Yamanote dialect of rich Tokyo families, with its -asobase and its onii-sama; Saionji the clipped orders of a swordsman; Akio the easy, self-mocking courtesy of a man educated abroad.
  • The narrator calls himself, or herself, "the author" (作者) and never says which.

The readings beside the words

Small papers printed the reading of every character, and the serial uses the space beside the text to play. Its most famous glosses became part of the language:

少女 → をとめ
The title's "girl", read otome, the older word for a maiden (Part XV).
決闘者 → デユエリスト
"Duellist", glossed with the English word in katakana, as political novels glossed liberty. The duel itself was news: 決闘 in the sense of a formal, pre-arranged fight was first debated in Japanese in 1881, glossed with the native word for a samurai's settled combat. documented
世界の果て
"End of the World", left unglossed throughout, so that readers could not be sure even how to say it. choice It is the serial's central mystery, and Part V lists the guesses.
牛鈴 → カウベル
"Cowbell", in chapter 16: a cow's bell, glossed as an English brand-name might be.
鳳学園 → おほとりがくゑん
"Ōtori Academy". Schools of 1885 were 学校, 女学校, 塾 or, for the mission schools, 学院; the compound 学園, "school-garden", has no dictionary example in our history before 1906. documented In this timeline the serial coins it, for a school that is mostly a garden, and it is the word every private school founded after 1890 wants in its name. choice
総代会 → そうだいくわい
"The Council". There were no student councils in Japanese schools before the Occupation, and the word 生徒会 is a post-war one. documented The serial uses 総代, the word for the pupil chosen to speak for a class at a ceremony, and makes a council of them. choice The nearest real thing was at the Meiji Girls' School, founded that year, whose pupils came to sit on its governing council and to run their own dormitory. documented The chairman of the board is the 校主, the proprietor, as private schools then called their owners, and Akio is his 代理; "chairman of the board of directors" would not be Japanese for another twelve years. documented for the dates of the words.
王子 → わうじ
"Prince". Meiji Japanese had no settled word for the prince of a fairy tale: a crown prince was 皇太子, an emperor's grandson 皇孫, and 王子 was the son of a foreign king. When Tsubouchi Shōyō retold Cinderella for a school reader in 1900, her prince became a 若殿, a young lord. documented To a Tokyo reader Ōji was also a village north of the city, with a famous fox shrine and the paper mill whose freight hoist, installed in 1875, was Japan's first lift. documented The serial's prince is therefore foreign by definition, and a little bit of a fox.
男装 → だんさう
"Men's dress", of a woman, in chapter 1. The dictionary's earliest example of the word is from a novel of 1889. documented Here it is four years older, and it is the serial's word for Utena's whole manner, not only her clothes. choice
永遠 → えいゑん
"Eternity". A new word: the philosophers' dictionary of 1881 had fixed it as the translation of the English eternity. Ordinary people said 永久 or とこしへ. documented The castle that holds "eternity" therefore sounds, in 1885, like something out of a lecture on Western philosophy, which is exactly the effect. inference
魔女 → ウヰツチ
"Witch", in chapter 34. Read in the ordinary way, the characters meant manyo, a she-demon of Buddhist scripture; the European witch did not yet have a Japanese word. documented The serial glosses it with the English word, so that the Rose Bride is accused in a foreign language. choice
彼の女 → かのをんな
"She", in the heading of chapter 31, in the older spelling; the newer 彼女, read かのぢよ, was just entering print.

The book editions

The paper reissued each part as soon as it finished, in three illustrated paper-covered volumes per part with colour-printed covers, in the format the old Edo picture-serials had used and that Meiji publishers still used for newspaper fiction. documented for the practice. Part Four appeared first as a typeset book in April 1887, published by Shun'yōdō, the house founded in 1878 that would later publish most of Ozaki Kōyō and Natsume Sōseki, and that had reissued a Liberal newspaper serial, Sakazaki Shiran's life of Sakamoto Ryōma, as a book in 1885 (documented for the firm; choice that it published this book), and all thirty-nine chapters were collected in four volumes in 1888. The textual history of those editions, which is more interesting than it sounds, is in Part XV.
