XIX

Documents of the rose

Fifteen period documents from this timeline, in English translation

Every document in this part is invented, and so is every person who signs one. The forms, places, institutions and dates around them are real, and each note says which real practice the document imitates. choice throughout.

Document 1 · 社告 · notice to readers

Jiyū no Tomoshibi, 1 April 1885

From tomorrow this paper begins a new serial story, Otome Kakumei Utena, by a contributor who has asked that his name, or hers, be left unasked. It concerns a school on a hill by the sea, a girl who wears a young man's coat, and a betrothal decided by the sword. Our readers know that this paper does not print idle tales. We print this one because it is not idle. Those who read it to their households may judge for themselves what it is about. Daily, with a picture.

Imitates the front-page notices with which small newspapers announced new serials. Unsigned; this timeline's historians attribute it to the paper's serial editor (Parts VI and XVII), who is invented. The paper's coy "his, or hers" is the first published hint that the author might be a woman.

Document 2 · 寄書 · readers' letters

Jiyū no Tomoshibi, 19 June 1885

To the editor. I am sixteen and attend a school on the Bluff, which I shall not name, where we read your paper in secret after lights out, one candle between six. We have noticed something your printers have not. Your author's name, read as you print it, is Hōsūshi. Read as a girl's name, 鳳雛子 is Ōtori Hinako, which is to say a daughter of the very academy in the story. We do not ask you to tell us who your author is. We only ask whether you knew. — A schoolgirl of Yokohama.

Imitates the readers' columns of the 1880s small papers, which printed letters, often pseudonymous, from students and, increasingly, from women. The paper did not answer.

Document 3 · 寄書 · readers' letters

Jiyū no Tomoshibi, 26 August 1885

Sir. My daughter is fourteen. Until the spring she called herself watakushi like a decent girl. Now she says boku and calls her brother kimi, and when her mother rebuked her she replied that there is no rule against it. I traced the phrase to your story. I do not ask you to stop printing it; my wife and her sister would not forgive me. I ask you to print, beneath the first installment when your story resumes, a line reminding girls that a novel is not a school. — A shopkeeper of Kanda.

Imitates complaints about schoolgirls adopting male students' speech (boku, kimi) and the new schoolgirl dialect, which were real and frequent in the press of the 1880s and 1890s.

Document 4 · handbill

A women's company at a Yokohama theatre, November 1885

Grand new piece in four acts, from the story everyone is reading: The Rose Bride of the Bluff. Iwai Tamaji's company of actresses, all parts played by women. Miss Tamaji as Tenjō Utena, in the coat; Miss Kogiku as the Bride. The duel in the forest garden with real swords and lanterns. The ball, with a Western orchestra. Doors at noon. Admission 6 sen, boxes extra. Ladies' gallery.

Imitates playbills of onna-shibai, the women's theatre companies that played in small houses in Meiji Tokyo and Yokohama. The company and actresses are invented.

Document 5 · 月曜続物評 · a critic's column

A small newspaper in Kyōbashi, Monday 8 February 1886

Serials of the week, by Suimutei. For ten months this column has told its readers that the serial in our neighbour's paper is a gesaku in a schoolgirl's hand, that its lift is better drawn than its people, and that its author should be sent to a girls' school to be cured. Yesterday our neighbour finished nine installments about a hall that burned. They do not tell us who lit the fire. They tell us that a hundred boys died for a man's idea of eternity; that the man stood outside and called their deaths necessary, as the coal is necessary to the engine; and that a woman who could not bear to watch roses die kept them in sugar. I have met that man. So have my readers; some of them work for him. I do not know who the author is. I know that he, or she, or they, have been to the funerals I have been to. Next Monday I shall return to disliking the comic chapters, which I shall go on reading. I withdraw everything. Except about the lift.

Imitates the columns in which Meiji papers and magazines reviewed fiction, including other papers' serials. The columnist, his pen name and his paper are invented. His columns were collected in 1888 as Notes on the Rose (Part XIII).

Document 6 · official notice

Home Ministry, August 1886

Under the Newspaper Ordinance, publication of the newspaper Tomoshibi Shinbun is suspended from this day until further notice, the said paper having in its issue of the 21st instant printed matter injurious to public morals in the story entitled Otome Kakumei Utena, and having been warned. The issue of the 21st is to be seized wherever found.

Imitates the terse suspension orders published in the official gazette under the press laws of the 1880s.

Document 7 · the blank frame

Tomoshibi Shinbun, October 1886

[A ruled rectangle the size of the daily illustration, empty but for four characters:] 此図差控. [Below the installment:] To our readers. The picture for today is withheld. The story will be concluded in book form and will not continue in these pages. We thank the readers who have followed it for a year and a half, and especially those who wrote. The serial editor.

Imitates the practice of printing an empty frame or a line of apology where an illustration or passage had been withdrawn. Signed by the paper's serial editor, an invented person.

Document 8 · price list

A Western-plant nursery, Yamate, Yokohama, spring 1887

Roses, grown on our own grounds from French and English stock. Utena (white, scented, flowers repeatedly): one plant, 3 yen. Bride (deep red-violet, few thorns): 15 yen. Duellist (striped red and white): 2 yen 50 sen. Black Rose (deepest crimson, very rare; we have four plants): enquire. Orders by post. We regret that we cannot supply the green rose of the story, which we have not yet been able to import.

Imitates the price lists of the Yokohama nurseries that sold Western plants to foreigners and to Japanese collectors. In 1885 a day labourer earned about 16 sen a day and a maidservant 75 sen a month, so "enquire" meant a great deal (Part XII).

Document 9 · from a dormitory rulebook

A girls' school in Tokyo, 1893

Article 11. Pupils shall not keep, lend or read in the dormitory the novel called Otome Kakumei, or any picture, verse, ring or flower relating to it. Article 12. Pupils shall not address one another as "Bride" or "Prince", nor exchange rings. Article 13. The forms of speech proper to young men shall not be used.

Imitates the dormitory regulations of Meiji girls' schools, which did ban particular reading and policed speech and friendships. The school is invented; Part VII discusses how common such rules became in this timeline.

Document 10 · review

A Paris weekly, 14 July 1900

Of the pieces Madame Sada Yacco plays at the Loïe Fuller theatre, the strangest is the fragment the programme calls La Fiancée des roses. A girl in a cadet's jacket fights a duel against a young lord over another girl, who stands between them in a crown and says nothing at all. The audience, which had come to watch a geisha die, did not know whether to laugh. Then the girl in the jacket won, held out her hand to the silent one, and the house rose. I have since learned that the original is a Japanese novel of 1885 called, if my informant is right, The Revolution of a Young Girl. Somebody should translate it.

Imitates Paris reviews of Kawakami Sadayakko's 1900 performances at Loïe Fuller's theatre at the Exposition Universelle, which drew writers and artists. The Utena fragment is this timeline's addition to her real repertoire.

Document 11 · letter

From a Chinese student in Tokyo to her sister in Shaoxing, 1906

You asked what the Japanese girls read. They read a book about a girl who becomes a prince in order to save another girl, and who fails, and they love her for failing. I have read it twice. Our teacher says it is a frivolous book. Then she lends it to us. When you come next spring bring your boots, because here we walk.

Imitates the letters of the Chinese women students who came to Tokyo in the 1900s, among them Qiu Jin. The writer is invented; Part VIII discusses how this timeline's critics read Qiu Jin's own verse-novel beside it.

Document 12 · 紙芝居 · a patter sheet

A street kamishibai man's notes for The Rose Knight, Tokyo, 1934

[In pencil, on the back of the last picture of the set.] Clappers. Sweets first, one sen, no sweets no story. Picture 1: last time! The princess in the cage on top of the clock tower, the Demon King's carriage coming up the road with no horses and no driver. Picture 2: who is that on the roof? Black jacket, red trousers. The ROSE KNIGHT. Make them shout it. Picture 3: the Demon King has no face, say so twice. Picture 4: he says, A girl cannot be a prince. Wait for the girls at the front to shout. Picture 5: the sword out of the rose. Slow. Picture 6: the clock strikes. Picture 7: the cage opens, the princess takes her hand, the floor gives way. Picture 8: falling, falling. Is the Rose Knight finished? Is the princess lost? Come back tomorrow. (The Asakusa man does the falling as a dream. Don't.)

Imitates the street kamishibai of the early 1930s, whose tellers sold sweets to their audiences and told serials such as Ōgon Bat from memory, each in his own way, with hand-painted pictures hired from a lender; most sets made before the war burned in 1945. The teller and his notes are invented.

Document 13 · censor's note

Home Ministry, Police Bureau, Book Section, 1937

Application to reprint: Shōjo Kakumei Utena (Meiji 21 text). Permitted on condition that the word "revolution" be removed throughout, including the title, without blanks or marks of omission; that the closing line of the so-called creed be deleted; that the illustration to chapter 34 not show the crowd in Western clothes as Japanese. Note for file: this is the eleventh application for this title since 1933. The book is not dangerous for what it says but for how many people know it.

Imitates the internal notes of pre-war censorship. Publishers had long blanked words such as "socialism" themselves to avoid bans, and from 1936–37 the Home Ministry suppressed the blanks too, because readers could guess what they hid; the order on this title is this timeline's.

Document 14 · examiner's sheet

Civil Censorship Detachment, Press, Pictorial and Broadcast Division, Tokyo, 1946

Title: Shōjo Kakumei Utena (restored text). Subject: novel, Meiji period, sword combat throughout. Recommendation: approve. Examiner's remarks: Sword fighting here is not feudal; the swords are drawn out of people's chests and the hero is a schoolgirl fighting the head of a household. The censored words restored in the title are "girl" and "revolution", which we are told were cut by the Japanese authorities in 1940 and 1937. Suggest no action. Suggest, in fact, that someone in G.S. read it.

Imitates the examiners' sheets of the Occupation's censorship, which did suppress "feudal" sword-fighting films and plays in 1945–49. G.S. is Government Section, which drafted the 1946 constitution.

Document 15 · サークルちらし · a fan circle's flyer

Comic Market, Tokyo, December 1978

Circle Bara no Niwa (Rose Garden), table 41. NEW: Chapter Forty, what Anthy found on the other side of the gate. Our longest issue ever, B5, 48 pages, printed cover, 300 yen. Reprinted by popular demand: The Exchange Notebook, Saionji's letters to Anthy, complete and annotated, 150 yen. Coming next summer: Nanami Lays Another Egg. We do not do Akio. Please stop asking. Members wanted, especially anyone who can draw a carriage. To the Tōga circle at table 40: we have your stapler.

Imitates the photocopied flyers that circles handed out at the Comic Market, the fan-run fair for self-published comics held in Tokyo since December 1975, where amateur parodies of famous works were sold from the first. The circle and its magazines are invented.
