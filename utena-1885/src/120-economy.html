XII

The money

A newspaper saved, a flower bubble, a schoolgirl market, a railway's theatre, and a classic nobody owns

Novels change economies in small, specific ways: they sell newspapers, create fashions, move the prices of particular things, and give businesses a story to sell. Utena did all of these in this timeline, and none of them made its author rich. This Part follows the money, using documented prices and figures as the yardstick for the invented ones.

The paper the serial saved

Jiyū no Tomoshibi was, by the dictionaries' account, for a time the best-selling paper in Tokyo in 1884–86; the Osaka Asahi, the country's commercial model, sold about 32,000 copies a day in 1885. documented In this timeline the Tokyo paper sells about 12,000 copies a day in March 1885, 18,000 when Part One ends in August, and about 30,000 at its peak in the summer of 1886, before the suspension. choice At a sen a copy, or a little less by subscription, the difference between 12,000 and 30,000 copies is some forty to fifty thousand yen a year in sales before costs, when the deposit the press law demanded of a Tokyo paper from 1887 was a thousand. inference That is the measure of the serial's first economic effect: it kept alive, for three years longer than its politics alone would have, a Liberal paper that the deflation and the government were both trying to kill. When the Osaka Asahi's owners bought it in 1888 to make their Tokyo edition, as they did in our history, they bought a subscription list built by a novel. documented for the purchase; inference for the rest.

The serial had a second economy that the paper never saw. Lending libraries cut serials out of newspapers, pasted them into scrapbooks and rented them round the student boarding houses at three sen a time. documented In this timeline the Utena scrapbooks are the most rented item in Tokyo's lending libraries for three years, and the libraries' wear-worn copies of chapter 31, with the passage the police blanked written back in by hand, are what the 1886 suspension was really fighting. choice

The author was paid, by post, through a box at the Yokohama post office, at about thirty yen a month while the serial ran, which is roughly what a newspaper's staff novelist could expect. Payments stopped with the suspension in August 1886, and the author never claimed the fees for the book editions, which Shun'yōdō banked against the day someone did. Nobody ever has. choice

The rose bubble, 1886–1888

The rose was, in the 1880s, a Western curiosity with a Chinese name. Japanese gardeners had grown wild roses and a few Chinese ones for centuries, but the rose became a loved flower in Japan only after the Restoration, and a popular one only in the Taishō and Shōwa periods; until the 1900s it was not a symbol of romantic love in Japanese poetry. documented In this timeline it becomes one in the spring of 1886, all at once.

  • The boom. White roses sold as "Utena" go for a yen a plant, six days' pay for a labourer, and a deep red-violet sold as "the Bride" for five, then fifteen. Nurseries in Yokohama and in the old nursery villages of Somei and Komagome, where Edo's gardeners had bred the Somei-Yoshino cherry, import French and English stock by the crate. documented for the Somei nurseries; choice for the rest.
  • The fraud. By 1887 "black roses" for chapter 14 are selling at thirty yen. They are crimson roses grown in shade and treated with ink. choice
  • The crash. In the spring of 1888 a Komagome dealer who cannot pay for forty crates of French roses kills himself, the papers expose the black roses, and prices fall by nine-tenths in a month. choice It is a small crash, as the omoto boom of the 1870s had been: a few dealers ruined, a few collectors embarrassed. inference
  • What it left. A dozen nurseries that know how to graft, grow and ship Western roses; a public that wants them; and a flower that means something. In our history the Yokohama nurseries built an export trade in Japanese lily bulbs from the late nineteenth century. documented In this timeline they also build a domestic trade in roses a generation before ours did, and by 1900 a rose bed is as standard in a girls' school garden as the chrysanthemum is in a temple's. choice

The schoolgirl market

The girls who read the serial were the first Japanese consumers to be sold to as a group. inference In our history that market took shape with the girls' magazines of the 1900s, which sold to the new middle class's daughters at its own schools. documented In this timeline it starts in 1885, with three products:

  • Jackets. The tailors of Kanda and of Yokohama's Benten-dōri sell black serge "Utena jackets" cut for girls, to be worn over kimono and hakama. By 1890 the jacket is the unofficial uniform of the Tokyo mission schools, and when the public girls' high schools set uniforms after 1899 several adopt a version of it. choice
  • Rings. Brass rings with a rose stamped on the bezel, made in Tokyo's metalworking quarters and sold for five sen at stalls near the schools, worn on a ribbon under the collar. The authorities call them a fad; the fad lasts sixty years. choice
  • Pictures. Single-sheet colour prints of scenes from the serial, then, as the print trade gave way to photography, photographs of the actresses who played Utena, and from October 1900, when private picture postcards were permitted, postcards. documented for the shift from prints to small photographs and for the postcard boom; choice for the subjects. The gidayū girl Takemoto Ayanosuke had sold hundreds of photographs a day in the 1880s and 1890s; in 1921 a single Shōchiku film actress's picture sold four thousand a day in Asakusa. documented Whoever played Utena at the Misaki-za in the 1890s sold more than Ayanosuke. choice

The stage and the railway

Most stage versions of Utena paid nothing to anyone but their casts: an author who would not give a name could hardly sue. inference The theatres made money; the money that mattered was made by a railway.

Kobayashi Ichizō's girls' opera of 1914 was a device for filling the trains of his Minoo-Arima Electric Railway on the way to his hot-spring resort; the audience saw the show free with their bath, about 1,100 of them a day in the first season, and the girls, most with only an elementary education, were paid as much as university graduates. documented In this timeline the Takarazuka Utena of 1919 is the company's first full-house run of a new piece, and the railway's Sunday traffic to Takarazuka doubles in the year it opens. choice Over the century the revue's Utena plays to more people than any other piece in its repertoire, more than the Rose of Versailles, which in our history reached five million spectators in 2014. documented for the Rose of Versailles; choice for Utena.

A classic nobody owns

Japan's first modern Copyright Act, of 1899, protected anonymous works for a fixed term from publication, after which they belonged to everyone. documented (to confirm term) In this timeline Utena, whose author never came forward, enters the public domain in 1917. choice The economic consequence is the opposite of what the book's publishers feared. A public-domain classic is a free raw material, and everybody uses it: eleven editions in two years, the cheapest of them ten-sen booklets at railway kiosks; films from 1918; the Takarazuka operetta; the one-yen sets of 1926–27, in which Kaizōsha's first set alone drew 230,000 subscribers at a yen a volume, about two per cent of a graduate's first month's pay. documented for Kaizōsha; choice for the rest. Every company that adapts the book adds to the audience for every other adaptation, and none of them owes the author a sen.

After the war the textbook editions make it one of the steadiest sellers in Japanese publishing (Part X), and the 1997 television series, which in our history earned its makers royalties on an original work, in this timeline earns them royalties on an adaptation of a free one, which is the business model of every adapter of Dickens and Austen. inference
