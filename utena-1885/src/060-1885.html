VI

Chronicle, 1885–1890

The serial years, and the six years in which Japan wrote down what a woman was

For five years Utena was news. It ran, it was stopped, it was staged, it was argued about in the letters columns and the lecture halls, and it gave a generation of girls a way of speaking, dressing and pairing off that their elders could see and could not stop. Its first effect on the law was the opposite of what its readers hoped.

Each year below threads the serial through documented events. Entries marked documented happened on those dates in our history; the threads are marked inference or choice.

1885The Council Book

Winter. The Matsukata deflation is at its worst; rice is cheap, farmers are selling land to pay taxes, and the Chichibu rebels of the autumn before are being tried. The Liberal Party has been dissolved since October. documented In February the first three chapters of a serial arrive at Jiyū no Tomoshibi by post. choice

2 April. Chapter 1 appears. It takes over the space left by Miyazaki Muryū's nihilist serial, which ends the next day, and for a month it is one entertainment among several in a paper that already has one of the largest sales in Tokyo. documented for Kishūshū and the paper's standing; choice for the rest. The first sign of something else comes from Yokohama, where the paper is sold at the station and in the foreign settlement's Japanese shops: the agent there orders more copies every week from mid-April, and by May the paper's Yokohama sale has trebled. The mission schools on the Bluff are reading it. choice

18 April. Itō Hirobumi and Li Hongzhang sign the Tianjin Convention, withdrawing both countries' troops from Korea. documented

May. Chapter 7, Juri's chapter, ends with the photograph in the locket. The letters column, which normally prints three or four letters a week, prints eleven, most of them from women, and the paper runs a second column of letters for the first time in its history. choice Some are outraged; more are grateful; one, signed "a woman of Kōjimachi, aged forty", says only that she had a friend like Shiori once and had not thought of her for twenty years. choice This is the moment when the serial stops being a political novel in disguise and becomes the thing its readers will remember: the first story in modern Japanese print in which a girl's love for a girl is the point of a chapter, stated in a picture and left without comment. inference

4–12 June. Fukuzawa Yukichi's editorials "On Japanese Women" run in his Jiji shinpō, with a continuation in July: Japanese men should imagine their positions and their wives' reversed, and a society that expects women to bear what men could not is not civilised. documented Tokyo's most influential liberal and its most popular serial are arguing about the same thing in the same weeks, and readers notice. Fukuzawa never mentions the serial; its letters column mentions him often. choice

June. The serial pauses for two weeks while the paper waits for manuscript. On 19 June a schoolgirl's letter points out that the byline can be read as a girl's name (Part XVI, document 2). choice

Summer. Girls on the Bluff begin to wear black boys' jackets over their kimono and hakama on the walk to school. In Tokyo the first are seen in Kanda, near the private schools, by August. choice Girls in men's clothes had been a scandal once already: in 1872 the government's first girls' school had put its pupils in samurai men's hakama, and the same spring Tokyo forbade women to crop their hair, on the ground that it blurred the line between men and women. documented The jackets reopened the question in a new form. Shops near the schools start selling "ウテナ形", Utena-style jackets cut for girls, and a Yokohama ring-maker sells cheap brass rings stamped with a rose for 5 sen, a day's pocket money. choice The girls' new habit of saying boku draws the letters that document 3 imitates. choice

20 July. Jogaku zasshi, the "Magazine of Women's Learning", begins publication in Tokyo. documented Its Christian editors will spend the next five years arguing with Utena without ever naming it in print. choice

21 August. Part One ends with chapter 13, the "story so far". The paper announces a second part for the autumn and the first three volumes of the book edition. choice By then its daily sale is half as large again as it was in March, and the paper is the best-selling in Tokyo by a distance. choice (Part XII gives the numbers.)

September. Tsubouchi Shōyō begins publishing The Essence of the Novel, in nine parts that will run to April 1886. documented

13 October. Part Two begins, and with it Akio. The same month Tōkai Sanshi's Chance Encounters with Beautiful Women appears. documented for Kajin no kigū; choice for the serial's date. For the rest of the decade students will argue about which of the two heroines, the Irish revolutionary Kōren or Utena Tenjō, a young man should prefer, and the girls of the Bluff will settle it for themselves. choice

November. A women's theatre company plays The Rose Bride of the Bluff at a small Yokohama house (document 4). It is the first of hundreds of unauthorised stage versions, and the first time a Japanese audience sees a woman play a girl who plays a prince. choice On 23 November police in Osaka and Nagasaki arrest dozens of former Liberals for a plot to send explosives to Korea and start a revolution there; among them is a twenty-year-old former schoolmistress, Kageyama Hideko. documented The government-friendly papers call her a Japanese Joan of Arc; others, which have read their rivals' serial, call her "the Utena of Osaka". choice The Home Ministry suspends Jiyū no Tomoshibi for ten days in December, nominally for its reporting of the arrests. choice The serial misses a week.

22 December. The cabinet system replaces the old council of state; Itō Hirobumi becomes Japan's first prime minister and Mori Arinori its minister of education. documented

1886The Black Rose and the Carriage

14 January. Jiyū no Tomoshibi becomes Tomoshibi Shinbun. documented The serial's chapter 21 runs in the last issue under the old name and chapter 21's second installment in the first under the new; readers say the paper changed its name and the serial did not notice. choice

February. Part Two ends with Nemuro Memorial Hall. The fire, the hundred boys and the professor who calls their deaths a necessary sacrifice arrive in a city where most adults remember the boys of Aizu on Iimori Hill and many families had sons at Saigō's academies in 1877. documented for both. Letters come from Aizu and from Kagoshima, each claiming the hundred boys for its own dead. The serial never says. choice

March–April. Mori's school ordinances reorganise Japanese education from the Imperial University down to the elementary schools, and make normal schools into barracks of discipline. documented The model of schooling the serial mocks (a school that trains its pupils to be pieces in a game run by an absent authority) becomes official policy the same spring. Mori himself, who had once published an argument for monogamy and married by written contract, is the cabinet's leading advocate of women's education, and the oligarchs' least predictable reader. documented for Mori's views; choice for his reading. In this timeline he reads the serial, and while he lives his ministry, alone among the government's, never moves against it. choice

Spring. The rose fever begins. Western roses had been grown in Yokohama's foreign gardens since the 1860s and were still a fenced curiosity in Tokyo, where crowds queued at the government's experimental farm to smell a new French rose. Japan already knew what a flower could do to its owners: around 1877 a boom in the potted omoto had sent single plants in Kyoto to a thousand yen. documented In the spring of 1886 the rose becomes a speculation in its turn. White roses sold as "Utena" change hands for a yen a plant; a dark red-violet sold as the "Bride" goes for five, then fifteen; nurseries in Yokohama and Komagome import French stock by the crate. choice Part XII follows the bubble to its crash.

11 May. Part Three begins, the Carriage Book. Within a month the press attaches Akio's name to every young man with a private carriage, and a fashion for driving oneself at night spreads among the sons of the rich, whom the police of Tokyo had never had to regulate before. choice

14–16 June. More than a hundred women of the Amemiya silk mill in Kōfu walk out against new guild rules that lengthen their day from fourteen hours to fourteen and a half and forbid them to move to another mill, shut themselves in a temple, and win a relaxation of the rules in two days: the first factory strike in Japan. documented Nothing connects them with the serial. Most of the strikers could not read, the paper did not circulate in Kōfu, and the story's academy is the opposite of their dormitories. inference Their strike is a reminder of how narrow the serial's world was: it was read in schools, shops and houses with a newspaper subscription, not in the mills, which employed more women than all the schools in Japan.

Summer. Cholera kills more than a hundred thousand people across Japan. documented The serial's readers, like everyone else, are frightened; the letters column shrinks.

21–22 August. Chapter 31 ends with Nanami behind the sofa. The Home Ministry orders the issue seized and the paper suspended for "injury to public morals" (documents 5 and 6). choice The suspension lasts nineteen days. When the paper returns, chapters 32 and 33 appear with passages blanked; on 19 October the last cut of chapter 33 is printed as an empty frame and the editors announce that the story will be finished as a book. choice

24 October. The British freighter Normanton sinks off Wakayama; its British officers take to the boats and leave all twenty-five Japanese passengers to drown, and a British consular court acquits the captain. documented The fury over extraterritoriality fills the papers for months and buries the serial's suspension as news. inference

December. Yajima Kajiko, the first head of a Tokyo mission girls' school, founds the Tokyo Women's Reform Society, the Japanese branch of the Woman's Christian Temperance Union, with herself as president. documented Its founders disapprove of the serial. Many of its younger members have read it twice. choice

1887The Apocalypse Book

January. The empress, who had first appeared in Western dress the previous summer at the Peeresses' School, issues a memorandum urging women to adopt it: the kimono of the day, it says, is a bad legacy of the age of civil wars, and Western dress, divided into upper and lower garments, is closer to the dress of ancient Japanese women. documented The girls who have been wearing a boy's jacket since 1885 take it as permission. choice

1 April. Tomoshibi Shinbun becomes Mezamashi Shinbun. documented The same month, Part Four appears as a book, after the Home Ministry's censor has removed one passage about the white horse (Part XV). choice It sells its first printing of 10,000 copies in nine days. choice Readers who had waited six months for the end got the Rose Gate, the coffin, the fingertips, Utena's disappearance and Anthy walking out of the academy gate in a pink travelling suit. The ending's force in 1887 is hard to recover. In a society where a woman left her father's house for her husband's and her husband's only for the grave, a young woman walking out of her brother's house alone, with a trunk and no destination, to look for someone she loves, was not a romance. It was a scandal and a promise. inference

20 April. Prime Minister Itō gives a fancy-dress ball at his official residence, and a scandal about the prime minister and a nobleman's wife follows it. The paper that had serialised Utena, renamed Mezamashi three weeks before, reports the scandal and is suspended for eight weeks; in all it spends 133 days of 1887 under suspension. documented (Part XVIII) Readers who had finished Part Four the week before needed nobody to tell them what Akio Ōtori was. The identification of Akio with Itō, which had been a joke, becomes a political weapon, and Liberal speakers begin quoting the serial's car-ride line, "I will show you the End of the World", in the halls where they attack the government's foreign policy. choice

Summer. Foreign Minister Inoue Kaoru's treaty-revision talks collapse under public anger at their concessions; he resigns in September, and the Rokumeikan era ends with him. documented The book of the ballroom, as some critics called Utena, outlives the ballroom. inference

Autumn. The first kabuki Utena, an authorised adaptation by the house playwright of the Shintomi-za, opens to full houses: a girl playing a prince, played by a man who specialises in women, which kabuki audiences found more natural than the women's companies did. choice Its most admired scene is the ball. Its most cut scene is the last.

25 December. The Peace Preservation Ordinance takes effect the day it is issued, and between the night of the 26th and the 28th some 570 activists are ordered to leave Tokyo, by the rule that bars anyone the police think dangerous from living within three ri of the palace. documented Among them is the paper's founder, Hoshi Tōru; without him Mezamashi falls into a crisis that ends with its sale to the Osaka Asahi. documented Among them too is the Liberal politician Nakajima Nobuyuki, and his wife, who appears on it as Nakajima Shōen: Kishida Toshiko. He goes to Yokohama and she follows; in March 1888 they settle in Ōta, below the Bluff, and in May she becomes an honorary professor at Ferris. documented In this timeline the expulsion list also includes the paper's former serial editor. choice The Nakajimas' move to the city of the Bluff, and Kishida's chair at the school every reader thought the academy was modelled on, are the foundation of the Kishida hypothesis about the authorship (Part XIV). inference

1888The collected edition and the crash

Spring. The rose bubble bursts. A dealer in Komagome who cannot pay for forty crates of French roses kills himself, the papers discover that the "Black Rose" plants sold for thirty yen were ordinary crimson roses grown in shade, and prices fall by nine-tenths in a month. choice What survives the crash is the trade: Japan now has a dozen nurseries that know how to grow, graft and ship Western roses, and a public that wants them (Part XII).

June. Tanabe (Miyake) Kaho publishes Warbler in the Grove, usually counted the first modern novel by a Japanese woman, a satire of the Westernised girls of the Rokumeikan years, written while she was still at school. documented In this timeline reviewers compare it, unfavourably, with Utena; Kaho's satire of a Westernised girl is read as an answer to the serial's girls, and the fee she earns will, a few years later, persuade Higuchi Natsu, the future Ichiyō, that a woman can live by writing, as in our history. documented for the fee's effect on Ichiyō; choice for the comparison.

10 July. The Osaka Asahi's owners relaunch Mezamashi Shinbun as the Tokyo Asahi Shinbun. documented In this timeline they pay noticeably more than they would otherwise have done, for a paper whose subscription list had been built by the serial and whose name was still attached to it. choice The collected edition of all thirty-nine chapters appears in four volumes the same year, with Juri's surname changed by one character (Part XV). choice

September. The duelling scandal. In June the magazine Nihonjin had exposed conditions in Mitsubishi's Takashima coal mine; when the journalist Inukai Tsuyoshi denied the report in the Chōya, its author, Matsuoka Kōichi, a pupil of the swordsman Sakakibara Kenkichi, challenged him to a duel, with the famous journalists Miyake Setsurei and Shiga Shigetaka as his seconds. Inukai refused, reportedly calling duelling "a barbarous survival". On 30 September, at a debate of the five private law schools in the hall of the Imperial University, the Paris-trained prosecutor Kōmyōji Saburō declared that "the duel is the flower of civilisation", and challenges followed one after another. documented In this timeline the papers have a word ready for every challenger: he is a 決闘者, read, as in the serial, duelist. choice

December. Sudō Sadanori stages his "political-youth" play in Osaka, the beginning of shinpa, the "new school" theatre. documented Shinpa will stage Utena more often than any other novel of the 1880s. choice

1889The constitution and the duelling law

3–28 January. The Yomiuri serialises A Mirror for Women by Kimura Akebono, the teenage daughter of a Tokyo restaurant owner, written between shifts in the family restaurant: its heroine quarrels with her father, runs away, studies in the women's college at Cambridge, works in a New York factory and comes home to build a factory of her own. It holds the dictionary's earliest example of the word for a woman's men's dress, 男装. Kimura died the next year, not yet twenty-one. documented In this timeline the word is already four years old, and the reviewers call her heroine an Utena who went abroad. choice

11 February. The emperor bestows the constitution on his subjects. Its first article declares that the empire shall be reigned over by a line of emperors unbroken for ages eternal. The Imperial House Law, issued the same day, restricts the succession to males of the male line, the first such rule in Japanese history. documented The same morning the education minister, Mori Arinori, is stabbed by a nationalist; he dies the next day. documented

The rule had not been certain. The Senate's first draft constitution of 1876 allowed a woman to inherit, and its draft of 1880 allowed the female line "if there is no help for it"; a public debate of the liberal Ōmeisha society in 1882, serialised in a Tokyo paper, ended against reigning empresses, the speakers arguing that an empress's husband would rule from behind her and that her son would not be of the unbroken line. Most strikingly, a draft of the house rules drawn up inside the imperial household under Itō in late 1885 and early 1886 provided that if the male line failed the throne should pass through the female line, and said whom an empress should marry. Inoue Kowashi attacked it, quoting the debaters of 1882: an empress's son would bear his father's name, and the dynasty would change. The next draft, in 1886, dropped every female provision, and none came back. The law of 1889 adds that a woman who serves as regent must be unmarried. documented The imperial household's female clause was therefore drafted and killed in exactly the months when the Black Rose Book was running in Tokyo. documented for the dates. In this timeline Inoue's memorandum does not mention the serial; no paper of his would. But the men who read it had all heard what the newspapers were saying about girls who wanted to be princes, and a clause that had been a lawyer's contingency now looked like an invitation. inference The serial's first effect on Japanese law is to help close the door it was knocking on. inference It will take a hundred and sixteen years for the question to come back (Part XI).

May. In this timeline the duelling craze of the previous autumn reaches the girls' schools. Two pupils of a Yokohama girls' school fight with fencing foils in a garden on the Bluff over a younger girl, before a ring of classmates, and one loses an eye. choice The papers call it "a duel for the Rose Bride". choice

30 December. The Senate, after readings on 21, 26 and 27 December, passes a short law, promulgated on the 30th, that makes it a crime to issue or accept a challenge (six months to two years), to fight a duel (two to five years), to act as a second or witness "under whatever name", or knowingly to lend the ground; mocking a man for refusing a challenge is punished as defamation. It was passed, in the words of the upper house's legislative bureau today, "for fear that the Western custom of duelling would take root". documented In this timeline the law's text mentions no novel, as no Meiji law would. But the article on lending the ground was read by everyone as aimed at the gardens of girls' schools, and the newspapers called it "the Utena law", a name it has kept. choice It is still in force; police used it in January 2026. documented

1890The settlement

1 July. Japan's first general election, for a lower house elected by about one man in a hundred. documented

25 July. The Law on Assembly and Political Associations bars women, along with soldiers, police, teachers, students and minors, from political meetings and political associations. documented In this timeline the women who object are more numerous and more organised than in ours, because they have been meeting for five years in the reading circles, letter-writing clubs and Christian societies that grew up around the serial, and because the government has given them a story about themselves to resent. inference The protests do not change the law. documented for the outcome in our history; inference that it would hold here too, since the oligarchs did not need women's consent for anything.

30 October. The Imperial Rescript on Education: filial piety, conjugal harmony, loyalty, and the duty of every subject to offer himself to the state. Schools begin reciting it in the following months, before the emperor's portrait. documented

11 November. The Ryōunkaku, the twelve-storey tower in Asakusa, opens with Japan's first electric lift, two cars for ten people each, which run from the ground to the eighth floor. Admission is eight sen. The lift breaks down from the first day and is stopped in May 1891; the owners fill the dead stairwell with photographs of a hundred geisha and let the climbers vote. documented Visitors, who have read about the council's lift for five years, call it "Utena's lift", and the nickname outlives it. choice The first Diet opens on 29 November. Its Speaker is Nakajima Nobuyuki, Kishida Toshiko's husband; its Vice-Speaker is Tsuda Mamichi, who in 1875 had written against equal rights for husband and wife and against votes for women. documented Women may not vote, attend political meetings or join parties; they may watch the Diet from its galleries and petition it, which is all the political rights they have. documented Histories of the women's movement tell of a draft rule that would have closed the galleries to women too, dropped after protests; I could not check it against the rules themselves. In this timeline the protest is a petition of several thousand signatures, presented by a delegation of former mission-school girls wearing black jackets over their kimono. choice On 20 January 1891 the temporary Diet building burns down before dawn, and the lower house moves into the Tokyo Girls' School. documented
