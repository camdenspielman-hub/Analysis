IV

The thirty-nine chapters

A concordance: each episode of 1997 beside the chapter of 1885–87 that tells the same story

Every episode of the television series has a chapter in the serial, in the same order, with the same events, the same people under the same names, and the same things left unexplained. The serial's author changed the furniture and nothing else.

The table gives each chapter's heading as it stood in the newspaper or the 1887 book, in the serial's literary Japanese, with a reading and a translation; then the episode it matches; then what the 1885 telling does with it. Two habits run through it. Where the anime used a machine that 1885 did not have, the serial substitutes the nearest thing an 1885 Tokyo reader could picture and, if possible, one that carries the same charge: an adult's carriage for an adult's sports car, a magic lantern for a planetarium projector, a family register for a blood-type chart. And where the anime's Japanese title rests on a pun, the serial finds a Meiji pun to put in its place. choice All headings are my compositions, and so is everything the third column and the notes below say about the serial's reception: reprints, readers' names and reactions, the censor's marks. choice The headings' spelling follows the mixed habits of the 1880s press, with historical kana in the readings.

The part titles are the newspaper's (第一編 to 第四編). The names in brackets are the ones readers gave them, and which stuck: the third part has been "the Carriage Book" since 1886, much as the anime's staff called their third arc the sports-car arc.

Part One 第一編 [the Council Book, 総代会の巻] · chapters 1–13 · Jiyū no Tomoshibi, April–August 1885

{{RUN_NOTE}}

1
薔薇の花嫁Bara no hanayomeThe Rose Bride

Episode 1: The Rose Bride · 2–10 April 1885

The chapter

Opens on a boxed "Western tale" printed in a ruled frame like a page of an imported picture book: an orphaned princess, a travelling prince on a white horse, a ring, and the narrator's gesaku aside that resolving to become a prince herself may not have been wise. Ōtori Academy stands on a bluff above a harbour in Kanagawa; its woman teacher objects to Utena's boys' high-collared jacket and red knee breeches, and Utena answers that no rule forbids them. She beats the boys' baseball nine single-handed. The birdcage conservatory is iron and glass, like the palm houses in imported prints. Wakaba's letter is pinned on the notice board; the challenge comes in the kenjutsu hall; the gate in the forbidden wood opens when water falls on her ring; the stone stair climbs to the duelling garden under a castle hanging upside down, which Saionji calls a mirage (蜃気楼). He draws the sword from Anthy's breast; Utena fights with a bamboo shinai cut short and wins with the stub. Anthy is waiting at the gate: she belongs to Utena now.

2
誰が為に薔薇は微笑むTa ga tame ni bara wa hohoemuFor Whom the Rose Smiles

Episode 2: For Whom the Rose Smiles · 11–19 April 1885

The chapter

The council rides a hydraulic lift up the tower. No reader had ridden one: Japan's only lift was a freight hoist at a paper mill, and the nearest thing in print was Kume Kunitake's account of the Iwakura Mission, whose envoys were hauled up a mine shaft near Newcastle in an iron cage and felt "as if rising from the underworld to heaven". On the way up the council recites the creed of the chick and the egg, printed in the clipped Sino-Japanese of a proclamation. The empty east dormitory; Anthy's tea; Chu-Chu, "a small beast of the southern seas, neither mouse nor monkey". Saionji's exchange diary becomes an exchange notebook of letters. Utena means to lose, shields her rose by reflex, and a young man in white with a red-lined cape dives from the castle into her. The illustration of the dive was reprinted as a single-sheet print within the month.

舞踏会の夜Butōkai no yoruThe Night of the Ball

Episode 3: On the Night of the Ball · 20–28 April 1885

The chapter

A ball at a mansion the chapter does not name, in the manner of the Rokumeikan, which had opened some seventeen months earlier: bustled gowns, a naval band, dance cards. The first chapter to be read as satire of the Rokumeikan.

Episode 4: The Sunlit Garden: Prelude · 29 April – 6 May 1885

The first comic chapter, in the old gesaku manner. Runaway horse, the boy Tsuwabuki and his embroidered handkerchief, the bull he once watched Tōga stop. Episode 8: Curried High Trip · 15–24 June 1885

The chapter

The anime's pun on 華麗 (splendid) and curry survives, because one Meiji spelling of curry was カレイ: the heading's ruby gives both. Saionji's notebook, Chu-Chu's banana, and the discovery that the explosion was simply Anthy's cooking.

14
黒薔薇の少年等Kurobara no shōnen-raThe Boys of the Black Rose

Episode 14: The Boys of the Black Rose · 13–21 October 1885

The chapter

Akio Ōtori appears: acting chairman, betrothed to the chairman's daughter Kanae. To an 1885 reader the different surnames of Anthy Himemiya and her brother explain themselves, though the chapter, like the episode, never says why: he must have been taken into the Ōtori house as adopted heir and son-in-law to be, a common arrangement in Meiji families. His tower room holds an orrery and a magic lantern that throws the stars on the dome. Under Nemuro Memorial Hall, Mikage interviews the troubled in a descending lift whose framed specimen regresses from butterfly to leaf. White lilies on the desks: the Easter lily, which Yokohama nurseries were beginning to ship to America.

The boy who wants to be grown up at once studies kissing, for which the translated fiction of the next year or two would make 接吻 the standard word, in a translated English novel of the kind that had been a runaway success in 1878–79, instead of the anime's films. Part Three 第三編 [the Carriage Book, 馬車の巻] · chapters 25–33 · Tomoshibi Shinbun, May–October 1886; suspended for nineteen days after chapter 31, and given up after chapter 33

{{RUN_NOTE}}

25
二人の永遠黙示録Futari no eien mokushirokuThe Eternal Apocalypse of the Two

Episode 25: Their Eternal Apocalypse · 11–22 May 1886

The chapter

The girls move into the tower. Akio explains his name: the morning star, Lucifer, who chose to be king of hell; 黙示録 was the new Bible's word for Revelation. Then the ride. Tōga cups his ear for the sound that races through the End of the World, and the sound is hooves and iron tyres: Akio's light open carriage, crest on the door, which he drives himself at night along roads lit by gas lamps. There were fewer than two thousand passenger carriages in all Japan, and a minister's had a coachman on the box: Ōkubo Toshimichi's had died defending him in 1878. Akio drives his own, and readers took the detail as the measure of the man. At the moment the anime has him vault onto the bonnet, he stands up on the box and lets the reins fall, and the horses run with nobody driving. In the duel, overturned carriages stand on the garden floor with their lamps throwing beams upward, and the Sword of Dios thins to nothing. Anthy draws a new sword out of Utena.

The recurring machinery

The anime's set pieces have to survive as print, and they do, in forms the Meiji press already had.

The storybook prologue
A boxed "Western tale" opening chapters 1, 3, 8, 13, 16 and 31, set in a heavier rule like the imported picture books sold at Maruzen. The prince's face is never drawn.
The creed
Printed in proclamation style, unattributed, as the council rides the lift in Part One; once more in chapter 15, with "cage" for "shell", and a last time in chapter 38. In between, the lift often rises in silence. From 1886 it was the most quoted passage in Japanese.
The shadow plays
Two girls drawn as black silhouettes against a rose-patterned wall, in the convention of kage-e shadow prints, trading a riddle or a skit in nearly every chapter, duel or none, most often just before the duel; in Part Two a single girl performs for a listener at the lockers. Some open with a short formula that readers chanted; the serial gives the girls no names.
The duel songs
Each duel installment prints a chorus in the new-style verse of the 1882 Shintaishi-shō, packed with the vocabulary of the decade's science (evolution, embryology, the galvanic cell) wound round Buddhist terms. They were set to music by the 1890s, and the stage versions sang them.
The descent
In Part One the figure who dives from the castle is drawn whole only twice, in chapters 2 and 11, as in the anime's first arc; later chapters give him a line of text and, in the cuts, a small diving shape, as the anime gives him a few frames.
The previews
Each chapter but the last ends with a 次回 notice naming the next heading and closing on the same cryptic tag, 絶対運命黙示録, "Absolute Destiny: Apocalypse". Nobody in the serial explains it.
