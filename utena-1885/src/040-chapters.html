IV

The thirty-nine chapters

Each episode of 1997 beside the chapter of 1885–87 that tells the same story, and how each chapter was received as it came out

Every episode of the television series has a chapter in the serial, in the same order, with the same events, the same people under the same names, and the same things left unexplained. The serial's author changed the furniture and nothing else.

Each entry below gives the chapter's heading as it stood in the newspaper or the 1887 book, in the serial's literary Japanese, with a reading and a translation, and the episode it matches with that episode's official English title. Under The chapter comes what the 1885 telling does with the episode; under As it came out, how it was received in the days and weeks it appeared. Two habits run through the chapters. Where the anime used a machine that 1885 did not have, the serial substitutes the nearest thing an 1885 Tokyo reader could picture and, if possible, one that carries the same charge: an adult's carriage for an adult's sports car, a magic lantern for a planetarium projector, a family register for a blood-type chart. And where the anime's Japanese title rests on a pun, the serial finds a Meiji pun to put in its place. choice All headings are my compositions, and so is everything the entries say about the serial's reception: the dates of each chapter's run, the letters, the prints, the columns, the censor's marks. Where an entry sets a chapter beside a real event of the same week, the event is real and labelled. choice The headings' spelling follows the mixed habits of the 1880s press, with historical kana in the readings.

The part titles are the newspaper's (第一編 to 第四編). The names in brackets are the ones readers gave them, and which stuck: the third part has been "the Carriage Book" since 1886, much as the anime's staff called their third arc the sports-car arc.

Part One 第一編 [the Council Book, 総代会の巻] · chapters 1–13 · Jiyū no Tomoshibi, April–August 1885

Eighty-four installments, one woodblock cut each, in a four-page paper sold for a sen a copy. choice The serial began as one entertainment among several, after the nihilists and beside the court news and the price of rice, and for its first month the only people who wrote about it were its readers. Small papers printed readers' letters in a column of their own, often under pen names, and answered them in print. documented The reception notes below draw on four sources of this timeline: that column; the Monday column in which a rival paper's critic, who signed himself Suimutei, reviewed other papers' serials; the memoir the serial editor published in 1909; and the letters and diaries of readers, a few of which were printed in the 1930s, when the first generation of readers began to die. choice

1
薔薇の花嫁Bara no hanayomeThe Rose Bride

Episode 1: The Rose Bride · 2–10 April 1885

The chapter

Opens on a boxed "Western tale" printed in a ruled frame like a page of an imported picture book: an orphaned princess, a travelling prince on a white horse, a ring, and the narrator's gesaku aside that resolving to become a prince herself may not have been wise. Ōtori Academy stands on a bluff above a harbour in Kanagawa; its woman teacher objects to Utena's boys' high-collared jacket and red knee breeches, and Utena answers that no rule forbids them. She beats the boys' baseball nine single-handed and explains the jacket: she means to be a prince who saves princesses. The birdcage conservatory is iron and glass, like the palm houses in imported prints; from a window Utena sees Saionji strike Anthy there. Wakaba's letter is pinned on the notice board; the challenge comes in the kenjutsu hall; the gate in the forbidden wood opens when water falls on her ring; the stone stair climbs to the duelling garden under a castle hanging upside down, which Saionji calls a mirage (蜃気楼). Anthy pins a green rose on him and a white one on Utena, and he knocks her down for wishing Utena luck. He draws the sword from Anthy's breast; Utena fights with a bamboo shinai cut short, remembers the prince's words, and wins with the stub. Tōga has watched it all through opera glasses. Anthy is waiting at the gate: she belongs to Utena now.

As it came out

Almost nobody noticed. The paper's readers were finishing the nihilists, who had one more day to run, and the first installment drew a single letter, from a reader in Honjo who took the boxed fairy tale for a translation and asked which Western picture book it came from; the paper did not answer. choice The first sign of anything more came from Yokohama, where the paper's agent, who sold it at the station and in the Japanese shops near the foreign settlement, ordered ten more copies for the second week and thirty more for the third (Part VI). choice The orders came from the Bluff, where pupils of the mission schools had noticed that an academy on a hill above a harbour, with Western buildings, a piano and a woman teacher who disapproved of everything, looked very like their own. inference Utena's answer to that teacher, that no rule forbids her jacket, was the first sentence of the serial to be repeated in a classroom, and the first to be punished there. choice

2
誰が為に薔薇は微笑むTa ga tame ni bara wa hohoemuFor Whom the Rose Smiles

Episode 2: For Whom the Rose Smiles · 11–19 April 1885

The chapter

The council rides a hydraulic lift up the tower. No reader had ridden one: Japan's only lift was a freight hoist at a paper mill, and the nearest thing in print was Kume Kunitake's account of the Iwakura Mission, whose envoys were hauled up a mine shaft near Newcastle in an iron cage and felt "as if rising from the underworld to heaven". On the way up the council recites the creed of the chick and the egg, printed in the clipped Sino-Japanese of a proclamation. The empty east dormitory, which Anthy is already cleaning; Anthy's tea; Chu-Chu, "a small beast of the southern seas, neither mouse nor monkey". Saionji's exchange diary becomes an exchange notebook of letters, his proof that Anthy must obey him. When she refuses him he strikes her to the ground, and Utena accepts a rematch, meaning to lose. In the garden it is Utena, champion now, who draws the sword from Anthy; Saionji fights with his own, which since the edict of 1876 a civilian might keep but not wear in the street. Utena shields her rose by reflex, and a young man in white with a red-lined cape dives from the castle into her; her next stroke cuts through his steel and his rose. Tōga, at his opera glasses, wonders whether that was the Power of Dios.

As it came out

The creed made the second chapter. Printed in the clipped Sino-Japanese of a proclamation and attributed to nobody, it read like the handbills of the People's Rights men, and Kanda's boarding-house students, who already read political fiction aloud to one another, copied it into their notebooks and chanted it. choice Reading aloud in company was still the usual way to take in a popular novel in the 1880s, as the critic Maeda Ai argued in 1973. documented The cut of the dive, the young man in white plunging from the inverted castle, was reprinted as a single-sheet print within the month, the first of hundreds. choice And on the Monday after the chapter ended, the critic of a rival small paper in Kyōbashi, who signed his weekly column on other papers' serials 酔夢亭, Suimutei, "the Pavilion of Drunken Dreams", noticed it for the first time: "A gesaku in a schoolgirl's hand, with a sermon pinned to it. The best-drawn character is the lift." choice He wrote about it nearly every Monday for two years, and never stopped saying he disliked it.

3
舞踏会の夜Butōkai no yoruThe Night of the Ball

Episode 3: On the Night of the Ball · 20–28 April 1885

The chapter

Utena wakes turning over her ring and breakfasts with Anthy alone in a hall of empty chairs. Tōga introduces himself, lifts a lock of her hair, has his hand knocked away, and shows Utena a ring like hers, worn, he says, so that he might meet her; she wonders whether he could be the prince. Three girls corner Anthy over Saionji's disgrace, and Nanami Kiryū, Tōga's sister, drives them off and sweetly invites Anthy to the ball as a candidate for its queen. It is held at a mansion the chapter does not name, in the manner of the Rokumeikan, which had opened some seventeen months earlier: bustled gowns, a naval band, dance cards. Tōga sends Utena a pink gown with a card bearing the chapter's heading in his hand, and Nanami sends one to Anthy. Anthy admits that crowds frighten her, because the faces all begin to look alike, and at the ball the cuts draw the guests as empty outlines. A "waiter" ruins Anthy's gown with champagne, and the three tormentors prove to be Nanami's own attendants. Utena comes down the stair in her own jacket, crosses the buffet, makes a gown of a tablecloth and dances with Anthy, and Chu-Chu cues the band. In the last cut the guests have faces, and Anthy looks up at Utena, startled into a smile.

As it came out

The first chapter to be read as satire of the Rokumeikan, the hall for Western balls that the foreign minister, Inoue Kaoru, had opened in November 1883 to show the treaty powers a Japan fit for equal treaties. documented The serial never names the mansion, and readers named it at once. choice Inoue's partner in that policy, Itō Hirobumi, had gone to Tianjin to negotiate with Li Hongzhang, and news of the convention they signed on 18 April reached the Tokyo papers in the same days as the ball. documented for the convention; inference for the timing. The cut that drew the guests as blank outlines was the joke of the season; the last cut, in which they have faces again, was the one readers kept. choice Nanami's three attendants drew their first letters, nearly all from girls who said they knew the type. choice On the Bluff, girls began to call a tablecloth worn as a skirt an "Utena gown" and to dance in pairs in the dormitory after prayers, which the teachers allowed until they learned where it came from. choice

4
光さす庭　序の曲Hikari sasu niwa: jo no kyokuThe Sunlit Garden: Prelude

Episode 4: The Sunlit Garden: Prelude · 29 April – 6 May 1885

The chapter

The chapter opens on a duel not yet fought: a cut of Miki saluting Utena in the duelling garden and saying he needs the Bride to win back "that music". Nanami fawns on Miki Kaoru, piano prodigy, and finds a carte-de-visite photograph of Anthy in his score; he plays one piece only, on an instrument most readers had never heard, hoping that "she" will hear it. When the three girls corner Anthy again Miki steps in, and each time she thanks him the text breaks for a bar of handwritten music before he blushes. He tutors Anthy, and swears to Utena that he will never duel for the Bride. Tōga asks whether he has found his "shining thing" (輝くもの), and the memory of two children at a garden piano is drawn with their faces left blank. Nanami's three "Operations" (snail, snake, octopus) are each undone by what Anthy keeps in her pencil box, drawer and cupboard; the snake meets a mongoose, a creature most readers knew only from Indian travel books. Anthy serves sweetened ice for dinner, crushed with a mallet in the manner of the ice-water shop Yokohama had had since 1869. Then she slips downstairs to the piano, and Miki hears in her playing the sound he lost as a child, his sister's. The last cut closes the remembered garden round Anthy.

As it came out

Few readers had ever heard a piano, and fewer could read the bars of music the chapter prints, in the Western notation that the government's Music Investigation Committee, set up in 1879, was teaching to the first trained music teachers; Uryū Shigeko, back from Vassar and often called Japan's first pianist, had been teaching there since 1882. documented Two music teachers wrote in. One wrote out how the bars would sound and found them the opening of something unfinished; the other said they could not be played by one person. choice Nanami's three Operations drew the first letters about her, nearly all hostile, and a cut of the mongoose meeting the snake was pasted up in a Kanda lending library. choice A careful reader in Shiba noticed that in the remembered garden the two children's faces were left blank, and asked whether the illustrator had run out of time. It is the first record of anyone noticing the serial's habit of erasing faces. choice

5
光さす庭　終の曲Hikari sasu niwa: tsui no kyokuThe Sunlit Garden: Finale

Episode 5: The Sunlit Garden: Finale · 7–15 May 1885

The chapter

Miki at last beats Juri at fencing, and she tells him his blade is not made for battle. Anthy plays, and Miki tells the twins' story: they wrote the piece together, and on the day Miki had measles his sister went on stage alone and never played again. The cut draws every face in the memory as a blank. Measles was no metaphor to the 1885 reader; most families had lost a child to it. When Utena says she cannot forgive a system that takes away Anthy's freedom, Miki moves to dissolve the council; Juri puts it down to love, Tōga to youth, and an apple on a chair, whole when the meeting begins, lies in six wedges at its end though nobody has touched it. Kozue leaves the music room straightening her dress; Tōga is inside, shirt open, and tells Miki that precious things are taken unless defended, and that only the Bride's betrothed can make her keep playing. Anthy agrees brightly that she would stop if Utena told her to. Miki hands Utena a white rose in class. He duels with his own fencing foil, evenly, until Anthy calls out for Utena; his face goes blank, his pale blue rose is cut, and the picture of the garden cracks. Afterwards Kozue tells a friend she never had any talent: her brother covered for her.

As it came out

Measles was the surprise. Miki's illness on the day of the recital was no metaphor to the families who read it, and most of the week's letters came from parents; a mother in Ushigome who had lost a daughter to it wrote that she had read the chapter twice. choice The first complaint about morals came the same week, from "a father in Hongō", who wished to know what Tōga had been doing in the music room with his shirt open, and was answered by another reader, who wrote that a father who did not know should not ask. choice Suimutei called the apple that falls into six wedges with nobody touching it "the only honest member of the council". choice On 13 May, while the chapter ran, the Yomiuri printed a reader's letter on reforming Japanese poetry, signed with a pen name, which also announced the treatise on the novel that its writer, the young Tsubouchi Shōyō, would start publishing in September. documented In this timeline nothing Shōyō published ever mentioned the serial (Part V). choice

6
七実様御用心Nanami-sama goyōjinTake Care, Miss Nanami

Episode 6: Take Care, Miss Nanami! · 16–22 May 1885

The chapter

The first comic chapter, in the old gesaku manner. Nanami, alone at night, runs as if followed; a figure stands under a gas lamp, and the chapter never explains it. A stray baseball of Utena's knocks her flat. Overhearing Tōga and Anthy plan a killing in the conservatory, she pictures them as lovers plotting her death; the victims are the ticks on the roses. Runaway horse, the boy Tsuwabuki and his embroidered handkerchief: her rescuer is a pupil of the elementary division, and she claims him on the spot as her sweetheart, then uses him as servant and bodyguard. Miki's diagnosis uses words 1885 had: she cannot see past her brother, and every other boy is tsuma, the garnish beside the sashimi, where the anime has parsley. Then the bull Tsuwabuki once watched Tōga stop: he confesses that he staged the accidents so that he could save her as her brother once did. Nanami records his confession on a phonograph (the 蘇言機, "machine that brings words back to life", first demonstrated in Tokyo in 1878), and the chapter's climax is a boxing kangaroo escaped from a travelling circus, felled by Tōga in Western boxing drawers after Nanami has carried the boy to safety. Nanami dismisses her suitor, and he asks to be her little brother instead.

As it came out

The first comic chapter split the readers. Liberal subscribers who had been reading a political novel wrote that the author had lost his nerve, or had been got at; the women who read the paper, by the editor's later account, wrote in twice as fast to say they had laughed aloud. choice Suimutei was delighted: the serial had "found its true reader at last, which is a kangaroo". choice The print-sellers could not keep up with Tōga in Western boxing drawers, a cut the paper had thought daring, and learned what they would sell from the serial for the next ten years: Tōga, half dressed. choice Readers who had heard the phonograph demonstrated in Tokyo in 1878 wrote to say that it could not have recorded a confession made across a garden, and the paper replied that it could at Ōtori Academy. documented for the demonstration; choice for the rest. About the figure under the gas lamp, nobody wrote at all, which the editor thought the strangest thing about the week. choice

見果てぬ樹璃Mihatenu JuriJuri Unfulfilled

Episode 7: Unfulfilled Jury · 23–31 May 1885

The chapter

Juri Arisugawa, captain of the academy's French fencing, duels to prove that miracles do not happen. The chapter opens on a hand laid over the young Juri's eyes. In the present she beats every fencer who faces her and agrees with Utena that duelling for the Bride is foolish; when Anthy offers her an orange rose, she knocks her to the floor. Shiori and the stolen boy are told in tableaux; the string telephone is a children's toy of the day, its line running past Juri, and Shiori covers Juri's eyes while the boy kisses Shiori. By a pond at night Utena tells the prince story; Juri strokes her hair, then tries to wrench the ring from her finger and, in tears, demands proof of the miracle by the sword. The shadow girls play a girl who says she is glad a cold kept her from the class outing to the animal garden at Ueno, opened three years before, and who really wanted to go. In the duel Juri calls Utena "prince" only to sneer; the castle flares with no figure in it, and Juri disarms her anyway. The sword falls from the air and spears Juri's orange rose; Utena never touches it. The locket holds a carte-de-visite photograph cut from a class picture, and the chapter's last cut shows whose face it is; no line of the text says so.

As it came out

The chapter that made the serial notorious. On the day the locket opened, the letters column, which usually printed three or four letters, printed eleven, most of them from women, and the editor opened a second column of letters for the first time in the paper's history (Part VI). choice Some letters were outraged and more were grateful; one, signed "a woman of Kōjimachi, aged forty", said only that she had once had a friend like Shiori. choice Suimutei called the chapter indecent, and then gave the whole of his next column to it, which his readers noticed. choice Photographers in Yokohama found girls asking them to cut a friend's carte-de-visite down to fit a locket, and a Ginza clockmaker advertised lockets "that open, in the Western manner" the following month. choice The first letters about Juri herself came from girls who wanted to know whether she would be punished, and from more who hoped she would not. choice Then the serial stopped for two weeks.

華麗なる天竺道中Karei naru Tenjiku dōchūThe Splendid Road to India

Episode 8: Curried High Trip · 15–24 June 1885

The chapter

The anime's pun on 華麗 (splendid) and curry survives, because one Meiji spelling of curry was カレイ: the heading's ruby gives both, and its 道中 is the road-journey word of Jippensha Ikku's comic travel novels. Nanami's girls say they have doctored Anthy's curry with the wrong tin of spice; the cooking-class curry explodes and swaps Utena's and Anthy's minds. The council meets in emergency, and the anime's telephone call reaches it through a speaking tube. The cuts tell the reader who is who by posture alone: "Anthy" slaps back at the bullies and hits the ball out of the grounds, while "Utena" waters roses. Nanami confesses, pleading dry-eyed on her brother's chest, and is packed off to India by mail steamer for the spice; every glimpse of India ends in elephants. The shadow girls lecture on four-character sayings, ending on an outlaw hit by her own bullet: 因果応報, deeds repaid in kind. Saionji drags "Anthy" to the gymnastics store and hands over their exchange notebook, and Utena, reading his insults about herself, writes a one-phrase reply in red, the colour of a teacher's corrections. Nanami comes back sunburnt with the spice. Chu-Chu's banana skin sends her sprawling and the spice is lost; the first tin turns up unopened, and the explosion was simply Anthy's cooking. A second curry puts the girls back and swaps Saionji with Chu-Chu.

As it came out

The fortnight's silence filled the town with rumours, and Suimutei collected them in one column: the author was ill, was in prison, was dead, was a woman whose husband had found the manuscript, was three people. choice (The last is what the scholars of this timeline now think: Part XVII.) Readers who had waited two weeks to learn what Juri would do next were given curry. The letters were furious and delighted in equal parts, and the delighted won: within the month Western restaurants in Tsukiji and Ueno served a rice curry under the chapter's heading. choice Curry had been on Tokyo's Western menus for a decade, and the cookery books of 1872 that first explained it to the Japanese listed a frog among the meats to put in it. documented On 19 June, in the middle of the chapter, the paper printed a schoolgirl's letter pointing out that the byline could be read as a girl's name (Part XIX, document 2). choice Fukuzawa Yukichi's editorials on Japanese women had appeared in his own paper in the fortnight of the pause (Part VI). documented

9
永遠ありといふ城Eien ari to iu shiroThe Castle Said to Hold Eternity

Episode 9: The Castle Said to Hold Eternity · 25 June – 5 July 1885

The chapter

Beaten again by Tōga at kenjutsu, Saionji tells Utena he wants the Bride only so as not to lose to him: with her he will possess eternity. His memory: the two boys share a hired tricycle in the rain, the toy every Tokyo boy wanted in the early 1880s, and enter a church with three coffins laid flat in the Western way, which to a reader used to seated barrel coffins was itself uncanny. In the one Tōga opens, a girl whose face the cut never shows lies among roses and wants to be left there: her parents are in the other two, she says, and nothing is eternal. Next day she walks in the funeral, and Saionji supposes Tōga showed her something eternal. A rose-sealed letter tells Saionji the castle will fall tonight, and an unseen voice tells Utena that Anthy has been taken. The serial uses the anime's own word, kamikakushi, spirited away, which in 1885 still described something that happened to children. Saionji drags Anthy to the gate while she protests that the rules forbid it; Utena finds him senseless there and Anthy in a coffin inside a giant rose on the duelling garden, and the chapter shows nobody felling him or laying her in it. The castle falls, Utena pulls Anthy out, and everything stands again. Saionji cuts at her with a live blade, and Tōga takes the cut meant for Utena's back, asking her whether her prince was not a man like him. In bed that night, not alone, Tōga writes to someone the chapter never names: he sent Saionji's letter himself, in that person's name, and Saionji will be expelled. Whoever believes in friendship, he adds, is a fool.

As it came out

Coffins sold papers. The three Western coffins laid flat in a church were what readers talked about, and the word kamikakushi, which mothers still used of children who vanished, made the chapter frightening in a way the fencing had never been. inference Two questions filled the letters column for the rest of the summer: who was the girl in the coffin, and to whom does Tōga write in bed? Every candidate was proposed, Anthy, Nanami, Juri and Utena herself among them, and the paper answered nobody. choice Readers waited until 1887 to hear whom Saionji would name, and have argued ever since about whether to believe him. choice Among the Kanda students the chapter made two parties, Tōga's and Saionji's, who lodged in the same boarding houses and fell out over the cut in which Tōga takes the blow meant for Utena: generous, said one party; staged, said the other. choice Suimutei sided with Saionji, "the only character in the book who is exactly as stupid as he looks". choice

七実の大切なるものNanami no taisetsu naru monoNanami's Precious Thing

Episode 10: Nanami's Precious Thing · 6–15 July 1885

The chapter

Saionji is expelled. He leaves his exchange notebook with Tōga, whose arm is in a sling and who calls them best friends; the next cut shows it burning. Anthy brings the moping Utena a kitten, and Nanami dreams of the birthday when she gave her brother a kitten and was kissed for it. Tōga's birthday party follows, a Western custom then practised only by the most Westernized families, since everyone else grew a year older at New Year; the sling is gone. Nanami knocks Utena's flowers away and slaps Anthy, whose present is the kitten, and remembers that her brother preferred the first kitten to her. A rose lands at Utena's feet, and only then does Tōga slide a ring onto his sister's finger, speaking as if she had worn one all along. Utena knocks Nanami's curved sabre into the air and cuts her pale-yellow rose; Nanami fights on with a dagger, cuts Utena's rose in turn, and stops only at her brother's shout, as her sabre snaps against the wall beside Utena's head. Then the memory she has kept from herself: a box sealed with pasted paper at the edge of a sluice, then in the current, with no kitten shown inside. Tōga holds her, thinking about the Power of Dios.

As it came out

The box at the sluice is the cut readers of 1885 remembered longest. Readers who had heard Kishida Toshiko speak in 1883 on "daughters in boxes" made the connection at once, and one of them wrote to say so, signing himself a former Liberal of Ōtsu, the town where she had been arrested for the speech. documented for the speech and arrest; choice for the letter. The serial does not say what was in the box, and the letters column filled with readers who were sure. choice Nanami became loved in this chapter. Until it she had been the villain of the comic chapters; after it most of the letters about her came from girls with elder brothers, and the first print of her alone, in her birthday dress with her brother's ring, sold out in Ginza. choice The Western birthday party puzzled readers who counted their ages from New Year, and the paper printed a short note on the custom. choice

優雅にして冷酷　其の花を摘む者Yūga ni shite reikoku: sono hana wo tsumu monoGraceful and Cruel: The One Who Plucks That Flower

Episode 11: Gracefully Cruel: The One Who Picks That Flower · 16–27 July 1885

The chapter

A picnic, with Chu-Chu in Anthy's three-tiered lacquer box. Tōga watches through opera glasses and sees a lonely princess; Utena sees him against the light, like the figure in her memory. In the birdcage conservatory, which Tōga calls Anthy's domain, Utena has Anthy say she hates being the Rose Bride, and Anthy says it in the words Utena hands her. Tōga claims to be Utena's prince, takes her ringed hand and leans in to kiss her, his hair hiding whether he does, then challenges her in the same voice. Utena resolves to keep Anthy even from her prince. Tōga fights with his own sword and leads the bout. The figure from the castle, drawn whole for the second and last time in Part One, dives into Utena; then Tōga lowers his sword and stands open, she sees him again asking whether her prince was not a man like him, and she falters. Her white rose scatters and the Sword of Dios melts in her hand. Anthy, smiling, says she is happy as the Rose Bride, and Tōga explains that she only ever followed the victor's wishes. Told by Tōga to say goodbye, Anthy calls Utena "Miss Tenjō", 天上さん where it had been ウテナ様: a surname and a plain san, for someone outside the house. Then she leaves on his arm. Utena kneels, repeating that it is not true.

As it came out

Tōga won, and Anthy went with him. The serial's readers had now watched a woman pass from one owner to a third, and the letters column filled. choice Most of the letters said one thing in different words, "give her back", and the editor, who could not print forty letters saying the same, printed the angriest of them with a note that the author had been told. choice Anthy's change of address, from ウテナ様 to 天上さん, was the sentence most often quoted, and girls on the Bluff began to call one another "Miss Tenjō" when they meant that a friendship was over. choice The mission schools broke up for the summer that week, and the serial went home with their pupils into households that had never taken the paper; the Yokohama agent's July orders were the largest of the year. choice On 20 July, in the middle of the chapter, the first number of Jogaku zasshi, the "Magazine of Women's Learning", appeared in Tokyo. documented It never named the serial (Part VI). choice

12
或は友情の為にAruiwa yūjō no tame niPerhaps for Friendship

Episode 12: For Friendship, Perhaps · 28 July – 9 August 1885

The chapter

Utena stays in bed; Wakaba, visiting, finds her jacket slashed, and Utena says she caught it on something. Next day she comes to school in the girls' uniform, which in 1885 means a Western day-dress of the kind the girls' normal schools were about to adopt, and calls it ordinary. At Ōtori Academy it is; in Tokyo such a dress was still mostly seen at the Rokumeikan. Anthy, Tōga's bride now, greets her as Miss Tenjō, and in the council room stands at his shoulder. At lunch Tōga propositions her. Wakaba, up on the table, throws a glass of water that lands on Anthy; when Wakaba blames Anthy, Utena slaps Wakaba and hands Anthy a handkerchief, which Anthy, left at Tōga's table, sits looking at. Then Wakaba, in tears, shames Utena into taking herself back. Utena challenges Tōga, and Juri lends her a foil for "the last scene". Tōga draws the Sword of Dios from Anthy and orders his Bride to give up her body and guard the sword; she kisses the blade, and it becomes a shaft of light that breaks Juri's foil and shreds the dress. Utena fights on with the stub. Anthy, watching with something like pity, thinks that she has seen this before; the light drains from Tōga's sword, a tear stands in her eye, and Utena cuts his rose with the broken blade, still in her red stockings. At night Anthy waits with her bag and begins the speech of chapter 1. Utena cuts her short: "Let's just go home."

The recurring machinery

The anime's set pieces have to survive as print, and they do, in forms the Meiji press already had.

The storybook prologue
A boxed "Western tale" opening chapters 1, 3, 8, 13, 16 and 31, set in a heavier rule like the imported picture books sold at Maruzen. The prince's face is never drawn.
The creed
Printed in proclamation style, unattributed, as the council rides the lift in Part One; once more in chapter 15, with "cage" for "shell", and a last time in chapter 38. In between, the lift often rises in silence. From 1886 it was the most quoted passage in Japanese.
The shadow plays
Two girls drawn as black silhouettes against a rose-patterned wall, in the convention of kage-e shadow prints, trading a riddle or a skit in nearly every chapter, duel or none, most often just before the duel; in Part Two a single girl performs for a listener at the lockers. Some open with a short formula that readers chanted; the serial gives the girls no names.
The duel songs
Each duel installment prints a chorus in the new-style verse of the 1882 Shintaishi-shō, packed with the vocabulary of the decade's science (evolution, embryology, the galvanic cell) wound round Buddhist terms. They were set to music by the 1890s, and the stage versions sang them.
The descent
In Part One the figure who dives from the castle is drawn whole only twice, in chapters 2 and 11, as in the anime's first arc; later chapters give him a line of text and, in the cuts, a small diving shape, as the anime gives him a few frames.
The previews
Each chapter but the last ends with a 次回 notice naming the next heading and closing on the same cryptic tag, 絶対運命黙示録, "Absolute Destiny: Apocalypse". Nobody in the serial explains it.
The recaps
Chapters 13, 24 and 33 are the serial's three "story so far" chapters, placed where a newspaper needed them, at the end of a part.
