V

How 1885 read it

Seven readings, all of them partly right

Meiji readers were trained to look through a story for the thing it was really about. The Edo habit of reading comic fiction and prints as coded comment on the authorities, and the 1880s fashion for political novels set in Greece or Ireland that were obviously about Japan, made every serious reader a decoder. documented Utena gave them almost too much to decode.

1. A political novel in disguise

The paper's own readers, Liberal men who had lost their party in October 1884, watched the Chichibu rising crushed a few days later, and spent the winter reading about Sophia Perovskaya in the same column, read it first as a political novel. documented for the dissolution and Chichibu. The creed of the chick that must break the world's shell read like a handbill. The Student Council, sons and daughters of rich houses who rule the academy from a balcony in the sky and take their orders from sealed letters signed "End of the World", read like the oligarchs of Satsuma and Chōshū and their circle, who governed by rescript from behind a screen. The Rose Bride, who belongs to whoever wins her by the sword, was "the people's rights": prized, fought over, and never consulted, like the geisha O-Ken ("Rights") of the first political novel, courted in 1880 by "People" and "Government". documented for the 1880 novel. Utena, the commoner in borrowed clothes who fights for the Bride and is not a prince, was the movement itself. inference The police read that way too. When Kishida Toshiko spoke on "daughters in boxes" at Ōtsu in October 1883, the inspector's report took the box for the government's press and assembly laws, the parents for the government, the daughter for the people, and the servants sent after a daughter who elopes for the police in the hall. documented Anyone who could decode a speech about daughters could decode a novel about a bride.

The names helped. Saionji, the hot-tempered vice-president who strikes the Bride and wants to possess her, shares a surname with Saionji Kinmochi, the young noble back from Paris whom an imperial order had forced out of a liberal newspaper in 1881. Arisugawa, the cool fencer who duels to disprove miracles, shares a name with an imperial prince's house. documented for the real Saionji and Arisugawa. Readers could take the borrowings as satire, as homage or as carelessness, and argued about which. inference When the Osaka Incident broke in November 1885 (Liberal activists arrested for a plot to start a revolution in Korea, with a twenty-year-old former schoolmistress, Kageyama Hideko, among them), the papers that disliked the Liberals had their headline ready: the Liberals' girl revolutionaries were no longer only in their fiction. Kageyama had, by her own account, cropped her hair and dressed entirely as a boy until she was sixteen, while the local children jeered at her as a "fake", and she had carried the plotters' explosive chemicals in a trunk because nobody searched a woman's luggage. documented for the Osaka Incident, Kageyama's arrest and her own account; choice for the headline.

2. A book about marriage

Women heard it differently, often aloud, often in the evening, at an age when few of them could read the paper themselves. inference To them the Rose Bride was not an abstraction. A Meiji bride was chosen by her parents through a go-between, transferred with her goods to her husband's household, entered in its register, and expected to obey its head; that is what "engagement" meant. The serial's word for Anthy's state, 婚約, was still a new and learned one: the dictionary's earliest record of it is the revised philosophers' lexicon of 1884. documented for the marriage customs and the word. Anthy, who is engaged to whoever holds the sword, calls each new owner by a new form of address and smiles, is the most exact portrait of a Meiji wife in Meiji fiction, and she is drawn as a horror. inference

The rest of the household is drawn as exactly. Akio's position, which a modern viewer must puzzle out, needed no explanation in 1885: a young man betrothed to the chairman's daughter and, as every reader assumed though the book never says so, taken into the house as adopted heir, ruling it while the old man is ill; that is why, readers decided, the siblings Himemiya and Ōtori have different surnames. The adopted son-in-law was a commonplace of the Meiji family, and so was his reputation for owing everything to his wife's house and resenting it. documented for the institution. Mrs Ōtori, who calls her daughter's fiancé "my only prince" in chapter 30, was the mother-in-law of a hundred kabuki plays turned inside out. inference And the boxes and coffins that run through the serial (the coffin girl in chapter 9, the box sealed with pasted paper at the sluice in chapter 10, Mikage's drawers dropping into the fire) began arriving less than two years after Kishida Toshiko had told an audience in Ōtsu that Japanese parents kept their daughters like goods "in boxes", and been arrested for it. documented for the speech and arrest. No woman reader in 1885 needed the connection made. inference

3. A schoolgirls' book

The girls of the mission schools on the Bluff read it as a book about themselves, and they were the first to read it in packs. They lived in dormitories, played the piano, learned English and French, had senior girls they worshipped, and were watched for signs of corruption by a press that had decided "schoolgirl" meant trouble. documented for the mission-school milieu and the press's attitude. In Utena they found a school that was theirs made strange and grand, a heroine who answered back to teachers, and a world in which the important relationships were between girls. inference Within a year they were exchanging carte-de-visite photographs in lockets, giving each other white roses, and calling each other by the serial's forms of address; within two, the word Bride had become the dormitory word for a younger girl attached to an older one, and prince for the older. choice In our history the schoolgirls had no name for these attachments until the slang o-me was reported in 1909 and esu ("S", for "sister") spread in the 1910s; the clinical 同性愛 came later still. documented In this timeline the words come a generation early, out of a novel, and carry its romance and its menace together. inference Part VII follows what became of this.

4. A Christian book

Japan's Christians in the mid-1880s numbered a few tens of thousands: about 30,000 Catholics in 1884, a Protestant membership that was growing fast after the revival of 1883, and the Russian Orthodox converts besides. documented They were a small community but a disproportionately literate, urban and female one, concentrated around the very schools where the serial was most read. inference Christian readers recognised what others missed. Akio introduces himself as Lucifer, the morning star who chose to rule in hell. The last arc's word for revelation is the new Bible's. The heroine's prince is called Dios, the Spanish for God. No Japanese text of the period uses that form, but Catholic readers heard in it でうす, Deusu, the sixteenth-century word for God that the French missionaries' catechisms of 1868–72 still printed as a gloss, and that their hidden-Christian grandparents had prayed to in secret. documented for the catechisms. And the Rose Bride, pierced by a million swords of human hatred and unable to die, comes out of a devotion the French priests had brought with them: Our Lady of Sorrows, whose soul, in Simeon's prophecy in Luke, a sword would pierce. documented for the devotion and the verse, which the New Testament of 1880 had put into Japanese; whether any Japanese church had an image of her with swords is not known. The missionaries were divided. Some preached against the serial's incest and its girls' love; some of their Japanese teachers, women above all, read it as a parable of the soul held captive by the world and of the one who reaches into the coffin to save it. inference

5. An allegory of the throne

The most dangerous reading took longer to form, and the government was quicker to see it than most readers were. The castle that hangs over the duelling garden is "the castle said to hold eternity". Japanese political rhetoric of the 1880s already described the imperial line as unbroken for ages eternal, and in 1889 the constitution would put the phrase in its first article. documented A castle said to hold eternity, a prince on a white horse whom everyone worships and who turns out to have fallen, a man who rules from a tower by projecting the castle onto the sky with a lantern, and a girl who is told that she cannot be the prince because she is a girl: by 1889, each of these had a meaning in Tokyo that the author of 1885 may or may not have intended. inference The parallel with Princess Kazu was easier still. Engaged as a girl to Prince Arisugawa Taruhito, she had been given instead to the shogun Iemochi in 1862 to bind the court to the shogunate, a Rose Bride transferred from one champion to another for reasons of state, and she had died only in 1877. documented Older readers who remembered her journey to Edo did not need to be told why a girl called Arisugawa stood on the council. inference Anthy's own surname made it worse. 姫宮, himemiya, is the word The Tale of Genji uses for an imperial princess, and in the 1880s, when each child born to the emperor was given a personal title ending in -miya, a commoner girl called Himemiya sounded at best presumptuous. documented for the word and the custom. A princess of the blood, kept in a tower, engaged to whoever wins her: on the proof of the 1887 book that the censor sent back, the serial editor recalled, the surname was marked every time it appeared. choice

6. A comic novel with a modern heart

The literary men read it as a gesaku, the comic popular fiction of Edo that the new criticism was trying to bury, and were unsettled to find it moving. Tsubouchi Shōyō was publishing The Essence of the Novel in parts from September 1885, arguing that the novel's subject was human feeling, shown truthfully, not moral lessons or marvels. documented In this timeline the treatise never mentions the serial, but the younger critics who took up its terms applied them to it at once, as a case of feeling truly observed inside a frame of marvels, and regretted the marvels. choice The verdict stuck to the book for a generation: true in its people, false in its castles. It took the critics of the 1920s to argue that the castles were the truth, and the people's feelings the projection. inference

7. A scandal

The police and the moralists read it as a list of offences. A girl in a man's coat: Tokyo's minor-offences ordinance had punished "women who dress as men" from August 1873, with exemptions for actors and for women in hakama, and in April 1881 the Yomiuri had reported a crop-haired fishmonger's daughter in Shiba, in a workman's coat and tight trousers, being summoned and "admonished" under it, after which she eloped with a girl from a tea stall at the Shiba Shinmei shrine. The reporter found a woman falling for a woman "too strange". documented The ordinance had lapsed when the new Penal Code took effect on 1 January 1882, and the Penal Code had dropped the clause; whether Kanagawa's police rules kept one is not known. documented So Utena broke no law that anyone could name, which annoyed the moralists more. Girls who love girls. A brother and sister. A grown man, engaged to be married, who courts a schoolgirl, and his fiancée's mother besides. Schools where boys and girls are taught together, which the Education Order of 1879 had forbidden above the elementary level. documented And the word revolution in the title, in the paper of a party that had just been dissolved. The surprise is not that the serial was suspended in 1886 but that it ran for nearly seventeen months before it was. The answer, in this timeline, is that for most of its first year the Home Ministry was watching the paper's politics, not its fiction: when it suspended the paper for ten days in December 1885, the order named its reporting of the Osaka arrests. The ministry's readers began to underline Juri's surname only in January 1886, and its press section warned the serial editor in July (Part IV); it did not treat the fiction as an offence in itself until chapter 31 gave it one. inference

Who was who

By the end of 1886 the guessing game had settled into a set of identifications that every reader knew and no editor would print. Akio was Itō Hirobumi, prime minister from December 1885, whose charm and appetite for women were common talk. documented for Itō's reputation; choice for the identification. Nemuro's hundred boys were the boys of Aizu who died on Iimori Hill in 1868, or the students of Saigō Takamori's academies who followed him to death in 1877; readers chose by region. documented for both events; choice for the readings. End of the World was the oligarchy, or the emperor, or the West, or simply adulthood, which is the reading the anime's own writer arrived at a century later. choice for the readers; documented (makers' statement: Enokido Yōji, 1998) for the writer's. Nobody identified Utena. She was, as the girls of the Bluff said, whoever was reading.

Her name did its own work. うてな was a word every reader knew from Buddhist sermons and the theatre: the lotus seat of those reborn in paradise, the seat that the lovers of The Tale of Genji vow to share, and so, through the puppet plays of Chikamatsu, the "one lotus" (一蓮托生) that lovers hope to share by dying together. documented for the senses of the word. A heroine called "the lotus seat" whose last meeting with another girl is a hand reached across a coffin lid was read in 1887, by everyone who knew their Chikamatsu, as a love-suicide refused, or postponed. The book does not say which, and the argument has never stopped. inference Older readers had a word for what she was, too: 変成男子, henjō nanshi, the Lotus Sutra's dragon king's daughter who becomes a man in an instant so that she can become a buddha, a phrase Edo comic verse had used since at least 1753 for a woman in men's clothes. documented In the serial's first year the word came back into the street as the name for a girl who dressed like Utena. choice
