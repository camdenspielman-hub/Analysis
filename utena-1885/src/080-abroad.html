VIII

Abroad

Paris, the Chinese students of Tokyo, Seoul, and the long way round to Europe's girls

Utena travelled by two roads. One ran west through Yokohama's foreign colony and the Japonisme of Paris, and made the book a minor classic of European modernism and a private text of Europe's women. The other ran through the Chinese and Korean students who came to Tokyo in their thousands after 1900, and made it part of the history of revolution in East Asia, in the sense its title meant.

The foreign settlement

The first Westerners to read the serial were the ones who lived beside it: the missionary teachers of the Bluff, whose pupils were reading it under the bedclothes. In this timeline a short-lived English-language weekly of the Yokohama settlement prints a paragraph about "a curious native romance of a girls' school" in the summer of 1885, and a longer, disapproving notice by a missionary in 1886. choice The serial's foreign reputation begins there, as gossip about what Japanese girls were reading, and the gossip is carried home on furlough.

The foreigners' handbooks followed. In this timeline a missionary's guide to Japanese reading, published in Yokohama in 1891 by a writer otherwise forgotten, gives it a short and sardonic paragraph, as a work every schoolgirl admired and no critic would defend. choice

Paris, 1900

The actress Kawakami Sadayakko, a former Tokyo geisha whose patrons had included Itō Hirobumi and Saionji Kinmochi, was billed as the star of her husband's touring company in San Francisco in May 1899 without being told, and found herself an actress. In the summer of 1900 the company played at Loïe Fuller's theatre at the Paris Exposition Universelle; Rodin, by her biographer's account, asked to sculpt her and was refused; André Gide went again and again and wrote that it was "beautiful as Aeschylus"; Picasso drew her; and a kimono-like "Yacco" dress became a fashion. documented Her company's pieces were melodramas built for foreigners, with a geisha's death scene at their centre. documented In this timeline the company adds a short piece cut from Utena: the duel in the forest garden, with Sadayakko as Utena in a cadet's jacket and a younger actress as the Bride, who never speaks. Paris had come to see a geisha die and was shown a girl win (Part XVI, document 9). choice Tokyo, reading the reviews, enjoyed the joke that Paris could not see: the actress playing the girl who defeats a council of rich men had been the favourite of two of the most powerful, and one of them was the man every reader took for Akio. inference

The translation followed quickly. Judith Gautier, novelist and Orientalist, had published Poèmes de la libellule, French versions of Japanese verse, in 1885, and in 1900 she and the composer Louis Bénédictus transcribed the music of Sadayakko's dances for publication; in 1910 she became the first woman elected to the Académie Goncourt. documented In this timeline she brings out La Fiancée des roses in 1902, from a literal version by a Japanese student in Paris. choice Her 1885 poems are often said to have been made from literal versions supplied by Saionji Kinmochi, which, if true, gives the book's French career a pleasing symmetry. documented for the claim being made; whether it is true is unverified. Gautier's version is free, lush and abridged: she cuts most of the comic chapters and all of the curry. It is the version Europe read for twenty years. choice

The women of the salons

In Paris in the first decade of the century a circle of women writers, many of them foreign, made a public culture of love between women: Natalie Barney's salon, the poet Renée Vivien, the young Colette on the stage. documented In this timeline La Fiancée des roses becomes one of that circle's books. It is the one modern novel they know in which two girls reach for each other's hands across a coffin lid, and in which the man is the villain without being the point. inference Copies passed hand to hand; "a reader of the Fiancée" became, for a generation of Parisians, a discreet way to describe a woman. choice The English version of 1911, Utena; or, The Rose Bride, by a translator who signed only her initials, carried the same reputation to London and New York. choice

The Demian question

In 1919 a German novel called Demian appeared under the pseudonym Emil Sinclair; its author, Hermann Hesse, was named only the next year. Its most quoted passage says that the bird fights its way out of the egg, that the egg is the world, and that whoever would be born must first destroy a world. documented In our history that passage is where the 1997 anime got its creed, as its director said. documented (makers' statement) In this timeline the creed was printed in Tokyo in 1885, and a German translation of Utena (from Gautier's French) had appeared in 1909. choice When Demian reached Japanese readers, they noticed. Whether Hesse, who was steeped in Asian thought, had read the German Utena is one of the minor scholarly quarrels of the twentieth century; his papers do not say, and both books were published under false names, which delights everyone who writes about it. choice

Butterfly

Pierre Loti's Madame Chrysanthème (1887), John Luther Long's story "Madame Butterfly" (1898) and Puccini's opera (1904) made the self-sacrificing Japanese woman who waits for her Western lover and dies of it the West's image of Japanese womanhood for a century. Loti took his own rented "wife", a young woman named Okane, in Nagasaki for a month in the summer of 1885, while Part One of the serial was running in Tokyo. documented Nothing in this timeline stops them being written. inference What changes is that they have a rival. From 1902 Western critics who write about Butterfly, more of them each year and most of them once the English version appears in 1911, have read, or have been told about, a Japanese novel in which the girl who looks like Butterfly, the smiling, obedient Anthy, turns out to be the most dangerous person in the book and walks out at the end. The stereotype is no weaker in the opera house. It is a little weaker outside it, especially among the women who read the Fiancée. inference

The other road: Tokyo's Chinese students

The modern Chinese word for revolution, geming, came back to China from Japanese, where kakumei had taken on the sense of the European word; Liang Qichao, who fled to Japan in 1898, took it up there, and a story told by Sun Yat-sen's associate Chen Shaobai, which some historians doubt, has Sun adopting it after a Kobe newspaper called his party "revolutionaries" in 1895; the Kobe paper that has been found calls him only the "ringleader of the Canton rioters". documented for the story and the doubt. Liang complained in 1902 that students back from Japan went about crying "geming, geming" because the Japanese used the word, and the same year called for "a revolution in the world of fiction", on the ground that to renew the people of a nation one must first renew its fiction. documented He was writing in Yokohama, a few streets from the Bluff. His essay names no Japanese novel. In this timeline the Chinese students who read it in Tokyo know that he means the political novels he had been translating since 1898, Kajin no kigū first among them, and add the one about the girls' school on the hill to his list themselves. choice

By the end of 1905 there were about eight thousand Chinese students in Japan. documented Among them was Qiu Jin, a poet from Shaoxing who had left her husband to study in Tokyo, at the Practical Girls' School of Shimoda Utako, the same educator who ran the Peeresses' School. In Tokyo she wore Western men's clothes, and at a meeting of students from her province she drew a Japanese sword from her boot and drove it into the lectern. She went home in 1906, edited a women's journal and planned a rising, and was beheaded at Shaoxing on 15 July 1907. documented

In this timeline the Chinese women students in Tokyo read Utena in Japanese and, from 1906, in a Chinese translation printed in Tokyo, whose title is simply 少女革命: in Chinese, unambiguously, "A Girls' Revolution". choice Qiu Jin's own unfinished verse-novel Stones of the Jingwei Bird, in which five rich girls flee arranged marriages for Tokyo to study and make a revolution, is a work of our history, written in the women's ballad form of tanci, whose most famous heroine, Meng Lijun, had fled a forced marriage in men's clothes and risen to chief minister. documented In this one critics read its opening chapters as a Chinese answer to Utena's last: girls who leave the house, and know where they are going. choice The Qing authorities ban the Chinese Utena in 1907, after her execution, and its readers keep it the way the schoolgirls of Tokyo had. choice When women's units formed in the revolution of 1911, as they did in our history, one in Shanghai took a white rose for its badge. documented for the women's units; choice for the rose. In the 1940s Shanghai's all-female Yue opera companies, which had trained girls to play men since 1923, put a Chinese Utena on the stage. documented for Yue opera; choice for the play.

Seoul and Taipei

Korean women who studied in Tokyo after 1910 brought back the vocabulary of the "new woman" and were punished for living it. The painter and writer Na Hye-seok, a student in Tokyo from 1913 to 1918 who admired Hiratsuka Raichō and Ibsen's Nora, held the first solo exhibition of oil paintings by a woman in Korea, in Seoul in March 1921, was divorced in 1930, published a frank "confession" of her divorce in 1934, and died poor in 1948. documented In this timeline they also brought back Utena, read in Japanese, since Korean translations were not permitted by the colonial censors until the 1920s and then only in an expurgated form. choice For Korean readers under colonial rule, a story about a school run by an absent authority through sealed letters, in which those who resist are expelled and forgotten, was not difficult to read. inference Taiwanese girls in the colonial girls' high schools read it in Japanese as part of the same curriculum of reading that colonial schools did not set and could not stop. inference
