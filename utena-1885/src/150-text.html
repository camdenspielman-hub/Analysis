XVIII

The text and its editions

Nine states of one book, and why no two readers born a generation apart have read the same Utena

Like many Meiji serials, Utena exists in several versions: a newspaper text, part-volumes, a book, a collected edition, censored and restored printings. Unlike most, its differences are about the things the story is about.

Part XXI gives sources for the practices.

The blank frame

The withheld picture of chapter 33 is the most famous image in Japanese illustration that nobody has seen. The newspaper's practice when a cut was refused was to print the frame and a line of apology, and that is what readers got on the morning the inn scene ended: a ruled rectangle the size of every other day's picture with four characters in it. choice Over the next century the empty frame became a motif in its own right. It was printed on fans in the 1890s, drawn into girls' magazine covers in the 1920s, reproduced as a full-page blank in avant-garde magazines of the 1960s, and hung as an empty white panel in the 2016 retrospective at the Yokohama Museum of Art, where visitors queued to stand in front of it. choice In the anime's version of this scene the camera spends nearly two minutes looking straight down at Utena's face, and never shows the person she is talking to. The serial's empty frame does the same thing by another means.

The two names of Juri

Arisugawa was the name of one of the four princely houses of the imperial family. Its head in 1885, Prince Arisugawa Taruhito, had commanded the imperial army that marched on Edo in 1868, and had once been engaged to Princess Kazu, who was married to the shogun instead. documented To put that name on a girl who fences, loves another girl and keeps her picture in a locket was either very careless or very pointed. The newspaper text printed it without comment. When the collected edition went to the censors in 1888, the publisher changed one character: 有栖川 became 有須川, with the same sound. choice For fifty-eight years Juri was "Arisugawa" in every reader's mouth and something else on the page, and the substitution is itself a small classic of Meiji censorship: a name nobody could say had changed, and nobody could say had not. The Arisugawa house died out in July 1913 with Prince Takehito. documented The restored text of 1946 put the original characters back. choice

The two readings of the title

The newspaper glossed 少女 as をとめ, otome. Both readings of the characters were centuries old; in Meiji print otome was the poetic choice and shōjo the plain one, and the plain one became the usual gloss only with the girls' magazines that began in 1902, though the word had begun to name a social type (the schoolgirl) in 1895, when the boys' magazine Shōnen sekai opened a girls' column headed 少女. documented Meiji readers said "Otome Kakumei". Their granddaughters, reading the 1920s paperbacks without ruby, said "Shōjo Kakumei". inference A few people in Japan still use the first reading, and saying "Otome Kakumei" now marks a speaker as a scholar or as the grandchild of a Meiji reader. choice
