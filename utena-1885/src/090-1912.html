IX

Chronicle, 1912–1945

The public domain, the revue, the vote that passed, and the war that buried it

In the Taishō years Utena stopped being a banned book and became a common one: out of copyright, everywhere, on stage, on film and in the cheapest sets of books in Japan. Its readers grew old enough to sit in the Diet's galleries and lobby the Peers. For one decade it looked as if this timeline's Japan would give women a share of power fifteen years before ours did. Then the army took the country to war, and the book's title was censored, one word at a time.

1912–1922A common book

1913–1916. Seitō changes its rules in October 1913 from "the development of women's literature" to "the awakening of women", is banned for an issue in 1915 over an essay on abortion, and under its last editor, Itō Noe, argues in public about chastity, abortion and licensed prostitution until it stops in February 1916. documented In this timeline its contributors quote Utena as their grandmothers quoted the Classics, by chapter number. choice

1 April 1914. The girls' singing troupe that Kobayashi Ichizō had formed in July 1913 to sell tickets on his Minoo-Arima Electric Railway gives its first performance at the line's hot-spring resort in Takarazuka, in a theatre made from an indoor swimming pool. The bill is a children's opera of Momotarō, a comic piece and a dance; the run lasts two months, and some 1,100 people a day see it free with their bath. documented Kobayashi's company was a business idea before it was anything else, and in this timeline he knows exactly which story every girl in the Kansai already loves. Its first full-length Utena, an operetta in three acts, opens in 1919, the year the company becomes the Takarazuka Girls' Opera and founds its music school. documented for the renaming and the school; choice for the operetta. In our history the girls played male parts from the first night, when Takamine Taeko was Momotarō; the Western men's dress of the later male-role star came with the move into the Grand Theatre in 1924, and the revue followed in 1927. documented In this one the girl who plays a prince, played by a girl, is the company's signature five years earlier, and has had a name since 1885. choice

1916–1924. Yoshiya Nobuko's Flower Tales, begun in 1916 as a reader's submission to Shōjo gahō and planned as seven stories, grow by readers' demand to more than fifty stories of girls' romantic friendships and make her the favourite writer of Japan's schoolgirls; her 1919 novel of two young women in a hostel attic depicts love between women openly. In January 1923 she meets the mathematics teacher Monma Chiyo, who becomes her partner for life; in 1957, adoption being the only legal tie open to them, Yoshiya adopts her. documented In this timeline Yoshiya is the greatest of the "Anthy story" writers, and the most open: her girls do not only leave home looking for someone, they find her. choice

1918. In July, fishermen's wives in Toyama start the rice riots that spread across the country and bring down a cabinet. documented Nothing links them to the book. The same summer the film theorist Kaeriyama Norimasa, whose 1917 book on the art of film drama called for screenplays, intertitles and actresses instead of female impersonators, shoots The Glow of Life with a young woman, Hanayagi Harumi, in the lead; the narrators who spoke over silent films in Japanese cinemas, whom his reforms would have made unnecessary, delay its release until September 1919, and Hanayagi is remembered as Japan's first film actress. documented In this timeline Kaeriyama's second experiment that summer is a two-reel duel scene from Utena, with women in every part, and the narrators cannot keep it off the screens, because every person in the audience already knows the story and does not need them. It opens in November 1918. choice Women had appeared in Japanese films before, a women's-kabuki actress among them as early as 1908; but the first actress cast on purpose, as a reform, in place of a female impersonator is therefore an Utena, ten months earlier than in our history. documented for 1908; inference for the rest.

1 January 1919. The last of Shun'yōdō's thirty years ran out at the end of 1918, and the whole of Utena, Part Four and the revised text included, now belongs to everyone. documented for the rule (Copyright Act of 1899, arts. 5, 8, 9 and 47); choice for the registration that set the clock. Within two years there are eleven editions in print, from cloth-bound sets to paper-covered booklets sold at railway kiosks for ten sen. choice

1920–1922. Hiratsuka Raichō, Ichikawa Fusae and Oku Mumeo found the New Women's Association (announced in November 1919, inaugurated at the Ueno Seiyōken on 28 March 1920) to amend Article 5. A revision passes the lower house in 1921 and dies in the Peers after a speech by Baron Fujimura Yoshirō. In February 1922 Sakamoto Makoto and Oku Mumeo call on the baron at his house in Nakano and win him over, and on 25 March, the last night of the session, the Peers pass the amendment at 11:50 p.m.: women may now attend and organise political meetings, though they still may not join parties. documented In this timeline the 1921 revision passes the Peers at the first attempt, after a member reads out the creed of the chick and the egg in the chamber and two dozen peers' wives watch from the gallery. choice The ban on party membership stays, as it did. inference

1923–1931The decade of the vote

1 September 1923. The Great Kantō Earthquake and the fires after it destroy most of Tokyo and Yokohama, the Bluff included, and kill more than a hundred thousand people. documented The fire takes the Home Ministry's censorship records too, so that nothing survives of the files on the serial's suspension. documented for the loss of the records; inference for what they held. The Tokyo Asahi's building burns, and the serial's manuscripts with it (Part XIV). choice On 16 September army police murder the anarchist Ōsugi Sakae, his partner Itō Noe and his six-year-old nephew. documented

1925. On 29 March the Diet passes the law giving the vote to all men of twenty-five, and Ichikawa Fusae writes in her diary that she will remember the day as the one on which women's political rights were taken away. On 22 April the Peace Preservation Law makes it a crime to organise against the "national polity" or private property. documented The word "revolution" becomes, in practice, a police word. inference

1926–1927. The "one-yen book" boom. In November 1926 the near-bankrupt publisher Kaizōsha offers a sixty-three-volume set of modern Japanese literature by subscription at one yen a volume, about two per cent of a university graduate's first month's pay, and 230,000 people subscribe; its first volume is Ozaki Kōyō. Other houses follow, Shun'yōdō with a sixty-volume set of Meiji and Taishō literature from June 1927. documented In this timeline Shun'yōdō's set opens with the book it had published in 1887, and its Utena volume outsells every other in the set. choice A generation of young women who had never seen the serial reads it now, in standard type without ruby, as Shōjo Kakumei.

1930–1931. The women's suffrage movement reaches its high point. In May 1930 a members' bill to give women civic rights in local government passes the lower house and is shelved by the Peers. In February 1931 the Minseitō cabinet brings in its own limited bill: women of twenty-five may vote in city, town and village elections, and may hold local office only with their husband's consent. It passes the lower house; the House of Peers rejects it. After 1933 no women's suffrage bill is even tabled. documented

In this timeline the Peers pass it. choice This is the largest single divergence in the document, so the case for it in full:

  • The real bill had the government behind it, passed the lower house, and failed in a chamber of hereditary and appointed men who were persuadable: the same chamber had killed the Article 5 revision in 1921 and passed it in 1922, after two women talked one baron round. documented
  • The pressure in this timeline is heavier. The women lobbying the Peers in March 1931 are the daughters of the Peers' own households, schooled in the same girls' schools for forty years; many of the Peers' wives had been "engaged" in a dormitory rose. inference
  • The bill's opponents had relied on the argument that Japanese women did not want the vote. In a country whose best-loved novel ended with a young woman walking out of her brother's house and through the gate on her own, that argument was harder to make with a straight face. inference

The bill passes the Peers by a narrow margin in March 1931, with the husband's-consent clause removed in conference. choice The same session, riding the same majority, takes up the outlines for reforming the family law that the government's own advisory council had drawn up in 1925 and 1927 and that, in our history, were never enacted, and adds to them by amendment the abolition of the wife's legal incapacity: "the Rose Bride articles" of 1898. documented for the council's outlines and their fate; choice for the rest. Japanese wives in this timeline become legally capable adults in 1931, sixteen years before they did in ours (1947). inference

18 September 1931. Officers of the Kwantung Army blow up a stretch of railway near Mukden and begin the conquest of Manchuria. documented Nothing in this history prevents it. inference From here on, every divergence is fought against the current.

1932–1945The shell closes

1932–1933. Kawashima Yoshiko, a Manchu princess raised in Japan who had cut her hair and taken to men's clothes after a suicide attempt at seventeen, becomes a celebrity as "the beauty in men's dress": Muramatsu Shōfū's novel based on her runs in Fujin kōron and is a hit by 1933, and in February 1933 the Asahi reports her appointment to command a militia in Jehol. documented In this timeline the papers call her "the Utena of Manchuria", and the book's most dangerous image, the girl who becomes a prince, is put to work for the empire. choice She was shot as a traitor in Beijing in 1948. documented

1933. Women vote and stand in city, town and village elections for the first time since a few women household heads voted in a ward of Kōchi and a neighbouring village in 1880–84. documented for Kōchi. Some 300 are elected across Japan, most to village assemblies, a handful to city councils. choice In June the women of the Shōchiku revue strike against wage cuts and dismissals under the eighteen-year-old Mizunoe Takiko, the short-haired star of men's parts whose fan club had some 20,000 members; about 230 of them barricade themselves in an inn at the Yugawara hot springs, their Osaka colleagues occupy a temple on Mount Kōya, and the strike ends with concessions on pay and days off. documented In this timeline their banners carry the last line of the creed. choice

1935. A revue star, Saijō Eriko, and a cross-dressed heiress, Masuda Fumiko, attempt a love-suicide together; the press reports it as a lesbian elopement and casts the heiress as a woman who betrayed her class's duty to be a good wife and wise mother. documented In this timeline the panic is louder and more political, because its target is not only the revue but the most popular novel in the country, and because the women it accuses now vote. inference The Home Ministry presses the revues to cut their cross-dressed roles; the Takarazuka Utena is withdrawn in 1936. choice (In our history Mizunoe recalled that by about 1940 the authorities had forbidden her to appear in men's clothes at all. documented, from her memoir.)

1937. War with China. A reprint of the novel is permitted only if the word "revolution" is removed throughout, title included (Part XVI, document 11). choice Removed, not blanked. For two decades publishers had blanked dangerous words with circles and crosses to avoid bans, "socialism" and "anarchism" even in fiction; but the censors distrusted the blanks as an invitation to guess, and in the autumn of 1936 a national conference of the thought police decided to stamp them out. By the summer of 1937 they had almost vanished from print. documented From 1937 the book is sold, when it is sold at all, as Shōjo Utena, "Girl Utena", with nothing on the cover to show that a word is gone, and its readers, who know perfectly well which word it is, write it back in by hand. choice

1939–1940. The illustrator Nakahara Jun'ichi, whose big-eyed girls fill the girls' magazine Shōjo no tomo, is driven off its pages in 1940 by military pressure, his girls called decadent and unhealthy. documented In this timeline the last pre-war edition of Utena, in 1939, has his illustrations, and his Anthy, all eyes, is the image of her the post-war decades inherit. choice In our history Nakahara's girls' illustration of the 1950s is one of the acknowledged sources of the 1997 anime's look. documented (makers' statement)

1940. The political parties dissolve into the Imperial Rule Assistance Association; the Women's Suffrage League dissolves itself on 21 September; and in October the Takarazuka Girls' Opera becomes the Takarazuka Opera, the word "girls" (shōjo) struck from its name, and every member is enrolled in the National Defence Women's Association. documented In this timeline the censor's last instruction on the novel, in December, removes its other word too, and for the rest of the war the most popular book in Japan is called simply Utena. choice Local assemblies are not abolished, but the women in them are swept into the national women's organisations and their committees for savings, rationing and send-offs to the front. inference

1942. The women's organisations are merged into the Greater Japan Women's Association, to which every woman except the unmarried under twenty must belong; by March 1944 it counts 21.49 million members. documented In this timeline a few women councillors resign rather than serve on its local boards, and their names are remembered. Most do not. choice

1944–1945. The novel goes out of print, partly for want of paper and partly because no publisher will apply. choice Takarazuka's Grand Theatre gives its last performance on 4 March 1944, to crowds that police hold back with drawn swords, and becomes a naval barracks; the Tokyo Takarazuka Theatre has closed three days earlier. documented On 15 August 1945 the emperor broadcasts the surrender. documented
