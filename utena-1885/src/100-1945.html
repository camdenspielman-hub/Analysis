Chronicle, 1945–1989

The Occupation, the classroom, the manga, and the mountain that moved

After 1945 Utena stopped being contested and became compulsory. The Occupation's reforms gave Japanese women in this timeline back what they had briefly held, and more; the new schools put the last chapter in their textbooks; the girls' comics that grew out of the war's ruins borrowed its princes and its roses by the cartload. By the 1980s it was a classic in the full, deadening sense, and the women's movement had to rediscover it, as every movement rediscovers the classics it was taught.

1945–1952Restoration, not a gift

October–December 1945. On 10 October the Shidehara cabinet decides to give women the vote; the next day MacArthur's list of five great reforms includes it. The Public Peace Police Law is abolished on 21 November, and on 17 December the revised election law gives the vote to every man and woman of twenty. documented In our history the only Japanese women who had ever voted were a few household heads in two wards of Kōchi, from 1880 until the government took the right away in 1884. documented In this one women had voted in local elections from 1933 until the war swallowed local politics, and some hundreds had sat in assemblies. choice The difference matters less in 1945 than later. For the next eighty years conservatives in our history could describe women's suffrage, with the rest of the post-war settlement, as something the Americans had imposed. In this timeline that argument never works, because everyone's grandmother had voted for the village assembly under the old constitution. inference

10 April 1946. The first general election with women voting. In our history 79 women stand and 39 are elected, 8.4 per cent of the house; about 13.8 million women vote, 67 per cent of those eligible. documented In this timeline 112 women stand, many of them former town and village councillors with their own local organisations, and 57 are elected. choice In the 1947 election the number of women in the lower house falls in both timelines, as the novelty fades and the parties reassert themselves: to 15 in ours, to 31 in this one. documented for 15; choice for 31.

February–October 1946. In the Government Section of MacArthur's headquarters a twenty-two-year-old civilian who had grown up in Tokyo, Beate Sirota, drafts the articles on women's rights for the new constitution; most of her text is struck out, but Article 24, which says that marriage shall rest only on the mutual consent of both sexes and that family law shall be written for "individual dignity and the essential equality of the sexes", survives from her draft. documented In this timeline the article reads the same, word for word. choice What changes is how it is received: in the Diet's debate on the constitution that summer, a woman member elected in April reads out the last page of chapter 39, the Bride walking out of the gate, and says that this is what Article 24 is for. choice She is an invented person; the article's history in our timeline is not.

1946–1948. The Occupation's censors ban 236 Japanese films as militarist on a single day in November 1945, forbid sword-fighting films as "feudal", and keep the great revenge play Chūshingura off the stage until 1947; a censor viewing a 1946 romantic comedy objects that it has no kiss and orders one added. documented In this timeline a studio's 1946 film of Utena is at first refused as a sword-fighting picture. The producers answer that it is the story of a girl who refuses an arranged engagement and wrecks a feudal institution to free another girl from it, and the censors, after some discussion, approve it, on condition that the duels be shortened. choice It is released in 1947, the first sword-fighting film made in Japan after the war. choice

22 April 1946. Takarazuka's Grand Theatre reopens with Carmen; its Tokyo theatre stays requisitioned, as the US Eighth Army's Ernie Pyle Theatre, closed to Japanese audiences, until 1955. documented In this timeline the company's Utena, withdrawn in 1936, returns that autumn, and becomes the piece every new male-role star must play once. choice

1 January 1948. The revised Civil Code takes effect and the household system is abolished: no head of house, no single heir, marriage by the consent of the two parties alone. documented In this timeline the wife's incapacity had gone in 1931. The rest of the household goes now, as in ours. inference

March 1947. Ichikawa Fusae, the suffragist, is purged by the Occupation for her wartime place on the board of a patriotic writers' association, and stays purged until 1950. documented It happens in this timeline too; a movement that had won something before the war had also collaborated more, and was purged more. inference

1950–1969The classic

1950s. The post-war reform of the writing system, with its new kana spelling and reduced character list, makes most Meiji fiction hard going for the young, and the publishers reissue the classics they can sell in modern spelling. documented for the reform. Utena is among the first, and from 1952 the senior high school Japanese textbooks of several publishers carry its first and last chapters, as they carry Sōseki and Ōgai. choice Every Japanese teenager from now on reads the gate scene in class, usually in the spring of the second year, and writes an essay on where Anthy is going. inference The textbook makes the book safe. Teachers learn to present it as a story of friendship and the triumph of individual conscience over feudal custom, which is the Occupation's reading, and to skip chapters 31 and 32. inference

January 1953 – January 1956. Tezuka Osamu's Princess Knight runs in Shōjo Club: a princess born, by an angel's mischief, with both a boy's heart and a girl's, raised as a prince because only men may inherit the throne. Tezuka had grown up in Takarazuka, next door to the revue's leading male-role star of the 1930s, Amatsu Otome, and modelled Sapphire on an actress of women's roles he had seen play a man. documented In this timeline the strip exists as in ours. choice It is read as Utena's granddaughter by way of the revue, and its premise, a girl barred from the throne by a succession law that had been written partly against her grandmother's favourite novel, has an edge that our history's version lacks. inference

24 May 1956. The Anti-Prostitution Law is enacted, its penalties taking effect in April 1958. documented As Part XIII says, nothing in this history touches it. inference

1950s–1960s. In our history women hold fewer than a dozen seats in the lower house through these decades, two per cent of it or less. In this timeline they hold between twenty-two and thirty, five or six per cent: still a parliament of men, but one in which every party has some women who have run local governments, and in which the women of the left and the right sometimes vote together on the household budget, schools and food safety. choice The Liberal Democratic Party, formed in 1955, governs in both timelines. documented for ours.

1970–1989The roses and the lilies

21 October 1970. Japan's first women-only demonstration; the group around Tanaka Mitsu hands out a hand-written leaflet called "Liberation from the Toilet", arguing that men had split women into mothers and sexual conveniences. The newspaper word for the movement, ūman ribu, had first appeared in the Asahi that month. documented In this timeline the marchers carry white roses, and the newspapers, which had spent a generation calling the book a harmless classic, rediscover that it is not. choice The ribu argument about the book is with the textbook reading: Anthy was not freed by a friend's conscience but by herself, and only after the friend had been destroyed by the institution that the textbooks praised as "school life". inference

1971. Two firsts. A small group of women in Tokyo founds the Wakakusa no Kai, Japan's first lesbian circle, which meets monthly in an apartment and over five years has some five hundred members. documented In this timeline there had been such circles since the 1920s, disguised as alumnae poetry clubs with rose names; the Wakakusa no Kai is the first to say what it is. choice And in July a small Tokyo publisher, Itō Bungaku, launches Japan's first commercial magazine for gay men. In our history he called it Barazoku, "the rose tribe", and "rose" became the Japanese code word for men's love, with "lily" following in the 1970s as its counterpart for women. documented In this timeline the rose has meant love between girls for eighty years, and his magazine is called Yurizoku, "the lily tribe", instead. choice Every flower in Japanese slang is the other way round, and so are a great many shop signs. inference

1972–1976. Ikeda Riyoko's The Rose of Versailles runs in Weekly Margaret from 1972 to 1973; Takarazuka stages it in August 1974, directed by the film star Hasegawa Kazuo, who had been trained as a kabuki player of women's roles, and well over a million people see it in its first three years. documented In this timeline it is the second great rose of Takarazuka, and its Oscar, the girl raised as a man who commands the queen's guard, is the most admired descendant of Utena, in a French uniform. inference The women manga artists born around 1949, the "Year 24 Group", who remade the girls' comic in these years, had read Utena in their textbooks and their mothers' editions. inference

1985–1986. Japan ratifies the UN convention on discrimination against women and passes an Equal Employment Opportunity Law that obliges employers only to "endeavour" not to discriminate in hiring and promotion. documented On 8 September 1986 Doi Takako becomes chair of the Japan Socialist Party, the first woman to lead a major Japanese party, in this timeline as in ours. documented

1987–1988. The first lesbian office in Tokyo opens, and a book of testimony from 234 women is published; in 1988 the writer Kakefuda Hiroko becomes the first woman to come out as a lesbian in the national media. documented In this timeline both are reported, as everything about women's love is reported in Japan, with a picture of a rose. inference

7 January 1989. The Shōwa emperor dies. documented On 23 July the Socialists, led by Doi, win the upper-house election on anger at the new consumption tax and a bribery scandal, the LDP loses its majority in that house, and so many new women are elected that the papers call it the "Madonna boom". Doi sums it up in four words: "The mountain has moved". She is quoting the first line of the first issue of Seitō, Yosano Akiko's "The day the mountains move is coming", seventy-eight years later. documented She says the same words in this timeline. choice It is the one place in this history where the two timelines meet in the same sentence, and it is a quotation from a poem, not from a novel.
