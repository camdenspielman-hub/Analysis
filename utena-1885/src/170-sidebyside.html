The two timelines side by side

Where they part, where they meet again, and by how much

Most of the dates in this document are the same in both timelines. The serial does not move wars, cabinets or the throne. It moves a handful of things that concern women, girls and the people who love them, by a few years to a few decades, and almost always in the same direction. The chart shows ten of those milestones; the table after it runs through the whole period year by year.

Read down the right-hand column and a pattern shows. The serial's effects cluster at three moments: the late 1880s, when it was news; the 1910s to the early 1930s, when the girls who had grown up on it were adults with votes to lobby for and stages to fill; and the present, when more than seventy years in the classroom have changed what a majority thinks is normal. Between those moments the two timelines run almost together, and at every point where the state used force, in 1887, 1890, 1925, 1937 and 1940, it did the same in both, and in this one struck at the book as well: one more name on the expulsion list of 1887, a word cut from its title in 1937 and another in 1940. inference That is this document's argument about what a book can do, in one table.

The table leaves out culture, because there the pattern is different. What Japanese people read, watch, sing, draw and say does not change at three moments but in every decade, from the sugoroku of 1886 to the comics of the 1970s, and Parts XIV and XV follow it. inference
