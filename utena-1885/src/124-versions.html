XIV

Versions and riffs

Continuations, parodies, plays, films and games: a hundred and forty years of other people's Utenas

Nobody in this timeline has read the same Utena twice, and most people have never read it at all. They have seen it, heard it, sung it, played it as a board game at New Year and argued about the ending of a version the author never wrote. The book was rewritten from its first autumn, and in a hundred and forty years almost every part of it has been changed by somebody, except the duel.

How Meiji treated a hit

The yardstick is what happened to the hits of our own history. When the poisoner Takahashi Oden was beheaded in January 1879, the small papers ran her story the next day, two rival picture-books raced each other into print, and Kawatake Mokuami's kabuki of her opened at the Shintomi-za four months after the execution, with two more illustrated books of the play on sale the same month. Ozaki Kōyō's Konjiki yasha reached the stage fifteen months after its serial began, with four years of the novel still to come; its most famous line, the vow to cloud next year's moon with tears, is the stage's tidy version of a speech Kōyō wrote as a stammer, and the frontispiece's leather shoes became, in a statue of 1985, the geta of the popular memory. Tokutomi Roka's Hototogisu collected an entire shelf of what its scholars call "Hototogisu things": a prequel of the lovers before their marriage, side-stories about each of them, "true story" exposés of the family behind it, knock-off sequels and a sequel to one of the knock-offs, songbooks, a naniwabushi text with the singing marks printed, biwa ballads, children's versions and a comic-strip version of 1928. Konjiki yasha was filmed twenty-two times by 1937, and its heroine was played on screen in 1918 by a man, the onnagata Kinugasa Teinosuke, who played Hototogisu's heroine the next year. documented (Part XXI)

The law did nothing to stop it. Until 1899 a novel had no performance right at all: the ordinance of 1887 protected only scripts and scores whose authors had published them marked "performance rights reserved", and the Copyright Act of 1899 gave the author of a novel the right to translate it but no express right to dramatise or adapt it; its drafter thought an adaptation earned no copyright of its own. When the kamishibai men of the 1930s told Ōgon Bat, each teller's was different, because the scripts were never written down and nobody in the trade thought of copyright. documented A serial larger than any of these, whose author would not give a name and so could not object, and which was entirely out of copyright from 1919 (Part IX), was going to be remade by everyone who could hold a brush, a sword or a camera. inference The "Utena things" (ウテナ物) of this timeline fill a bibliography of more than four thousand entries, compiled since 1951 by a society of collectors in Yokohama and still growing. choice

Before the ending, 1885–1887

Continuations. In the eight weeks between Parts One and Two, three rival small papers printed continuations, unsigned, in which Utena won Anthy back from Tōga in a single installment; one ran for three weeks (Part IV). In the six months before Part Four they were sold as books, and by the spring of 1887 there were at least nine endings on sale, among them a marriage of Utena and Akio, a death of Anthy, a convent and a steamer to France. choice

The first parody. In the spring of 1886 a Tokyo comic weekly in the manner of Punch ran Revolutionary Boy Tenjō, in which a schoolboy dresses as a princess to win back his bride from a council of girls, who settle everything by sewing contests. choice The comic papers already ran parody serials of other people's genres: Kibi dango, the sister magazine of the satirical weekly Marumaru chinbun, had serialised a mock life of a "poison woman" in 1879. documented It is the first of the turned-around Utenas, of which there are now hundreds, and its central joke, that a boy in a dress was funnier than a girl in a jacket, told 1886 something it did not want to know about itself. inference

Games and pictures. For the New Year of 1886 a Tokyo print publisher issued an Utena sugoroku, a race game printed on one sheet, which starts at the academy gate and ends, for the winner, in the castle; a throw of six on the square of the lift sends a player straight to the top, and the square for chapter 11 sends everyone back to the beginning. A set of karuta cards with the creed split across the reading cards followed the next year. choice Picture sugoroku tied to a novel were an Edo habit (one for a version of Hakkenden survives from 1852), and in 1890 a boys' magazine gave away a sugoroku of student types as its year-end supplement. documented The Utena sugoroku has been reprinted, redrawn or reinvented nearly every New Year since. choice

The first stage. The Rose Bride of the Bluff, by a women's company at a Yokohama theatre in November 1885 (Part XIX, document 4), invented a scene the book does not have: Anthy, alone on the stage after the duel, takes off her spectacles and looks at the audience. Every stage version for the next twenty years kept it. choice

The Meiji stage and the storytellers, 1887–1912

Kabuki, 1887. The Shintomi-za's Utena (Part VI), staged with the publisher's leave, as the playbills of the day credited publishers, gave the book its first new ending: Anthy, walking out of the gate, finds Utena asleep in the forest beyond it, and the curtain falls on the two of them under a rain of paper roses. choice The kabuki stage had always preferred a reunion to an absence, and audiences who had read the book cried at both. inference The reunion has been the commonest ending of Utena ever since. More people in this timeline have seen Utena and Anthy reunited at the gate than have read the book in which they are not. choice

The first sequel, 1888. Utena, the Later Tale (後のウテナ), anonymous, in three paper-covered volumes, sends Anthy and Chu-Chu across Japan in search of Utena and ends with them finding her in a mission hospital in Nagasaki, without her memory, being nursed by Juri. choice It was a great success and was followed by a sequel to itself, The Later Tale Continued (1889), in which the memory comes back. choice The pattern is the one Hototogisu's knock-offs followed twenty years later in our history: a sequel to a knock-off. documented for the Hototogisu titles.

A ghost story, 1889. San'yūtei Enchō, the greatest storyteller of the age, had spent the late 1880s turning French and English novels, recited to him by educated friends, into Japanese tales for the yose, and his ghost stories were best-sellers in shorthand transcript. documented In this timeline, in the winter of 1888–89, he tells a ghost story of his own about a burned seminar hall: no duellists, no Utena, only a young professor who trades a hundred boys for a teacup with a woman's rouge on it, and a sister who keeps roses in sugar because she cannot bear to watch them die. Enchō's version answers the question the book will not: the professor lights the fire with his own hands, and his ghost walks the ruins every year on the night of the fire, counting to a hundred. choice That is Enchō's answer, not the book's, and it is the one most Japanese children know, because the kamishibai men of the 1930s borrowed it. choice

Gidayū, 1889. The duel in the forest garden, newly written for the chanters, was the girl chanter Takemoto Ayanosuke's most requested piece (Part VII). choice

A villain's life, 1891. A kōdan storyteller's True Record of Ōtori Akio, taken down in shorthand and sold in eight parts, gives Akio the childhood the book never does: a gifted boy, a prince to every child who saw him, adopted into the Ōtori house, and ruined by a sister's sacrifice he could not forgive. choice It was the first version to make Akio the hero of his own story, and the first to be accused of excusing him. Both kinds of version have been written ever since. inference

Shinpa, 1894. The new-school theatre staged Utena more often than any other novel of the 1880s (Part VI), and in its own way. Its most successful version, The Witch of the Rose (1894), makes Anthy a "poison woman" in the tradition of Oden, who has bewitched her brother and destroys the innocent Utena, and ends with Anthy's death by her own hand in the gateway. choice Shinpa audiences, who in our history would weep a few years later for the faithful Namiko of Hototogisu, wept for Utena and hissed Anthy every night for a decade. choice Women readers of the book hated it, and said so in letters to the women's magazines; it is the version against which the "Anthy story" of the 1900s was written (Part XV). choice

Women on stage, 1893–1908. The Misaki-za's all-woman Utena of 1893, with Ichikawa Kumehachi as Akio (Part VII), kept the book's ending and nobody else did; Kawakami Sadayakko's Paris fragment of 1900 (Part VIII) ended on the duel; the Imperial Actresses' Training Institute played the ball (Part VII). choice

Small change. In 1907, as the last volume of Natsume Sōseki's I Am a Cat appeared and its imitators began (I Am a Mouse that year, I Am a Silkworm the next, I Am a Frock Coat in 1910), a hack in Tokyo published I Am Chu-Chu, in which Anthy's small animal narrates the whole book with comments on the food. documented for the cat and its imitators; choice for the mouse of the southern seas. By 1912 there were also a prequel, The Prince in His Youth; a side-story for every member of the council; an exposé, The Truth of Ōtori Academy, which claimed to name the real school and named three; a New Utena set in a Kyoto girls' high school; songbooks of new-style verse; and an English crib for students. choice

Free for all, 1912–1945

Film. After the two-reel duel of 1918 (Part IX) came a full-length Utena from Nikkatsu in 1920, with the onnagata Kinugasa Teinosuke, who had played Konjiki yasha's heroine in 1918 and Hototogisu's in 1919, as Anthy. documented for Kinugasa's earlier roles; choice for the third. It was the last time a man played her on screen; the reform that the Utena of 1918 had begun finished with her. choice By 1940 there had been at least twenty-six Utena films: fewer than the sixty Chūshingura films made by 1926, the story the studios reached for whenever they were short of a hit, and more than the twenty-two Konjiki yasha films of our history by 1937. documented for Chūshingura and Konjiki yasha; choice for the count. Some were faithful, most were not, and three of them changed the book for good:

  • The car, 1926. A silent remake from Shōchiku's Kamata studio set the story in the present and gave Akio a motorcar in place of the carriage. It ends with Utena killed when the car goes off the coast road and Anthy walking away from the wreck. Critics hated the car; audiences came to see it. choice Every later modern-dress version has a car in it, and so, in 1997, did the anime (Part XI). inference
  • The sword-fighting Utena, 1930. In the year an independent producer turned Konjiki yasha into a sword-fighting film, another made Utena a wandering swordswoman of the Edo period who fights her way into a castle to free a captive princess. documented for the Konjiki yasha film; choice for the Utena. It has no Akio, no council and no school, and its heroine wins. It was the most profitable Utena film before the war. choice
  • The talkie, 1932. The first sound Utena used the book's dialogue, and audiences heard the creed spoken for the first time by actors instead of chanted by students. Its Anthy, whose low, polite voice was the first thing every review mentioned, is the voice most Japanese still hear when they read the book. choice

Rensageki. In 1915 the chain-drama houses, where live actors alternated with filmed scenes on a screen lowered in front of the stage, played an Utena in which the duels were filmed in a real forest and everything else was played live. The form lasted only until the police regulations of 1916–17 ended it. documented for rensageki; choice for the play.

The revue. The Takarazuka operetta of 1919 (Part IX) gave the reunion ending its music: the two girls meet beyond the gate and sing. In 1927, the year the company staged its first revue, the last scene became a descent of the whole company in white down a great staircase, with Utena and Anthy at the bottom, and it has stayed so. choice In Asakusa, in the same years, the light-opera houses played a parody, Utena of Asakusa, in which the council are rival opera troupes and the Rose Bride a soprano everyone is trying to hire; the opera fans who sang mocking words to the Carmen prelude had new words for the creed. documented for the Asakusa parodies and fans; choice for this one.

Records and radio. A naniwabushi Utena was recorded in 1912, and benshi narrations of the films were sold on disc through the 1920s, as they were for Hototogisu and Konjiki yasha. documented for those records; choice for Utena's. Radio broadcasting began in 1925, and by 1934 the broadcaster's drama department had serialised the whole book in thirty-nine weekly episodes, with a chorus for the duels. documented for 1925; choice for the serial.

The Rose Knight, 1933. Street kamishibai, which in 1930–31 had invented Ōgon Bat, a golden-skulled hero born in the last episode of a villain's serial, made the most changed Utena of all. The Rose Knight (薔薇の騎士) has a girl in a black jacket who fights the Demon King of the Night Carriage, a cloaked figure with no face, with a sword that comes out of a rose; Anthy is a princess in a cage who is rescued in every episode and captured again in the next. documented for Ōgon Bat; choice for the Rose Knight. Each teller's was different (Part XIX, document 12, imitates a patter sheet). A generation of Tokyo children knew Utena as a superhero before they knew she was a girl in a book. inference

The national-policy Utena, 1939. The last pre-war film, released under the censored title Shōjo Utena (Part IX), turned the duels into kendō matches at a girls' school, made Akio a foreign agent posing as a benefactor, and ended with Utena, the swordswoman of her school, leaving for the front in China as an army nurse while Anthy, freed, marries a naval officer. choice It is the only version in which every character ends where the state wanted them, and it is the version critics have in mind when they say that Utena can be made to say anything (Part XIII). inference

The classic and its children, 1945–1989

The Occupation film, 1947. The first post-war film (Part X) ends with Utena and Anthy leaving the academy together, through the gate, while the police take Akio away in the back of a jeep. choice It is the most cheerful ending the book has ever had, and the one the textbooks quietly assumed for the next twenty years. inference

Tezuka's Utena, 1953–1956. Tezuka Osamu, who adapted Faust in 1950 and Crime and Punishment in 1953, ending the second with the Russian Revolution breaking out, and who had grown up in Takarazuka next door to a star of its male roles, drew an Utena for the girls' magazine Shōjo Club from January 1953 to January 1956, in the slot where in our history he drew Princess Knight. documented for Tezuka's adaptations, his childhood and the slot; choice for the manga. It is Tezuka's own book: a mischievous angel gives the infant Utena a boy's heart as well as a girl's, which explains, as the book never does, why she wants to be a prince; the castle is a real castle; Akio is a usurping regent; the duels are fought by a girl with two hearts against a court of boys; and it ends at the gate, with both girls riding out on the white horse. choice That is Tezuka's answer to the book's question, not the book's. Princess Knight was never drawn in this timeline. Its premise, a girl raised as a prince because only a man may inherit, went into the Tezuka Utena as the angel's mischief, and its heroine Sapphire does not exist. choice Mushi Production animated Tezuka's version for television in 1967–68, the years in which it animated Princess Knight in ours. choice

The underground, 1968–1971. The comics for grown-ups of the late 1960s produced a hard-boiled Akio (1968), told from inside the carriage, and the theatre produced the most important stage Utena since 1893. choice Terayama Shūji, whose troupe Tenjō Sajiki had cast a man as the heroine of The Hairy Mary in 1967, and whose composer from 1969 was J.A. Seazer, staged Utena, or the Bride in the Coffin in Tokyo in 1970: the council as masked chorus, the audience seated in the duelling garden, and the duels sung by a rock chorus with Seazer's music. documented for Terayama, the troupe and Seazer; choice for the production. Its ending, in which the actress playing Utena walks out through the audience into the street and does not come back, was the one the student movement remembered. choice The choruses Seazer wrote for it stayed in his repertoire, and in 1997 some of them came back to Utena (Part XI). choice

Takarazuka, 1974–1975. In our history the revue's great boom of the 1970s came from The Rose of Versailles, staged in August 1974 under the direction of the film star Hasegawa Kazuo and seen by well over a million people in three years. documented In this timeline the boom comes from a new two-part Utena, directed by Hasegawa in 1974 and 1975, which for the first time stages Parts Three and Four in full, carriage and all, on the revue's stage. choice Why there is no Rose of Versailles on that stage is a question for Part XV.

The fans, 1975 onward. At the first Comic Market, in December 1975, among a few dozen circles selling their own magazines, several sold Utena parodies, and Utena circles have been a fixture of every one since. documented for the first Comic Market; choice for the circles (Part XIX, document 15, imitates a flyer). The fan parodies did what no publisher would: they paired Utena with Anthy in plain words, paired Tōga with Saionji, paired Juri with everyone, and made Akio a joke. choice

Play. A home video game of 1987, Utena: Gate of the Rose, made the player Utena and the duels into a fencing game; its bad ending, in which Utena becomes the Rose Bride, was found by nearly every player first. choice

The faithful one, and after, 1989–2026

The 1997 television series (Part XI) was faithful in a way no screen version had been for eighty years: it kept Parts Three and Four whole, the carriage (as a car), the inn, the siblings and the ending, and dropped every reunion, angel and jeep that adapters had added since 1887. choice That was its shock. Viewers who had known Utena from Takarazuka, Tezuka and the textbooks discovered in the autumn of 1997 what the book actually does to Utena in chapter 38, and the broadcaster received more complaints about the last six episodes than about anything it had shown that year. choice

The director's film of 1999, Adolescence of Utena, is in this timeline one more free version, and one of the oldest kind: it begins again from Utena's arrival, redraws everyone, and at its climax turns Utena into a car, which Anthy drives out of the academy. documented for the film. Since 1926 every modern Utena had had a car in it; the 1999 film is the first in which Utena is the car, and critics who knew the history called it the end of a seventy-year-old joke. choice

Since 2000: a light-novel sequel series in which Anthy, grown up, runs a tea shop on the coast road and waits; two "2.5-dimensional" stage musicals, after the manner of the ones our history staged in 2018 and 2019; a picture book for small children, with no Akio in it; three novels told by Akio, each attacked as an apology; a retelling by Anthy, which won a literary prize in 2011 and is taught beside the book in some schools; an isekai parody in which an office worker wakes up as the Rose Bride's cowbell; and, in 2025, a streamed live-action series in twelve parts. documented for our history's musicals; choice for the rest.

What changes. The ending, first: most versions bring the two girls together or kill one of them, and very few leave them, as the book does, apart and looking. Akio, second: he has been a demon, a spy, a usurper, a tragic hero, a car and a joke, and only the book and its few faithful versions let him be what he is, a charming man nobody stops. The brother and sister, third: stage, screen and textbook have cut, softened or explained Akio and Anthy more often than any other thing in the book, from the kabuki of 1887, which made them cousins, to the national-policy film, which made them strangers. choice

What never changes. The duel: no version, from the comic weekly of 1886 to the cowbell isekai, has left out a sword drawn from a girl. Nanami: every version has her, and every version makes her funny. And Chu-Chu, who has been a monkey, a mouse, a kitten, a kangaroo, a dragon and a car's mascot, and is in all of them. choice
