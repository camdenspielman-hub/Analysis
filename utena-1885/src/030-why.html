II

Why 1885

The earliest year the story can be told whole, and the last in which it can still change the laws

1885 is the first year in which every piece of Utena's furniture exists in Japan or in Japanese print, and the year before the state began, law by law, to fix the shape of the modern Japanese family. A story about breaking the shell of the world lands while the shell is still being made.

Japanese names and a Japanese setting were a preference of the request, and they rule out most of the world. Within Japan I weighed six moments. The table compares them on the five things the premise needs: a medium that can carry thirty-nine episodes with pictures; a world that can hold the series' Western furniture without magic; names that read naturally; a censor strong enough to make the book dangerous but not strong enough to erase it; and decisions still ahead of it that a book could tip.

An earlier sketch of this premise set the story in Tenpō Edo, as a gōkan. The medium fits beautifully: the gōkan was the most picture-led serial form in the world before the twentieth century, illustrated on every page, with characters drawn in the likeness of kabuki actors, the nearest thing pre-modern Japan had to an animated cast. documented But the story's furniture is Western, and the Edo telling would have had to invent a European academy, a European prince, a European witch and a rose garden out of nothing. In Meiji Japan those things were arriving by the shipload, and the Edo version would have spent its energy explaining what 1885 could simply show. inference

The medium threshold

By 1885 Tokyo had two kinds of newspaper. The "large papers" (ōshinbun) were written in dense literary Japanese for officials and political men. The "small papers" (koshinbun) printed every character with its reading beside it, ran crime, scandal and theatre news, and from the late 1870s carried tsuzukimono, serial stories in daily installments, each with a woodblock illustration. documented The small papers were read aloud in households, barbershops and bathhouses, so their audience was larger than their circulation and included many people, women above all, who could not read a large paper. documented (Part XVIII)

A daily installment with one picture is the closest thing to an animated episode that 1885 possessed. Every morning a reader got a scene, a cut and a cliffhanger, and a serial's chapter of five to eight installments ran about a week, the same rhythm as a weekly television episode. Serials were then reissued as illustrated volumes and, if they were popular, staged. documented A girl who reads Utena in 1885 hears it read aloud at breakfast, buys the picture of the duel as a single sheet, sees the women's troupe play it at a Yokohama theatre, and reads it again in a book. That is the life of a television series, rebuilt from what 1885 actually had. inference

The Western threshold

Every piece of the anime's furniture has an 1885 equivalent that a reader had seen or read about:

  • The academy: the mission schools on Yokohama's Bluff, Ferris above all, founded in 1870 and on the Bluff since 1875, with Western buildings, dormitories, pianos and English teachers; the Peeresses' School opened in Tokyo that year. documented
  • The ball: the Rokumeikan, the government's hall for Western-style balls, open since November 1883. documented
  • The duels: Western fencing taught to army officers; Japanese swordsmanship in police training and public exhibitions after the 1876 ban on wearing swords, a ban on wearing them, not on owning them. documented
  • The car: the private carriages of ministers and peers, the most visible luxury in Tokyo. documented
  • The lift, the phonograph, the telephone: a freight hoist at a Tokyo paper mill since 1875, and a mine-shaft cage in the Iwakura Mission's published record; a phonograph demonstrated in Tokyo in 1878 and shown to the public in 1879; about two hundred telephones in government offices and none in private hands. documented
  • The planetarium: the orrery, which is what English called a "planetarium" until the projector was invented in the 1920s, and the magic lantern, which the Ministry of Education had been sending to normal schools since 1880 with sets of astronomical slides, and which one of its photographers had been showing free to Tokyo crowds since 1883. documented
  • The roses, the prince, the witch, Lucifer: Western roses in Yokohama gardens; Aesop and the Arabian Nights in Japanese since 1875, the first books of Grimm from 1887; the New Testament complete in Japanese in 1880. documented

None of this was ordinary. All of it was known, which is what a fantasy needs: the reader must be able to picture the conservatory, the carriage and the lift without ever having been inside one. inference

The legal hinge

Between 1884 and 1900 Japan wrote down, for the first time, who a woman was in law. In 1884 it created a modern peerage, with princes. In 1889 the Imperial House Law restricted the throne to men, the first such rule in Japanese history, which had had eight reigning empresses. In 1890 a law on assemblies barred women from political meetings and associations, and the Imperial Rescript on Education set out the family morality every school would recite. A fight over the Civil Code from 1889 to 1892 ended with the liberal draft shelved, and the code of 1898 made the household head the master of the family and the wife a legal incompetent. In 1899 the state organised girls' secondary schools around the ideal of the "good wife and wise mother", and in 1900 it reaffirmed the ban on women in politics. documented (dates in Part VI)

A serial that began in 1885 and finished in 1887 reached its readers before every one of these decisions. A girl of fourteen who cried at chapter 39 in 1887 was twenty-five when the family law of 1898 came into force. That is the leverage the premise needs. Earlier, there is no newspaper; later, the law is written. inference

The censor

The press law of the 1880s let the Home Ministry suspend a newspaper or seize an issue for disturbing public order or public morals, and it did so constantly; editors went to prison by the dozen. Books had to be submitted before publication. In December 1887 a new ordinance expelled hundreds of activists from the capital overnight. documented (Part XVIII) That is enough to make Utena dangerous, to cut it and to stop its serialization, and not enough to destroy it. Meiji Japan banned many books; it did not have the means or the will to pulp a best-seller that had already been printed in tens of thousands of copies. inference

The reach

The 1880s and 1890s were the high point of Western Japonisme. Japanese prints filled Paris, Japanese plays toured Europe and America by 1900, and a Japanese book with pictures could cross into French or English within a decade. In the 1900s Tokyo also became the place where young Chinese, and later Korean, women and men came to study modern ideas, and they read what Tokyo read. documented A serial of 1885 has both routes open, and Part VIII follows them.

What 1885 costs the scenario

Honesty requires the downsides. In 1885 about a third of Japanese girls of school age were enrolled in elementary school, and girls' secondary schooling barely existed outside the mission schools. documented There was no "girls' market" for publishers to sell to; Utena has to build one. The small newspapers' buyers were mostly men, and the serial's first audience is a household's, read aloud by a father or an elder brother. The paper that runs it is a party organ without a party, in political trouble from the start. The story's third part, with an adult man's courtship of a girl of fourteen and an incestuous brother, is more dangerous in 1886 than in 1997, not less. Each of these is a friction, and each, worked through, produces history rather than preventing it.
