XIII

The critics

A hundred and forty years of arguing about what the book is, from a Monday column of 1885 to the stylometrists

Every generation of critics in this timeline has had to decide first what kind of thing Utena is, and has decided differently: a gesaku with ideas above its station, a romance of pure love, a fairy tale for schoolgirls, a parable of capital, a hymn to sacrifice, a lesson in democracy, a feminist manifesto, a text about texts, a lesbian classic, a book about abuse. Each of those readings is still held by somebody, and each of them can quote the book.

The critics who speak in this Part are invented, with their essays and their sentences, and each is placed among the real critics, schools and magazines of their decade, whose documented ideas are the frames inside which they argue. No real critic's words are applied to the book. Where a real critic appears, it is with something they really wrote, about something else. choice throughout, except where marked.

The Monday columnist, 1885–1888

The first critic of Utena is the one who disliked it longest. Suimutei, the anonymous columnist of a rival small paper in Kyōbashi, reviewed it nearly every Monday from April 1885 to the end of the run (Part IV), and in 1888 a small Tokyo house collected eighty-three of his columns as Notes on the Rose (薔薇随筆), the first book about the book. choice It is still the most quoted. Suimutei never solved the serial and never pretended to; what he did was notice. He was the first to say in print that the faces in the cuts are erased on purpose, that the comic chapters and the others "were not written by the same pen, or not on the same day", that the creed is a riddle and not a slogan, and, on the book's last chapter, that "the author has given us a heroine who disappears and a bride who walks, and asks us which of them is the story". choice Who he was is unknown. A note in his book says that he was a former samurai of a northern domain, a claim that fits half the journalists in Tokyo. choice

The literary men of the 1880s read the serial in the terms Tsubouchi Shōyō's Essence of the Novel had just given them: the heart of the novel is human feeling, "manners and customs come next", and fiction must be rescued from being the "plaything of women and children". documented Utena was, embarrassingly, all three: true in its feelings, exact in its customs, and read above all by women and children. The verdict that stuck was the one Part V records, true in its people and false in its castles, and a reviewer of the 1888 collected edition put it in the form everyone repeated: "Take away the castle and you have a novel. Take away the novel and you have a castle." choice

Love and the bride, 1890–1905

In February 1892 Kitamura Tōkoku opened an essay in Jogaku zasshi with the sentence "Love is the secret key of human life", argued that "marriage makes a person worldly", and shocked a generation of young intellectuals. documented His followers, the romantic critics of the 1890s, found in Utena what Tōkoku's essay described and no Japanese novel had shown: a love that marriage never touches. The fullest statement was Love Without a Husband (1894), by Kōno Seiu, a young poet who published it in a coterie magazine of his own: the love between Utena and Anthy, he argued, is the purest in Japanese literature because it has no future, no household and no name, and the book is right to end it at a coffin. choice It is a beautiful essay and, as later critics pointed out, it needed the girls to be apart.

The women's answer came four years later, from Aoyagi Fusa, a graduate of a Yokohama mission school who taught in one. Her essay On the Bride (花嫁論, 1898), printed in a Christian women's monthly in the month the Civil Code's books on the family took effect, read Anthy as the wife the new code had just created: legally incapable, transferred from owner to owner, addressing each in the form the household required, and smiling. choice It was Aoyagi who called the code's articles on wives "the Rose Bride articles" (Part VII), and Aoyagi who first argued that the book's ending is not a love story but a legal one: in a world where a wife can do nothing on her own account, the one thing Anthy can do is leave. choice She also took on the shinpa stage, whose Witch of the Rose (Part XIV) had made Anthy a poisoner: "They have made her a witch," she wrote, "because they could not bear to make her a wife." choice Aoyagi married in 1902 and published nothing more. Her essay was reprinted in 1971 by the women's liberation movement and is now in every anthology. choice

The naturalists' fairy tale, 1906–1920

Naturalism, as Shimamura Hōgetsu and Hasegawa Tenkei preached it from 1906 and Tayama Katai's Futon practised it in 1907, wanted literature to lay reality bare, and came to mean the frank confession of the author's own life. documented To the naturalists Utena was everything they were against: anonymous, allegorical, crowded with marvels, and popular. The most quoted dismissal is the critic Sakaki Tōsen's, in 1909: "a fairy tale for schoolgirls, which schoolgirls are right to love and men are wrong to discuss". choice Sakaki made one exception, and it became famous: the only chapter of the book that a naturalist could respect, he said, was chapter 8, in which a cooking class goes wrong and two girls wake up in each other's bodies, "because it is the only one in which the author admits that nothing is happening". choice The joke was on him. By 1920 the comic chapters had their own defenders, who argued that they were the book's realism and the duels its dream, and the naturalist verdict survived mainly as the thing every later critic began by refuting. inference

The commodity and the gate, 1920–1945

The Marxist critics of the 1920s inverted the Meiji verdict. When Aono Suekichi argued in 1926 that the spontaneous expression of the oppressed must be raised to purposive class consciousness, and Kurahara Korehito in 1928 called for a proletarian realism with the party's point of view at its centre, they gave younger critics a method for reading anything, a girls' novel included. documented Its best application to Utena is Oda Rintarō's The Rose Bride as Commodity (1929), in a proletarian monthly: Anthy is a thing exchanged among owners; the duel is the market; End of the World, who issues orders and is never seen, is capital; Akio, who projects a castle everyone works for, is ideology; and Utena, the girl who tries to win the commodity's freedom by beating its owners at their own game, is the petty bourgeoisie, heroic and doomed. choice Oda's conclusion, that the castle is the truth of the book and the feelings of its people the projection, was the one Part V mentions, and it lasted. choice So did his last line, which read Anthy's walk out of the gate as "the first strike in Japanese literature", and annoyed historians of the real one at Kōfu ever after. choice Oda was arrested in 1933 and recanted in 1934, and wrote nothing about the book again.

The most dangerous reading came next. Yasuda Yojūrō and the Japan Romantic School, from 1935, sang what the dictionaries call an "aesthetics of defeat", and drew young readers away from both Marxism and the realist novel toward a lofty, ironic love of beautiful failure and the neglected classics. documented In that air the critic Hasebe Sōun, who never joined the school and is always counted with it, published The Gate (門, 1938): Utena's fall at the Rose Gate is the most beautiful death in modern Japanese literature, because it is useless; the coffin is a lotus seat; the hand that reaches for her is the nation's; and Anthy's walk out of the gate, which spoils all this, is either cut from his account or, in one notorious footnote, called "a desertion". choice The essay was read by students who went to the war, and some of them wrote about it in letters home. choice It is the reading that the national-policy film of 1939 (Part XIV) put on the screen, and the reason the book's post-war critics have always been nervous about its coffin. inference Hasebe served on the criticism section of the Patriotic Association for Japanese Literature, which existed from 1942, and was purged from public life after the war, as Yasuda was in 1948. documented for the association and Yasuda's purge; choice for Hasebe.

Two other things happened to the book's criticism in these years. The Home Ministry's instruction to publishers of children's books in October 1938, which told them to avoid depictions of romantic love, ended open writing about the girls' culture that had grown from the book (Part XV). documented for the instruction. And the women who had read it as girls in the 1890s were now writing their memoirs, and the memoirs, more than any critic's essay, recorded what the book had been to them. choice

Whose castle? 1945–1970

In 1946 Maruyama Masao described Japanese ultranationalism as a "system of irresponsibility", in which authority flowed from a centre that nobody occupied and every order came from somewhere else. documented Three years later Nozaki Hiroshi, a young political scientist, applied the idea to the book in an essay whose title became a classroom question, Whose Castle? (1949): the letters signed End of the World, which everyone obeys and nobody admits to sending, are the system of irresponsibility exactly, and Akio, who turns out to be End of the World and says he only shows people what they want to see, is the man at its empty centre. choice Nozaki's reading and the textbooks' (Part X), which taught the book from 1952 as the triumph of individual conscience over feudal custom, were the two halves of the post-war Utena: one said what the castle was, the other who had fought it. inference

The 1960s added doubts. When Etō Jun argued in 1967 that maturity in Japanese fiction requires the collapse of the mother, the critic Miura Ryō answered, in an essay on the book called A School Without Mothers (1968), that Utena had been there first: it has no mothers at all, only a fiancée's mother who calls a young man her prince, and its children are therefore never allowed to grow up, which is the point. documented for Etō; choice for Miura. And when Yoshimoto Takaaki called the state a "communal illusion" in 1968, the students who read him read the castle as one, and painted it on the barricades at two universities. documented for Yoshimoto; choice for the rest.

Anthy left, 1970–1989

The women's liberation movement found the textbook Utena intolerable (Part X), and its answer was a pamphlet. Anthy Left (アンシーは出て行った, 1972), by Saeki Kazue, a high-school teacher who had taught the book for ten years, mimeographed and sold at the movement's meetings, argues that the textbooks had the wrong heroine. Utena, the girl who wants to be a prince and save a princess, is the fantasy; Anthy, who is saved by nobody and leaves, is the woman; and the book's whole action is the destruction of the fantasy so that the woman can walk out. choice Saeki's last page, which asks every girl who has written the essay on where Anthy is going to write instead about where she herself is going, was read aloud at the movement's meetings for a decade. choice

The scholars came next. Maeda Ai's The Formation of the Modern Reader (1973) taught Japanese literary studies to ask who read a book and how, and argued that the readers of the 1880s were still reading aloud, in company. documented His student generation took the method to the serial's letters columns. Tachibana Mie's The Readers of 1885 (1979) reconstructed, installment by installment, what the paper's readers wrote, who they were and what they argued about, from the letters column, Suimutei's book and the editor's memoir; it is the source of everything Part IV records about how each chapter came out. choice Its most quoted finding is that from chapter 7 onward more of the letters came from women than from men, and that the women wrote longer. choice The same year a word-frequency study found at least three hands in the text (Part XVII). choice

Two real books of these years have, in this timeline, the same titles and the same subjects as in ours, and readers of Utena claim both. Komashaku Kimi's The Logic of the Witch (1978), which re-read Sōseki and Genji as a feminist and was called a bible of women's liberation, never mentions Utena, and every reader assumes the witch is Anthy. documented for the book; choice for the readers. And when Sata Ineko challenged, in 1985, the long-standing reading that the change in Midori at the end of Takekurabe was her first menstruation, and Maeda Ai and others answered, the quarrel over what had happened to a girl offstage in a classic was one that readers of Utena had been having about chapter 31 for a century. documented for the Takekurabe debate. The "sofa debate" (ソファ論争) of 1986, between a novelist who said that every reader knows what Nanami saw and a scholar who said that the whole force of the chapter is that no reader does, ended, like the Takekurabe one, with both sides keeping their supporters. choice The book has not settled it either.

Then theory. In 1983 Asada Akira's Structure and Power, inviting the young to "a gradual sideways slide into knowledge", started the boom a newspaper named the New Academism. documented Kuga Shin's The Projected Castle (1984) is the book's contribution to it: the castle is an image with no original, the lantern the first postmodern machine in Japanese fiction, and the book a text that knows it is projected; Anthy's walk out of the academy is the sideways slide, out of a story that had no outside. choice Kuga's book sold surprisingly well, and is now mostly remembered for its cover, which is blank. choice

Roses and lilies, 1990–2026

The criticism of the last thirty-five years has mostly been about what earlier critics would not name. inference Mizuhara Aki's The Rose Was Ours (1995) is the history of a century of reading Utena as a story of love between women: the dormitory roses of the 1890s, the alumnae poetry clubs, the flower that means women here and men in our history (Part X), and the critics who looked away. Its argument, that the book's love between girls was always the open secret its readers kept for one another, is now the common reading. choice The yuri magazines that began in the 2000s in our history began in this one too, and their critics argue about whether Utena is the first yuri story or the thing yuri was invented to escape. documented for the magazines; choice for the argument.

After 2004, when the law first let people in Japan change their registered sex, critics began to ask what Utena's jacket is. documented for the law. The question is old (Meiji readers called her henjō nanshi, Part V), but the answers are new: a girl who wants to be a prince, a prince the world calls a girl, or someone the book's own language has no word for. The book says she is a girl and says she wants to be a prince, and critics have built on both statements. choice

Since about 2010 the most-read criticism has been about Akio. Read in an age that has learned the word grooming, the chapters the textbooks skipped (Part X) are a precise account of how a charming adult isolates and uses two girls, and of how a whole institution helps him; several critics have argued that the book is the first in Japanese to show it from inside, without either excusing it or making it lurid. choice Others have written about Anthy's skin, which the colour prints of the 1880s made a deep red-brown (Part III), and about what it meant for the most frightening woman in Japanese fiction to be drawn darker than everyone else. choice

The tools changed too. The 2002 critical edition (Part XVIII), a digitised run of the newspaper with its letters columns, and the stylometric studies of 1998 and 2017 (Part XVII) have made Utena one of the most studied texts in Japanese digital humanities. In our history the method was tried on Genji's Uji chapters in 1957, and in 2018 it found Kawabata's girls' novel Hana nikki to be a co-authorship with Nakazato Tsuneko. documented In this timeline those studies were done too, after the method had been tested on Utena. choice

Abroad

Foreign criticism began with the French, who read Judith Gautier's abridged Fiancée des roses of 1902 as Japonisme and a little as a book for women (Part VIII). The first complete English translation was Ruth Penhallow's, in 1962, by an American who had taught English at a Tokyo girls' school before the war; her introduction, which compared the book to Alice, Jane Eyre and Revelation in that order, sent a generation of American students of Japanese to it. choice The English-language scholarship that in our history wrote about Takarazuka, the "S" relationships of girls' schools and the girls' magazines (Jennifer Robertson in 1998, Deborah Shamoon in 2012, Hiromi Dollase in 2019) writes in this timeline about the same subjects, and in each case about the book behind them. documented for the books; choice for their subject.

The standing arguments

Six questions have divided the book's critics in every decade, and none is closed. choice

Who failed at the gate?
Utena, because she tried to be a prince (Hasebe, admiringly; Saeki, gladly); the prince, because there was none (Kuga); the reader, who wanted one (Mizuhara).
Victim or witch?
The shinpa stage and the play inside chapter 34 say witch; Aoyagi, Saeki and most critics since say a woman made into one; a minority, citing the blade in chapter 38, says the book refuses to choose, and that is the point.
Is the book in love with Akio?
The readers of 1886 were, and said so (Part IV). Critics since have asked whether the book's charm is his, and whether a reader who enjoys the carriage chapters is being groomed with Utena.
What are the comic chapters for?
Filler (Sakaki), relief, the book's realism (the 1920s), or its other argument: that Nanami, who never fights for the Bride, is the only character who changes without anyone's help.
Does the castle exist?
Yes, as a slide (chapter 38); no, as anything else (Kuga); yes, as everything the characters do for it (Oda); and in the essays of a great many schoolchildren, yes, and Utena is in it.
Where is Anthy going?
The question every senior high school student in this timeline has written on since 1952 (Part X). The book says she is looking for Utena. Saeki's answer, "out", is the most quoted.
