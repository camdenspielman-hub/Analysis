XV

The culture it made

Words, genres and stock figures that exist only here, and the famous things that, because of it, came out differently or not at all

A classic does not only inspire. It takes up room. Once a story is in every classroom, its shapes become clichés, its words become slang, and the young artists who might have invented its kind of thing for themselves either rework it or run from it. This Part follows both: what Utena added to Japanese culture, and what it displaced.

Two rules govern what follows. A real work made after 1885 still exists in this timeline unless the serial gives its maker a strong reason to make something else (Part XVI); and where the reason is strong, the work changes, and is described here and labelled, never quoted. choice

Words

The serial's vocabulary has been in this timeline's dictionaries since the 1890s. Some of its words are given in earlier Parts: 学園 for a school (Part III), the Duellist and the Utena law (Part VI), Bride and Prince in the dormitory (Part VII), "Let's just go home" (Part IV). The rest:

アンシーする, to "do an Anthy"
To walk out, with nowhere in particular to go: of a marriage first, from about 1905, then of a job, a family or a political party. In the 1920s the papers' divorce reports used it of wives who left without a settlement; in the 1980s it was what a woman did when she left a company that expected her to resign on marriage. choice
To "graduate" (卒業)
From Akio's order in chapter 23 (Part IV): to be dropped by someone who has used you. Schools still graduate their pupils, but the word has an edge: in our history a Japanese pop group says a departing member has "graduated", and in this one no agency would dare. documented for our history's usage; choice for the rest.
An Akio
A charming older man who preys on girls and is protected by the institution he runs. Women's magazines warned against "the Akio type" from the 1920s; since the 2010s the word has been used in reporting on abuse in schools and sports clubs. choice
A Nanami
A vain, absurd, devoted younger sister; by extension, anyone who is the comic relief in her own tragedy and survives it. Usually affectionate. choice
From the End of the World (世界の果てから)
Of an order that nobody admits to giving. Office slang since the 1950s, helped by Nozaki's essay (Part XIII): a memo "from the End of the World" is one whose author cannot be found. choice
Ten-years tea (十年後の茶会)
From chapter 37: a class's reunion ten years after leaving school, held by girls' schools from 1897 and now by schools of every kind. The tea is mandatory; the laughing is the point. choice
Rose (薔薇)
Love between women, since the 1890s (Part VII). Lily, in consequence, is love between men (Part X).

Genres that exist only here

The Anthy story (アンシー物), 1902–1930s. A girl leaves home with a trunk and a small animal to look for someone she has lost (Part VII). It is the first plot of the Japanese girls' novel, printed by the hundred in the girls' magazines, and its greatest writer is Yoshiya Nobuko, whose Flower Tales (1916–24) exist in this timeline too, as stories of girls who look for each other and, increasingly, find each other (Part IX). choice The convention it set lasted into the girls' comics of the 1970s. In our history the love stories between girls drawn in those years almost all ended in separation or death, which their critics have blamed on everything from patriarchy to the end of the girls'-school culture. documented In this timeline they end, as often as not, with one girl walking out of a gate to look for the other, and the genre's critics call these "gate endings". choice

The duellist novel (決闘小説), 1888–1904. The boys' answer to the girls' book: middle-school boys who fight secret duels for honour, a friend or a girl, in boarding houses and on riverbanks, under rules taken from the serial's. It boomed with the real duelling craze of 1888 (Part VI), was banned from school libraries after the Duel Act of 1889, sold all the better for it, and died when the war with Russia gave boys' fiction real battles to write about. choice Its last trace is in the school stories of the 1910s, in which a challenge is always issued by letter, sealed with a rose. choice

The carriage melodrama, 1890s–1920s. In the domestic novels that filled the newspapers from the late 1890s, whose heroines suffered for husbands, mothers-in-law and money, and which shinpa staged as fast as they appeared, this timeline adds one stock villain: the charming man with a carriage, later a car, who drives a girl out at night to show her something. documented for the domestic novel; choice for the villain. Readers called him "an Akio" before the novels did.

The Black Rose mystery, 1920s–. When Japanese detective fiction came into its own in the 1920s, with Edogawa Ranpo's first stories in 1923, it found a setting waiting for it: the school with a secret society beneath it. documented for Ranpo's debut. Stories of a seminar, a hall, a fire long ago and a lift that goes down are a recognised sub-genre in this timeline, called after Part Two, and they are still written. choice

The shadow-girl duo, 1930s–. When comic double acts in the new manzai style filled the variety halls of the 1930s, women's duos in schoolgirl costume, one sharp and one slow, performed in silhouette behind a lit screen and called themselves "shadow girls" after the serial's. documented for 1930s manzai; choice for the duos. The form passed to radio, then to television, and every Japanese comedian of this timeline knows the opening formula. choice

The prince play, 1890s–. Girls' schools' drama clubs have staged a play with a girl as the prince every year since the 1890s, at first in secret, then at the school festival; at many schools the part is chosen by the outgoing seniors and is the highest honour the school has. choice

Stock figures

By the 1950s the book's people had become types, and editors used their names as shorthand. choice The girl in boy's clothes who wants to be a prince is "an Utena", and by 1960 an Utena was the most familiar figure in Japanese girls' fiction, more a cliché than a heroine. The silent, smiling girl who belongs to somebody is "a Bride". The comic rival-sister is "a Nanami", the predator "an Akio". Every girls' story has a small animal that understands everything and eats too much, and the trade calls it "the Chu-Chu". And every school story has a letter from someone the characters never see. choice

What came out differently

This is where the book's weight shows, because a classic changes what the next generation can do with the same materials. inference

The revue's princes. Takarazuka's male-role players had their signature part from 1919 and their Western men's dress from the first Utena, five years before our history's (Part IX). choice In our history the revue's audience changed from mostly male to mostly female in the late 1930s, with the cropped-haired male-role star. documented In this timeline women were the audience from the first Utena, and the revue never had to be discovered by them. choice

No Princess Knight. Tezuka Osamu's Princess Knight (1953–56), one of the first story comics for girls in our history, is not drawn in this timeline. Tezuka, who in our history called the strip the sum of his Takarazuka experience, draws an Utena instead, and its premise, a girl raised as a prince because only a man may inherit, goes into his Utena as a mischievous angel's gift (Part XIV). documented for our history; choice for this one. The difference is that Tezuka's girl in armour, in this timeline, is somebody else's heroine, adapted, and Japanese girls' comics begin with a classic, not with an original. inference

No Oscar. In our history Ikeda Riyoko's The Rose of Versailles (1972–73) began as the story of Marie Antoinette, and its guards' commander became a woman raised as a man because, Ikeda has said, she thought a woman would be easier to draw freely; the character, at first a supporting one, took over the series, and the Takarazuka stage version of 1974 began the revue's greatest boom. documented In this timeline a woman raised as a man was, by 1972, the most familiar figure in girls' fiction, an Utena, and drawing one was the opposite of drawing freely. Ikeda keeps the commander a man, as he was in history, and her Rose of Versailles is the story of Antoinette and Fersen and the Revolution: a solid success and a long seller, but not a phenomenon, and Takarazuka does not stage it. choice The revue's boom of 1974 comes instead from its two-part Utena (Part XIV). choice Of all the changes in this Part, this is the one most open to argument, and the one that takes the most away: the most famous girl in a uniform in our history does not exist in this one.

The flight to the boys. The women artists born around 1949, the "Year 24 Group", remade girls' comics in the 1970s, and their great innovation in our history was the love story between boys, from Takemiya Keiko's In the Sunroom (1970) to Hagio Moto's The Heart of Thomas (1974) and Takemiya's Song of the Wind and the Trees (1976). Takemiya has said that drawing a man and a woman in bed could get an artist called in by the police, so she moved the subject to two boys; the friend who urged her on gave her Hesse's Demian among other books. documented In this timeline the same artists make the same move for one more reason. A love story between two girls was not new in 1970; it was in the textbooks, and their mothers had read it. Yamagishi Ryōko's Two in the White Room (1971), the story of two girls at a French boarding school that our history counts the archetype of the yuri story, is in this timeline one more Anthy story, well done. The one love story the classroom had not got to first was between boys, and the young artists went there. inference The works themselves are the same. choice What changes is what they meant: in our history a rebellion against the girls' comic; in this one a rebellion against the classic behind it, and against a teacher's reading of it. inference

Girls' schools in fiction. Kawabata Yasunari's Otome no minato (1937–38), a story of a love triangle between girls at a mission school in Yokohama, drafted by Nakazato Tsuneko and revised by Kawabata, exists in this timeline as in ours. documented Every reader in this one takes its school for Ōtori Academy, and the booksellers sold it as an Anthy story. choice

The uniform. The sailor suit came to girls' schools in 1920–21 and conquered them in both timelines. documented In this one it competed with the black jacket with gold buttons that several girls' high schools had adopted from the Utena jacket after 1899 (Part XII), and lost everywhere except a few dozen schools, whose girls still wear the jacket and are told every April which book it comes from. choice

Things nobody could have predicted

  • Chu-Chu. A tin toy in 1886, a celluloid doll in the 1920s, a mascot on everything from savings-bank money boxes to a railway's commuter pass in the 1980s. Its species is still argued about. choice
  • The New Year sugoroku. Printed, redrawn or reinvented almost every year since 1886 (Part XIV). Throwing a six on the lift square is, in some families, a reason to pour the first sake. choice
  • White roses at graduation. Girls' schools gave each leaver a white rose from the 1890s, and the custom spread to every school after the war; a red rose given to one particular friend is a different matter. choice
  • Girls named Utena. The name, a Buddhist word with no history as a girl's name (Part V), had two spikes: 1886–90, when registrars objected and parents insisted, and 1997–98, after the television series. choice
  • No red-violet at weddings. Japanese brides of this timeline, who began to wear Western white in numbers after the war, avoid one colour, the deep red-violet of the Rose Bride's gown, and dressmakers know not to suggest it. choice
  • Nemuro. The real city at the eastern tip of Hokkaido, which has no memorial hall, has received letters every year since 1886 asking for directions to it. choice
  • The rose tribe's room. In our history the gay magazine Barazoku opened a column for women readers in the 1970s called "The Lily Tribe's Room", and the word yuri followed. documented In this timeline the magazine is Yurizoku (Part X), and its women readers' column is "The Rose Tribe's Room". choice
