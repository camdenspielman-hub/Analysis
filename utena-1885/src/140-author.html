XIV

Who was Hōsūshi?

The authorship question, as the scholars of this timeline argue it in 2026

Nobody knows who wrote Otome Kakumei Utena. A hundred and forty years of argument have produced a short list of serious candidates, one strong piece of internal evidence, and a consensus that the question cannot be closed.

The name on the page

The byline 鳳雛子 carried the newspaper's reading ほうすうし, Hōsūshi: "Master Fledgling Phoenix". 鳳雛 is the sobriquet of Pang Tong, the strategist of the Three Kingdoms paired with Zhuge Liang as "the Crouching Dragon and the Fledgling Phoenix", whom the novel kills young at a place called Fallen Phoenix Slope. documented Every reader of 1885 knew the story. So the pen name says, at the least, "a young talent who expects to fall", and it holds the academy's own phoenix (鳳, ōtori) and the creed's unhatched chick (雛). choice

The same three characters can be read another way. 鳳 alone is Ōtori, and 雛子 is the girl's name Hinako. Read as a name, the byline is Ōtori Hinako, a girl who shares a surname with the academy. A reader's letter pointed this out in the paper's correspondence column on 19 June 1885 (Part XVI, document 2), and the newspaper never replied. Whether the ruby ほうすうし was the author's or a compositor's guess is unknowable, since the rest of the manuscript burned in 1923 and the surviving leaves carry no byline. A literatus's sobriquet on the surface, a girl's name underneath: the byline does what Utena's jacket does, and generations of readers have taken that as a hint. choice

What the paper knew

Very little, by its own account. The fullest testimony is the memoir of the paper's serial editor in 1885, published in 1909, which says that the first three chapters arrived by post in February 1885 with an unsigned note asking that no inquiry be made; that later chapters came in batches of two or three, posted in Yokohama and Tokyo; that fees went by postal money order to a different payee each time, collected at Yokohama's main post office; and that he never met, saw or identified the author. choice Postal money orders had existed in Japan since 1875, so the method was available to anyone. inference The law allowed the arrangement. The press law of 1875 had required writers to sign with their real names and punished pen names; its replacement of 1883 required only the paper's proprietor, editor and printer to be named, made them answer for what it printed, and made the author liable as an accomplice if found. documented A pseudonymous serial was legal so long as an editor would stand behind it, and this one did. The memoirist was writing twenty-four years later and had reasons to enjoy the mystery. Nobody else at the paper left an account. choice

The manuscripts stayed in the newspaper's files as it passed from Jiyū no Tomoshibi to Tomoshibi to Mezamashi to the Tokyo Asahi, and they burned with most of that paper's Tokyo archive in the fire after the earthquake of 1 September 1923. choice Twenty-three leaves survive because the serial editor had taken them home: pages of chapters 7, 22 and 39 as the post brought them, in a quick, practised hand with corrections in a second ink. They were sold in 1931 and are now in the Tsubouchi Memorial Theatre Museum at Waseda, a home whose irony every scholar of the question enjoys. choice

The evidence of the text

Whoever wrote it knew three worlds well. The first is Yokohama and the Bluff: the academy's harbour view, its Western buildings, the foreign words, the habits of a mission girls' school from inside (dormitory rules, the piano, the hierarchy of seniors), the phonograph and the lift. The lift, though, the author describes like a reader of the Iwakura Mission's published record rather than someone who had ridden one. The second is the old high culture: the fairy tale's frame is Western, but the narrator's asides, the punning headings and the Sino-Japanese of the creed belong to someone schooled in Chinese classics and Edo comic fiction. The third is the politics of 1884–85: the creed reads like a People's Rights handbill, and the letters from End of the World arrive with the gravity of imperial rescripts. inference

The text is also in two minds about Christianity, which it knows well. Lucifer, Revelation and the "sting of death" are used with exactness, and so is the Buddhist image hidden in the heroine's name: utena is the calyx of a flower and also the lotus throne on which the saved are reborn in the Pure Land, where to share one lotus seat (一蓮托生) once meant to share one fate after death. documented for the Buddhist usage. The serial's last chapter holds that image out and leaves it unsettled: two hands on opposite sides of a coffin lid, and no word on whether they share a fate. inference

The three hands

The strongest single finding came in 1979, when two statisticians, Ōta Keiko and Hayashi Satoshi, ran the full text through a word-frequency study on a university mainframe. They found not one author but at least three. choice

HandChaptersCharacter of the writing
A, "the principal hand"1–5, 7, 9, 13–15, 22–23, 25–26, 30, 33–34, 37–39 (20 chapters)The creed, the castle, the coffins, the Black Rose and its fire, Akio's three chapters of reckoning, the ending. Long sentences, heavy Sino-Japanese, every chapter that carries the design.
B, "the comic hand"6, 8, 10, 16, 18, 24, 27, 31, 32 (9)Nanami's chapters: the kangaroo, the curry, the cowbell, the egg, the diary; then, surprisingly, the two chapters in which she searches the family register and is left with something she cannot say. Short sentences, puns, Edo comic diction.
C, "the melancholy hand"17, 20, 21, 28, 29, 35, 36 (7)Shiori, Wakaba, Keiko, Ruka, Juri's broken locket, Tōga's confession. The most attentive to what women do to each other.

The study has been replicated twice, in 1998 and 2017 (the second time with machine-learning methods), and the A/B/C division has held each time. Its implication is that Utena was a planned work written by a small circle under one designer, who decided what every chapter must do and gave some chapters to other writers. That is how the ending of chapter 30 can be in hand A while chapters 31 and 32 are in hand B without the story losing its thread. The circle is usually called the "Hōsū group" (鳳雛社). Whether it included the designer's friends, students, a married couple or a teacher and her pupils is the whole of the modern debate. choice

The five hands map exactly onto the five people credited with the anime's scripts: Enokido Yōji (A), Yamaguchi Ryōta (B), Tsukimura Ryōe (C), Uemura Kazuhiro (D) and Igarashi Takuya (E), with Ikuhara Kunihiko, the director, and Enokido, who also did the series composition, between them in the designer's place. The scholars of this timeline cannot know that.

The candidates

Kishida Toshiko (1864–1901) the "Shōen hypothesis"

A former tutor to the empress, the most famous woman speaker of the People's Rights movement from 1882, arrested after her Ōtsu speech on "daughters in boxes" in October 1883, and married, in 1884 by most accounts, to the Liberal leader Nakajima Nobuyuki, who had governed Kanagawa prefecture in 1874–76; the marriage was registered in August 1885. documented Her essay to her "sisters" had run in ten installments in this very paper in May 1884, and she had known Miyazaki Muryū since 1881. documented She was baptised with her husband in 1886, taught Japanese and Chinese studies at a Tokyo mission girls' school from 1887, followed him into exile in Yokohama after the expulsions of December 1887, and from May 1888 was an honorary professor at Ferris. documented She knew the court, the party, the paper, the Kanagawa coast and the girls' schools from inside. Against her: her known prose is stiffly classical and humourless, and hand B could not be hers; her supporters reply that hand A could be, and that the comic chapters were given to someone else. This is the leading hypothesis in surveys of Japanese literary scholars, and has been since the 1950s. choice

Wakamatsu Shizuko (1864–1896) the "Ferris hypothesis"

Born in Aizu in 1864, four years before its siege, raised in Yokohama, one of the first graduates of the mission school on the Bluff, in 1882, and a teacher there, a Christian who read English as easily as Japanese and who would later translate Little Lord Fauntleroy as Shōkōshi, the little young lord. documented She had the school, the language, the fairy tales and the Aizu dead (whom chapter 22 seems to remember). Against her: she turned twenty-one the week the serial began and published almost nothing before 1886, and her later style is plainer than hand A. Her supporters make her the designer and the youngest member of a circle. choice

Miyazaki Muryū (1855–1889) the "house writer" hypothesis

A Tosa journalist and one of the Liberal press's leading writers of political fiction, who had adapted Dumas's French Revolution and written of the Russian nihilists and their women. documented His nihilist serial had just finished in this very paper, and its book was banned and he convicted while Utena was running. documented The hypothesis is that he wrote the next serial for the same paper and disguised himself to protect it, and the Kishida–Muryū "circle" is the commonest form of it, since the two had known each other since 1881. Against him: his known fiction has no comedy and no interest in girls as anything but martyrs, and he died in 1889 having never hinted at it. He is the favourite of political historians and nobody else. choice

Tōkai Sanshi (Shiba Shirō, 1853–1922) the "Aizu hypothesis"

An Aizu samurai's son whose mother and sisters died in the siege of 1868, back from Pennsylvania at the end of 1884, whose Chance Encounters with Beautiful Women began appearing in October 1885 and filled students' heads with beautiful women revolutionaries. documented The coincidence of year and subject made him a candidate in the 1890s. Against him: he was writing a very different book at the same time, in a very different style. The hypothesis survives mainly in combination: an Aizu circle (Shiba, Wakamatsu Shizuko and Ōyama Sutematsu) is a staple of popular histories. choice

Tsubouchi Shōyō (1859–1935) the "experiment" hypothesis

The young critic who was publishing The Essence of the Novel in parts from September 1885, arguing that fiction should depict human feeling as it is, and his own novel of student life under a pen name. documented The theory is that Utena was his experiment in feeling within fantasy, run anonymously so as not to compromise his critical position. Against him: his correspondence shows no trace of it, and his treatise never mentions it, though his followers judged it by his terms (Part V). That silence is, inevitably, offered as evidence for him. choice

Others

  • A former lady of the shogun's inner palace. The "Ōoku hypothesis" (first proposed in 1956): only someone who had lived in a closed world of women run by an absent man would write End of the World's letters so. No candidate has ever been named. choice
  • Ōyama Sutematsu or Tsuda Umeko, the American-educated women who had sailed with the Iwakura Mission as girls in 1871 and come home in 1882. Both had largely lost their Japanese (Tsuda had left at six), which disposes of the theory and keeps it popular. documented for their language difficulties; choice for the theory.
  • The illustrator (Part III), on the ground that he knew the paper and the story better than anyone; no more. choice
  • Higuchi Ichiyō, who was twelve. A modern novelist's conceit that every few years someone takes seriously. choice
  • The Empress Haruko, a 1960s pulp theory built on her poetry and her patronage of girls' schools. Nobody takes it seriously. choice

The cancelled ending

One leaf of chapter 39 has been read, under infrared since 1988, as a cancelled ending in which it is Anthy who vanishes and Utena who walks out of the academy alone. A single line, struck through, ends with Utena at the gate and the gate closing behind her. choice If the reading is right, the author changed the story's end at the last stage, and chose the more surprising of two endings: the heroine disappears and the Rose Bride leaves. Scholars disagree about what this means for the authorship question. That the change happened at all is accepted.

This too has a twin outside this timeline. The anime's director said in 2003 that the opening credits show the ending as first planned, the two in armour, the castle falling, and Anthy disappearing, and that he reversed it in production because nobody would expect the heroine to vanish. documented (makers' statement, per the archive)

Where the question stands

A 2024 survey of 312 scholars of Meiji literature found 41 percent naming a circle with Kishida Toshiko at its centre, 22 percent a circle centred on Wakamatsu Shizuko, 9 percent Miyazaki Muryū, and 19 percent declining to name anyone. choice Most agree on three things: that more than one person wrote the text; that at least one of them was a woman who had been inside a girls' school; and that whoever designed it meant the byline to be read both ways.
